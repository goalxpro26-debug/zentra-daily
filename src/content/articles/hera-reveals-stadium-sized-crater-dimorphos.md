Four years after NASA slammed a spacecraft into an asteroid to test whether humanity could deflect a dangerous space rock, Europe's follow-up mission has shown what the blow actually did — and the damage is far greater than expected.

The European Space Agency released the first close-up images of Dimorphos on Wednesday, taken by its Hera spacecraft. The 151-metre-wide moonlet, which orbits a larger asteroid called Didymos, now has one side flattened into a crater the size of a sports stadium — a scar that spans the entire width of the face NASA's DART probe struck.

"The face struck by DART has been flattened into an impact crater the size of a sports stadium, which extends across the asteroid's entire width," said Patrick Michel, the mission's principal investigator.

The surprise was not only the crater. The rest of Dimorphos looks much as it did before the impact, its boulder fields apparently unchanged — yet the moonlet itself is now tumbling chaotically in its orbit, its steady spin knocked out of kilter by the blow.

Hera also found the asteroid pair surrounded by hundreds of boulders drifting in space, some just a few metres across, with more than 100 of them between the size of a football and a washing machine. The debris cloud is a hazard the mission team must now take into account as Hera moves closer.

DART — the Double Asteroid Redirection Test — hit Dimorphos on 26 September 2022 at about 22,500 kilometres per hour, shortening its 11-hour-55-minute orbit around Didymos by 33 minutes. It was the first time humans had deliberately changed the motion of a celestial body, a proof of concept for planetary defence: if an asteroid were ever found heading for Earth, a spacecraft impact might nudge it aside.

But until Hera arrived, scientists could only guess at what the impact had done to the asteroid itself. Hera, a car-sized spacecraft launched in October 2024, reached the system about three weeks ago and is now observing from 25 to 30 kilometres away. It will move to within about 13 kilometres next month, and in January it will release two shoebox-sized CubeSats to probe even closer — one to map the surface minerals, the other to peer inside Dimorphos with ground-penetrating radar.

The images were unveiled by mission manager Ian Carnelli in the dinosaur hall of Berlin's Museum für Naturkunde — a deliberate reminder of what an asteroid impact did 66 million years ago.

"NASA's DART mission demonstrated that we can change an asteroid's orbit," Carnelli said. "Hera is now revealing what happened to the asteroid itself."

The Didymos system is currently about 193.5 million kilometres from Earth. What Hera measures over the coming months — the crater's depth, Dimorphos's exact mass, the structure of the rubble inside — will decide whether the deflection technique can be trusted the next time it matters.
