A genuinely new web browser engine is a rare event, and one has just arrived. Ladybird, an independent browser built around an engine written from scratch, entered its first alpha on Monday, with test builds available for macOS and Linux.

Developer Andreas Kling announced the release after more than four years of development. Ladybird began as part of the SerenityOS project before spinning out into its own effort, and it is backed by a nonprofit whose stated goal is an independent, fast and secure browser that respects user privacy and fosters an open web.

What makes the release notable is what Ladybird is not: a fork. Nearly every browser in everyday use runs on one of three engines — Blink, which powers Chrome and Edge; Gecko, behind Firefox; or WebKit, behind Safari. Ladybird's engine was written from the ground up, making it the first browser in years to arrive with an entirely new one.

Kling is candid about the alpha's limits. He described the browser around the engine as "modest but functional," and warned that some websites will not work and the browser will sometimes crash. That is expected at this stage: the alpha is aimed at early adopters willing to test and report problems, and a broken site can be reported from inside the browser itself.

For now, the alpha is limited to macOS and Linux. A Windows version is planned for a later stage of the project, and the team says its JavaScript engine is already built and tested on Windows. The browser is free and open source.

The project is small by browser standards, with a small full-time engineering team supported by community testing and bug reports. Its bet is that the web needs a fourth engine that answers to no browser vendor — one built on web standards and free of the commercial interests that shape the existing three.

Whether Ladybird can survive contact with the real web, and with the attackers who probe every new browser, is the question the alpha is meant to answer. For now, anyone curious can download it and find out first-hand.
