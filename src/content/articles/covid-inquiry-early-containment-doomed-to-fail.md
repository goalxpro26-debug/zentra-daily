The United Kingdom's early efforts to contain Covid-19 were doomed to fail, the public inquiry into the pandemic has concluded, because the country had no test, trace and isolate system able to cope with a fast-moving outbreak when the virus arrived.

The finding comes in the inquiry's seventh report, a 245-page assessment of border controls through contact tracing. Community testing and contact tracing were abandoned in mid-March 2020, after which the virus spread virtually unchecked. Ministers said testing capacity was being overwhelmed, and the four UK nations were effectively blind to how quickly Covid was spreading. The absence of a robust system from the start, it says, helped force harsher measures, including lockdown and school closures.

Testing capacity did eventually grow: NHS Test and Trace, launched two months later, became one of the largest testing services in the world relative to population. More than £30 billion was spent across the UK on testing, tracing and isolating. But the inquiry questions why testing capacity in hospitals, universities and the private sector was not used from the start, instead of relying only on 12 government laboratories.

Inquiry chair Baroness Hallett said the UK should have been better prepared, learning from countries with experience of other outbreaks, such as Sars — notably Hong Kong, South Korea, Singapore and Taiwan, which had systems ready to go. UK simulation exercises had already flagged problems with test, trace and isolation that appear not to have been fully acted on.

Support for people told to self-isolate was also criticised. Many felt forgotten and let down by the lack of practical or financial help, the report says, and in the early months estimates suggested less than half of people complied with isolation rules. Fines of up to £10,000 were introduced for non-compliance, and £500 payments to help people on low incomes stay at home did not arrive until September in England — later in Scotland and Wales.

On borders, the inquiry found the UK lacked a clear early strategy. Suspending all flights from China in February 2020 could have provided valuable additional time to prepare healthcare systems, Baroness Hallett said; that did not happen. By April 2020, 93% of countries had put in place measures such as flight suspensions, border closures and travel restrictions. The UK, by contrast, only asked travellers from high-risk areas to self-isolate for 14 days, and introduced no further border measures until June 2020.

The report is more positive about smartphone apps, saying digital tools can significantly reduce the spread of infection. But three separate apps were developed — one for England and Wales, one for Scotland, and one for Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland — which took up valuable time. In summer 2021, around 7% of all UK adults were told to self-isolate over a seven-day period, an episode the press dubbed the "pingdemic". Treasury estimates suggested that may have reduced economic growth by as much as 0.3% that month.

The recommendations include putting international border control measures in place at the earliest stages of a future pandemic, and developing a single UK-wide digital tool rather than separate apps. The overall message: the UK should have had a robust, ready-to-go test, trace and isolate system, learned from other countries and its own exercises, and provided better practical and financial support for isolation.
