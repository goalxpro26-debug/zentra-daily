Seventy-five baby olive ridley sea turtles have made it into the Pacific Ocean from the first sea turtle nests ever recorded on the US West Coast, after two months of careful incubation at the Aquarium of the Pacific in Long Beach.

The first 54 hatchlings came from a nest at Seal Beach and were released on the same beach in the dark early hours of Saturday, November 14. A second group of 21, from a nest near Huntington Beach Pier, followed early on Sunday morning. All of the released hatchlings reached the water.

The story began in September, when two olive ridley turtles were seen nesting on Orange County beaches — one at Seal Beach on September 16, the other near Huntington Beach Pier three days later. It was the first recorded instance of any sea turtle nesting on the West Coast. Until then, the northernmost known olive ridley nest was roughly 700 miles south, in Baja California Sur, Mexico.

Because a tropical storm was approaching and the nests faced high tides and flooding, biologists moved all 204 eggs — 104 from Seal Beach and 100 from Huntington Beach — to the Aquarium of the Pacific on September 25. The eggs spent 58 days in temperature- and humidity-controlled incubators under sand.

## A Saturday morning eruption

Aquarium staff first noticed signs of hatching on November 12, when the sand in the Seal Beach incubation container began to crack and dip. The first turtle emerged at about 7:15 p.m. on Friday, November 13, and 54 had surfaced within about two hours.

"It kind of went from looking like very smooth beach sand to looking like an eruption of baby sea turtles," Dr. Lance Adams, the aquarium's director of veterinary services, said at a press conference on November 14.

The hatchlings are tiny — under two inches long, each roughly the size and weight of a large strawberry. They were released before sunrise so they could navigate toward the ocean using moonlight, then began a "frenzy period" of swimming continuously for 24 to 48 hours. About 50 eggs from the Seal Beach nest had still not hatched at the time of the press conference, and staff were continuing to monitor them. By Monday afternoon, the aquarium said 94 hatchlings had hatched and been released in total.

## Why did they nest in California?

Scientists think unusually warm Pacific water is the main reason. The ongoing "super" El Niño has raised sea surface temperatures, and one theory is that the mother turtles lost their way because of a hurricane. A biologist with the US Fish and Wildlife Service also pointed to decades of conservation work: olive ridley populations have grown substantially, putting more turtles along the California coast to take advantage of the unusual conditions.

Olive ridleys are the smallest sea turtle species, growing to about two to three feet long and weighing 70 to 100 pounds as adults. They can live for 50 years, but only about one in a thousand hatchlings survives to adulthood.

A third nest was found on November 1 at San Buenaventura State Beach in Ventura County, about 100 miles north, but it was destroyed by high tides.

## What is still unknown

Scientists do not yet know whether the California nests are a one-off event or the start of a new pattern. DNA testing of shell fragments is planned to identify which Mexican breeding colonies the mother turtles came from, and staff are still watching the remaining eggs to see how many more hatch.
