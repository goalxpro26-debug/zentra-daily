A mathematician at Anthropic says he has used the company's AI system Claude to prove the Riemann hypothesis, a problem about prime numbers that has resisted mathematicians since 1859. If the claim survives scrutiny, it would settle what many regard as the most important open question in pure mathematics. It has not yet been verified.

Levent Alpöge, a number theorist who works at Anthropic, announced the result on Friday in a four-page note on his personal website. "I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis," he wrote. He said the method also resolves a broader version of the problem known as the generalized Riemann hypothesis.

The hypothesis, first proposed by the German mathematician Bernhard Riemann, concerns the distribution of prime numbers — the numbers divisible only by themselves and one. It conjectures that all the non-trivial zeros of a function called the zeta function lie on a single vertical line, the "critical line".

Alpöge did not publish a conventional paper. Instead, he released the argument as code in Lean, a programming language that lets computers check mathematical reasoning step by step. He said a full written version would be extremely long and that he had not yet mastered all of the mathematics involved.

Early checks have been encouraging. Within hours, mathematicians including Andrew Sutherland of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology downloaded the Lean code and reported that it compiled without errors. "Honestly the chance that anything is fishy here is approximately zero," Sutherland said. But a further check, known as a comparator run, is still under way to confirm the code does not redefine any of the symbols in the statement.

Reaction has been mixed. Brian Conrey, a leading specialist on the hypothesis, called it "a monumental moment in human history", while admitting he had not yet seen a manuscript. Andrew Granville, a number theorist at the University of Montreal, was more cautious, describing the released note as garbled and saying a clearer write-up should have come first.

The announcement came the day after Anthropic's record stock-market debut, which initially valued the company at $1.5 trillion. Alpöge said he had consulted chief executive Dario Amodei before announcing and acknowledged the timing could be viewed cynically, calling it "horrible" but saying he could not wait any longer.

The claim arrives two months after rival OpenAI said an AI model had solved another of the seven Millennium Prize Problems, on the equations that govern fluid motion. That proof also awaits full vetting. The Riemann hypothesis carries a $1 million prize from the Clay Mathematics Institute, but a solution must be published in a peer-reviewed journal and withstand two years of scrutiny.

In August, Anthropic said an unreleased Claude had improved a related result, raising the proven share of zeros on the critical line from 41.6% to 67.2% — while stressing it had not proved the hypothesis. Whether this claim closes the gap is now for the mathematics community to decide.
