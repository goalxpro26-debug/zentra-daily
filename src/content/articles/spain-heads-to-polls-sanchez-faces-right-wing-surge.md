Spain goes to the polls on Sunday in a snap general election that could end eight years of Socialist-led government and bring the far right into national power for the first time since the death of dictator Francisco Franco in 1975.

Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez called the early vote in October after parliament rejected two of his housing decrees, gambling that public anger over housing costs would work in his favour. Instead, almost every opinion poll published before the campaign's close points the other way: the conservative People's Party (PP) is narrowly ahead, and only a coalition with the far-right Vox appears able to command a majority in the 350-seat Congress.

The PP, led by Alberto Núñez Feijóo, is polling at around 31 percent, worth roughly 127 to 136 seats. Sánchez's Socialists (PSOE) have climbed to about 29 to 31 percent in the final surveys, close enough that a handful of pollsters put them first. Vox, led by Santiago Abascal, stands at roughly 17 to 18 percent, or about 51 to 66 seats. Together, the PP and Vox are projected to win between 186 and 199 seats — above the 176 needed for an absolute majority.

The one notable outlier is the state-run Centre for Sociological Research, which puts the Socialists clearly ahead at 34.4 percent. Spanish law bans the publication of voting-intention polls in the five days before an election, so no new surveys will appear before Sunday.

The campaign has been fought over housing, corruption and rail safety. Sánchez sought to make the vote a referendum on affordability after mass protests over rents and a shortage of social housing, but his government has been dogged by corruption scandals touching his inner circle and family, and by legislative gridlock that left him governing as a minority.

Sánchez is also one of Europe's last leftist leaders, and his record of defying Donald Trump has become part of the story. Spain was the only NATO member to refuse the alliance's pledge to spend 5 percent of GDP on defence by 2035, with Sánchez insisting the country could meet its capability targets at 2.1 percent. Trump responded by threatening tariffs and, at one point, to cut off trade with Spain altogether.

Feijóo, a former president of Galicia who has led the PP since 2022, has pitched himself as a moderate administrator. But governing with Vox would give the far right a cabinet role for the first time in half a century — a prospect that has drawn attention well beyond Spain. Vox has grown from a minor force into the country's third-largest party, propelled in part by younger and male voters.

The new lower house is due to meet on December 23 to elect a speaker. Whether it then produces a PP–Vox government, or a surprise Socialist survival, will be decided on Sunday.
