The night sky as we know it may not survive the race to put artificial intelligence into orbit. A new report warns that sprawling networks of space-based data centers could make stargazing far more difficult — in many places, satellites could become the most visible lights in the sky, outnumbering the stars themselves.

The report, "Space-Based Data Centers Roadmap: The Case for Caution," was published this week by the Innovation for Cool Earth Forum and led by David Sandalow of Columbia University's Center on Global Energy Policy. It argues that companies and policymakers should proceed with great caution before launching large numbers of AI-powered data centers into orbit: the risks are not yet understood — or governed.

## A sky full of satellites

The warning is not about a handful of satellites. Some orbital data centers are already operating, and the filings are enormous: SpaceX has applied for one million data-center satellites, Starcloud for 88,000 and Blue Origin's Project Sunrise for 51,600, according to the Secure World Foundation. Investment tied to orbital data centers reached $197 million in 2025 and $1.11 billion so far in 2026, per market research firm Quilty Space.

In the report's night-sky chapter, Sandalow and Columbia Climate School dean Alexis Abramson write that such constellations could dramatically transform night skies worldwide, with satellites outnumbering visible stars in many places — though technical fixes could soften the effect, with trade-offs.

## The launch bill — and the other risks

Putting the hardware up there is itself a vast undertaking: placing just 10 percent of the AI data centers anticipated by 2030 into orbit would require more than 700 launches of large rockets a year — about two a day, indefinitely. The centers would run on solar power, but those launches would produce greenhouse gases and black carbon.

The night sky is only one concern. The report also points to debris fields in low Earth orbit, collisions with thousands of facilities travelling at about 17,000 miles per hour, damage from satellites falling back to Earth, ozone depletion, and the possibility that the centers could become military targets. Existing governance, the authors write, cannot manage risks that need coordination on a global scale.

## "Slowly and with care"

Sandalow told Axios there are still "unknown unknowns" about the effects, and that deployment "should happen slowly and with care." The report proposes guardrails in the meantime: brightness limits, mandatory impact assessments before launch, required mitigation, caps on satellite numbers, and international standards.

It also urges policymakers not to allow large constellations until there is more operational experience, citing Deepwater Horizon and Fukushima as reminders of what happens when a technology scales faster than understanding of its risks.

Not every filing will become a launch — Quilty Space cautions that companies often apply for far more satellites than they intend to deploy. But the report's central point stands: what the night sky looks like a decade from now is being decided in filing cabinets and boardrooms today, largely out of public view.
