In the mid-Cretaceous, a tropical rainforest in what is now Myanmar was dripping with sticky tree resin. One drop caught a very peculiar praying mantis — and held it for 98 million years. Scientists say the insect is the earliest clear evidence in the fossil record of a mantis mimicking plant leaves.

The fossil's abdomen carries leaf-like extensions that closely resemble the fronds of an extinct clubmoss, *Selaginella grimaldii*, which is itself known from amber found in the same region. Researchers have named the insect *Folimantis hyalinala* in a paper published in the journal *New Phytologist*.

The name is descriptive: the genus *Folimantis* translates literally as "leaf mantis", while *hyalinala* refers to the glass-like transparency of the creature's wings. Lead author Hongru Yang and colleagues note that this style of mimicry has never been reported before, and no living mantis is known to copy *Selaginella*.

## A disguise that started at the back

Modern mantises are famous for their camouflage. The orchid mantis of Southeast Asia looks and even sways like a flower. But when *F. hyalinala* lived, flowering plants had not yet taken over the landscape, so the insect evolved to look like leaves instead.

There is something odd about this fossil, though. Modern plant-mimicking mantises wear leaf-like features across different parts of their bodies. *F. hyalinala* carries them on its abdomen only. The researchers suggest this could be a hint that plant mimicry began at the rear, with changes to the wings and thorax emerging later in mantis evolution.

The likeliest explanation is the usual one: a mantis that looked more like its host plant had a better chance of sneaking up on prey while staying hidden from its own predators. That is an evolutionary interpretation of the anatomy, not behaviour observed in the fossil.

The discovery also pushes the known history of this trick back a long way. Mantises share an ancestor with cockroaches that lived around 274 million years ago, and the first distinct mantises are thought to have emerged about 185 million years ago, during the Jurassic. This specimen shows leaf mimicry was already part of their toolkit at least 98 million years ago — and other insect groups, including cockroaches, grasshoppers and stick insects, were evolving plant-like features around the same time.

## The amber question

The find comes with an ethical footnote. Amber mining in Myanmar's Kachin region is linked to ongoing conflict, and there are concerns the trade helps fund abuses the United Nations has condemned. Myanmar banned fossil exports in 2015, though specimens embedded in amber have fallen into a legal grey area, and in 2021 the Society of Vertebrate Paleontology called for a moratorium on publishing research on amber fossils obtained from Myanmar sources after January that year.

This specimen predates those lines. It was acquired in 2015 by collector Fangyuan Xia, who donated it later that year to the insect evolution laboratory at Capital Normal University in Beijing, where the research was carried out.
