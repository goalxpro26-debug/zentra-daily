The James Webb Space Telescope has found water and carbon dioxide ice swirling around a young star — and the ice appears to be older than the star itself.

The discovery comes from Beta Pictoris, a star about 64 light-years from Earth that is only around 20 million years old, a newborn by cosmic standards. It is surrounded by a vast disk of dust and rubble where planets are still forming; astronomers have already found three worlds there. A team led by Sarah Betti of the Space Telescope Science Institute pointed Webb's Near Infrared Spectrograph at the disk on December 7, 2023, and recorded the light from two spots roughly 100 astronomical units from the star, on opposite sides.

In both places, the spectra showed the unmistakable fingerprints of frozen water and frozen carbon dioxide. Water ice absorbs light strongly near a wavelength of 3 micrometres; carbon dioxide leaves its mark at 4.27 micrometres, with a second signal at 4.39 micrometres from the heavier carbon-13 form. Earlier searches had come up empty, finding neither water vapour nor the 3-micrometre ice signature. Webb's sensitivity made the difference.

What makes the find striking is the ice's condition. The water ice is almost entirely glassy — amorphous, like frozen glass, rather than arranged in an orderly crystal lattice. Less than 7 percent of it is estimated to be crystalline. Glassy ice can only survive if it stays colder than about 100 Kelvin (minus 173°C), which means this material has spent its life in deep cold, undisturbed by the heat of a forming star.

The carbon dioxide is also telling. Rather than sitting in separate grains, it is trapped inside the water ice, and it makes up about 11 percent of the mixture — slightly more than one carbon dioxide molecule for every ten water molecules. That proportion falls squarely within the range measured in comets in our own Solar System, which carry between 2.5 and 30 percent carbon dioxide.

## A leftover from before the star

Taken together, the clues point to an origin that predates Beta Pictoris itself. The researchers concluded the ice most likely formed in the interstellar medium, building up on dust grains in cold, dense, water-rich clouds during the earliest stages of star formation — before the star and its planets began to take shape. The disk we see today is, in effect, still carrying the frozen raw material of its birth cloud.

That matters because Beta Pictoris is one of the best-studied young systems in the sky. Its disk was among the first ever imaged, back in 1984, and we see it almost exactly edge-on, which makes it a natural laboratory for watching planetary systems grow. If its building blocks arrived pre-frozen from interstellar space, the same may be true for other young stars — including, long ago, our own Sun.
