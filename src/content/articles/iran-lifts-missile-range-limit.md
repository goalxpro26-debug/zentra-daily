Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps said on Saturday that it has lifted its self-imposed 2,000-kilometre (1,250-mile) limit on the range of its ballistic missiles, in the clearest sign yet that Tehran is widening the scope of its war with the United States and Israel.

"This limitation has been lifted for us," IRGC spokesman Brigadier-General Hossein Mohebbi told a news conference in Tehran. He did not say whether Iran already has missiles that can fly beyond the cap, saying Tehran would show its capabilities "through action rather than statements". He added that Iran has shifted towards an "offensive" military doctrine.

The announcement came days after Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei warned that "any kind and level of threat" from the United States or Israel would be met with a response unlimited by geography.

## What the cap meant

For years, the 2,000km limit was presented as a reassurance to Europe — a promise that Iranian missiles would not be aimed beyond the Middle East. It was self-imposed, not a treaty obligation, and the United Nations curbs in Security Council Resolution 2231 expired in October 2023.

For Iran's Gulf neighbours, little changes: they already sit well within the 2,000km range and have come under repeated Iranian missile fire since the war began on February 28. Beyond that line, Iran's existing arsenal can already reach the entire Arabian Peninsula, the Levant and most of Egypt, plus parts of southeastern Europe, including Greece, Bulgaria and Romania.

## What Iran can — and cannot — reach

Iran currently has no intercontinental ballistic missiles. But a 2019 British, French and German assessment put a lighter-warhead version of Iran's Khorramshahr missile at roughly 3,000km.

In March, the United Kingdom accused Iran of firing missiles towards the joint US-UK base at Diego Garcia, some 4,000km from Iran. Tehran denied targeting the base. Before the war, Iran held the largest ballistic missile stockpile in the Middle East, estimated at between 2,500 and 6,000 missiles.

## Why now

Analysts read the move as both deterrence and bargaining. As a warning, it tells Washington — and European governments hosting US aircraft — that renewed strikes could bring a wider response. As leverage, the cap can now be offered back as a concession at the negotiating table. Muhanad Seloom of the Doha Institute for Graduate Studies argued Tehran sees a narrow window before the new US Congress convenes in January, in which President Donald Trump will want a deal he can present as a success. Trump said on Monday there was "positive momentum" towards ending the hostilities after talks with the Qatari prime minister in Washington. Seloom called the announcement "worrying … but not alarming" for Europe, since missiles would have to travel far to reach the mainland, giving NATO members early warning and time to intercept them.

## What remains unknown

Whether Iran actually fields a missile that can fly beyond 2,000km is unproven — Mohebbi offered no evidence of one. Iran has also claimed it has built underground "missile cities" to keep weapons production running under bombing, a claim that cannot be independently verified.
