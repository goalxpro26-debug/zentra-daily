Confirmed Ebola cases in the Democratic Republic of Congo have passed 11,000, government data showed on Tuesday, in an outbreak that is now the second-largest and deadliest ever recorded anywhere in the world.

The data put confirmed infections at 11,042, with 5,387 deaths. Only the 2014–2016 West African epidemic, which infected more than 28,600 people and killed more than 11,000, was larger.

The outbreak, caused by the rare Bundibugyo species of the Ebola virus, was declared in mid-May. There are no approved treatments or vaccines for this species of the virus, which sets it apart from the better-known Zaire strain, for which vaccines exist.

## Eight provinces affected

The virus has now reached eight of Congo's 26 provinces, and many of the deaths have occurred outside treatment centres — a sign that cases are being detected late, or that sick people are not reaching care at all.

Health officials have pointed to three forces driving the spread: delayed detection, conflict, and health services stretched thin. Together, they have allowed the virus to move faster than in previous outbreaks.

The geographic reach is also unprecedented. The disease has now been reported in East, Central and West Africa, giving this outbreak the widest spread of any Ebola outbreak on the continent to date.

## Why this strain is harder to fight

Ebola spreads through direct contact with the bodily fluids of a sick person, or with surfaces and materials contaminated by them. Outbreaks are usually contained through rapid case-finding, isolation, contact tracing and safe burials.

The Bundibugyo species complicates that work. Because it is rare, there is far less experience fighting it, and the tools developed for other Ebola strains — including vaccines — are not approved for use against it. That leaves the response dependent on the basics: finding cases early, isolating the sick, tracing their contacts, and protecting health workers.

The International Organization for Migration has separately warned that a funding gap risks leaving the outbreak out of control, with dozens of cases already detected along travel routes out of the affected areas.

## What is still unknown

The government figures are confirmed cases only, and the true scale of the outbreak may be larger, given the deaths occurring outside treatment centres and the difficulty of surveillance in conflict-affected areas. How quickly the case count is still rising, and whether the response can close the funding and detection gaps, will determine whether the outbreak stays the second-largest on record — or closes in on the West African epidemic it now trails.
