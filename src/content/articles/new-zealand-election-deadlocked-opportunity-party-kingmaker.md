New Zealand's election has ended in a hung parliament after the final vote count, published on Friday, stripped the ruling centre-right coalition of the wafer-thin majority it held on election night — leaving a brand-new centrist party holding the balance of power.

The election on November 7 was the tightest in years, and the conservative bloc finished the night with a slim lead. But after the counting of 484,865 special votes — ballots cast overseas or outside a voter's home electorate, which tend to lean left — the Electoral Commission said Prime Minister Christopher Luxon's National Party had lost two seats, finishing with 40. National won 28.35 percent of the party vote; Labour, on 29.88 percent, won 36 seats.

With its partners ACT on 13 seats and New Zealand First on 12, the right-wing bloc now holds 65 seats in the 130-seat Parliament — exactly one short of the 66 needed for a majority. The left-wing opposition, led by Labour's Chris Hipkins, holds 58 seats, with the Greens on 17 and Te Pāti Māori on five. National also lost the electorates of Wairarapa and Hamilton East to Labour, which cost it two seats outright.

That leaves the Opportunity Party, a centrist group that won seven seats on 6.1 percent of the vote, as the decider of what the next government looks like. It is the first party founded outside the modern political establishment to enter New Zealand's parliament. Its leader, 38-year-old business consultant Qiulae Wong, became leader less than a year ago after answering a job advertisement, after the party's three failed attempts to win seats.

Wong, a former KPMG consultant, has said she is unaligned with either bloc and could work with either side, and that she will negotiate first with the party that won the most seats. Her party would consider a formal coalition deal or a confidence-and-supply agreement, under which its lawmakers would back government bills case by case. "We're ready to go, straight away," she said of the negotiations.

The arithmetic gives Luxon the only workable path: even Labour, the Greens, Te Pāti Māori and Opportunity together would hold just 65 seats, one short of a majority. Luxon, who had ruled out working with Opportunity before the election, has shifted his position and said he is optimistic about forming a government. But the talks will not be easy — Opportunity's five "bottom line" policies include stronger measures on climate change and an end to bottom-trawling fisheries, both opposed by ACT and New Zealand First.

The result was a rebuke to the two parties that have long dominated New Zealand politics. Turnout was the second-lowest on record, and the two big parties' combined vote share the lowest ever, with 2,931,604 votes cast in total. The New Zealand dollar strengthened slightly after the announcement, having hit a 17-year low against the US dollar earlier in the week.

No timeline has been set for the negotiations; if the talks fail, a fresh election has been raised as a possibility.
