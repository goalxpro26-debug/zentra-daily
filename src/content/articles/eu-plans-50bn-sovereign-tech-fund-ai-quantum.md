The European Union is planning a new technology fund worth up to €50 billion (about $55 billion) to back European artificial intelligence and quantum computing companies, in its biggest attempt yet to cut the bloc's dependence on American and Chinese technology.

The proposed Sovereign Tech Equity Fund was reported by the Financial Times on Wednesday, citing two officials involved in the discussions, and the European Commission formally presented it the same day alongside a draft law to boost quantum technology. The fund would take direct equity stakes, writing larger cheques, taking on more risk and holding investments for longer than the EU's existing financing schemes. Its main targets would be AI and quantum firms, with strategic semiconductor technology also potentially in scope. "We want to build a fund and our target scale is now 50 billion euros," EU tech chief Henna Virkkunen told a press conference at the European Parliament in Strasbourg.

The push reflects a chronic financing gap. Europe attracts only about 5% of global venture capital, against 52% in the United States and 40% in China, according to figures cited in coverage of the plan. In quantum, the imbalance is starker in private money: European start-ups raised €1.1 billion last year, against €7 billion in the US — even though about a third of the world's quantum companies are based in the EU, and European public quantum funding of €8.6 billion exceeded America's €5.3 billion, though it trailed China's €13.6 billion. The Commission's June 2026 tech sovereignty paper found Europe depends on non-EU providers for more than 80% of key digital products and intellectual property.

The fund was unveiled with the draft Quantum Act, which aims to make Europe a global leader in quantum technology by 2030. The draft law would push public authorities to favour European businesses in procurement tenders and sets out measures to turn the bloc's research strength into industrial leadership. It still needs to be negotiated with EU member states and the European Parliament before it can become law.

Much remains undecided. Operational details are expected in February, and it is not yet clear where the €50 billion would come from — the EU budget, member states, or the European Investment Bank. The Commission has confirmed the fund's name and its €50 billion target scale, but the final size and structure could still change. The Financial Times reported the Commission aims to attract private investors alongside the public money, targeting up to €6 of investment for every €1 committed.

The move follows the 2024 Draghi report, which warned that Europe was falling behind the US and China on innovation and urged far higher investment. If the fund reaches its target scale, it would dwarf the EU's existing financing vehicles — and test whether public money can keep Europe's most promising deep-tech companies growing at home instead of selling out or moving abroad.
