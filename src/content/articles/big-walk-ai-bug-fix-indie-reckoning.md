A ten-line bug fix has become one of the sharpest arguments in indie gaming. House House, the studio behind Big Walk, has confirmed it used a chatbot to help solve a stubborn engine problem late in development — and 19 Independent Games Festival jurors say that alone should disqualify the game from awards consideration.

The studio says the chatbot was used sparingly, only for technical problems, and the AI-assisted code is well under 0.1% of the game. No generative AI touched art, writing, music or gameplay.

## The bug that started it

The problem was in Unity's rendering pipeline: in some situations, shadows stretched to what the studio called infinite length, halving performance for about 20 seconds at sunrise and sunset. House House says it researched the issue extensively, and a Unity engineer concluded a fix was not possible.

Out of options, the developers put the problem to a chatbot. After a few dead ends it suggested a fix, which the team reworked heavily into about 10 lines of code.

House House had disclosed the AI use when submitting for festival consideration, which asks about all forms of AI use. It had not mentioned it on Steam, saying Valve does not ask developers to disclose code or chatbot help — its form covers AI-generated content, not coding assistance.

## The jurors' line

On Monday, 19 members of the festival's 50-plus-member jury released a signed statement saying they would not vote for any game that used generative AI "in any capacity". They cited a sharp rise in AI-built submissions and ecological, economic, psychological and labour harms, urging the festival to bar games containing any AI-generated work.

Not every juror agrees. Liz Ryerson said she did not find the minimal use personally disqualifying. But Austin Walker and Damon Reece said even a small amount of AI use outweighed their regard for the game; Reece wrote that jurors had their hands on the lever and needed to speak clearly for human creativity and labour.

## What House House said

In a statement added to the game's public FAQ, the studio said it was disclosing the use because it had become a matter of public interest, and apologised that it "may not meet their standards" for some. It stressed the disclosure was not an endorsement of AI tools, however minimal, and said it supports efforts to limit generative AI's harm — while worrying that voluntary disclosure might itself help normalise the technology.

The fallout has been swift: more than 400 negative reviews have hit Big Walk's Steam page, though Steam's anti-review-bombing tools appear to have limited the damage to its overall "Very Positive" rating.

## What is still unknown

The festival has not said whether it will adopt the stricter policy, or how it would police AI use. The dispute leaves a wider question open for the industry: should the line sit at AI-generated content, or at any AI assistance at all — including debugging help many developers now use routinely?
