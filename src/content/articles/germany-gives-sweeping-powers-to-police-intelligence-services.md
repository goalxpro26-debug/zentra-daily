Germany's parliament has approved the biggest overhaul of the country's intelligence laws in decades, giving its spy agencies powers to act against espionage, sabotage and cyberattacks rather than merely watch and report.

The Bundestag passed the reform package on Friday, with the governing coalition of the conservative CDU/CSU and the Social Democratic Party voting in favour. The vote was 309 in favour, 190 against, with 71 abstentions.

Under the new rules, the Federal Intelligence Service (BND), which handles foreign intelligence, will be allowed to answer cyberattacks from abroad with counterattacks of its own. The measures can range from deleting and manipulating data to shutting down entire systems and, if necessary, destroying physical weapons such as drones deployed against Germany.

The domestic service, the Federal Office for the Protection of the Constitution, gains operational powers for the first time. Until now it has been limited largely to gathering information and monitoring. Under certain conditions its officers will be permitted to enter homes and business premises covertly, for example to prepare surveillance operations, and to act against explosives and weapons. They will not be allowed to use force against people.

An independent oversight board is to be established to monitor the agencies' new powers.

Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt, who championed the reform, has described it as a generational change. "Anyone who attacks us in this way must know that we are prepared to strike back," he said. The government argues the changes are needed because of growing hybrid threats, pointing to the activities of Russia, China and Iran, as well as drone sightings and sabotage.

The head of the Federal Criminal Police Office, Holger Münch, has argued that modern hybrid attacks are blurring the old line between police work and intelligence gathering. He pointed to figures showing crime originating abroad rose by 24 percent in the first six months of 2026 compared with the same period last year.

Opposition lawmakers and legal experts warn the reform blurs the post-war separation between police and intelligence services, a principle meant to prevent the concentration of unchecked power. Critics are expected to challenge the law before the Federal Constitutional Court. Most provisions are due to take effect on December 31, after the Bundesrat, the upper house, considers the bill.
