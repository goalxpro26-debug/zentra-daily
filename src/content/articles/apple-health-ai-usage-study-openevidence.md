Apple is taking a research-first route into health AI. The company announced on Saturday the launch of the Apple Health AI Usage Study, a virtual research project in the United States that will enrol up to 200,000 participants to find out how people ask health questions of an AI assistant — and whether the answers actually help.

The study is run in collaboration with OpenEvidence, a clinical AI platform widely used by healthcare professionals. Participants use a dedicated study app to ask everyday health questions, as they would in a chatbot, and receive simplified answers grounded in peer-reviewed medical literature from sources including the New England Journal of Medicine, the Journal of the American Medical Association and Nature. Each response carries linked citations; participants rate the answers and take monthly surveys. They can also choose to share HealthKit data — resting heart rate, activity, sleep or lab results — so researchers can see whether personal context changes how useful the answers are.

## A study, not a product launch

Apple is careful to draw a line between the study and a consumer feature. The app is not meant to diagnose or treat any condition, and participants are told to consult a doctor about health concerns or to verify medical information. Safety guardrails are designed to detect certain concerning questions and suggest following up with a provider.

"At Apple, we believe responsible innovation starts with scientific rigor and clinical grounding," said Sumbul Desai, Apple's vice president of Health and Fitness. "Conducting a dedicated research study is the most thoughtful way to understand how people engage with, comprehend, and evaluate health AI in everyday life."

The research is open to adults aged 21 and over in the United States who have an Apple Intelligence-enabled iPhone — an iPhone 15 Pro or later running iOS 26 or later. Apple aims to run the study for more than 12 months. The app is available to download now for US users.

## Privacy is the centrepiece

The study's privacy terms are unusually strict. Data is anonymised before it leaves the phone for a secure third-party cloud provider, it will not be used to train any AI models, and OpenEvidence deletes query and response data after 24 hours. Participants keep control over whether they share any HealthKit metrics or survey responses.

Earlier this year, OpenAI said users were asking more than 300 million health-related queries each week. Apple has not said whether the capability will eventually reach its Health app or Siri — for now, the study itself is the product, and its findings are meant to guide the responsible design of health AI.

"Patients and clinicians are both turning to AI, but the tools they use are being built and tested in separate worlds," said Robert M. Wachter, professor and chair of the Department of Medicine at the University of California, San Francisco. "When patients and doctors draw on versions of the same trustworthy tools, patients arrive prepared, conversations improve, and next steps happen seamlessly."
