The failure of the COP31 climate summit in Antalya has turned into a debate about the summits themselves, with former US vice-president Al Gore, the conference's own co-chair and leading scientists all saying the way the United Nations reaches climate decisions needs urgent reform.

The talks, co-chaired by Turkey and Australia, ended late on Sunday without the sweeping "Antalya pact" they were meant to produce, after running more than two days past their scheduled Friday close — making COP31 the longest climate summit on record. Under the UN climate convention's rules, decisions can only be adopted by consensus. When a group of countries formally objected to the final text, the pact could not go through.

The draft that died had dropped every mention of fossil fuels — widely seen as a concession to oil-producing states. The EU, Panama, Belize, Vanuatu, the UK, the Marshall Islands and Norway were among those that refused to accept it. Ireland's Darragh O'Brien, speaking for the EU, said the bloc would not endorse a deal that did not even name fossil fuels, the main driver of climate change.

Gore called the outcome an outrage and said significant reform was needed. "World leaders no longer want to settle for the lowest common denominator," he said, adding that most countries had come to Antalya in good faith and that it was time for a process reflecting the will of the majority.

Australia's climate minister Chris Bowen, who co-chaired the summit, pointed directly at the decision rule. "The fundamental root problem is that when Cops were created, for reasons I understand, it was made consensus only," he said. "That's a very tough ask when you also need consensus to change consensus."

The UN's chief climate negotiator, Simon Stiell, defended the consensus system, noting that it produced the 2015 Paris agreement and helped lower expected end-of-century warming from almost 5C to 2.6C. But he conceded it had fallen short in Antalya and said he was championing a review to seek outside advice on reform.

Other voices went further. Johan Rockström of the Potsdam Institute said the summits needed to move from "negotiating promises and commitments that we never deliver on" to holding countries accountable for what they have already promised. Tobias Holle of Shifting Advocacy said the process was "not fit for purpose any more". During the talks, Panama released an open letter calling for a path away from consensus toward simple majority voting.

Former UN climate chief Christiana Figueres has called the decision to walk away a "sign of courage". China, India and many developing countries did not formally object, and later expressed disappointment that it failed. Ghana's delegation told the plenary: "Africa has always chosen to build multilateralism rather than walk away from it."

The question now passes to Ethiopia, host of next year's COP32 — along with an open argument over whether nearly 200 countries can keep deciding only when every one of them agrees.
