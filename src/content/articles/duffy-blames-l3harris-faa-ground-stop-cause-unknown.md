U.S. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy continued on Wednesday to blame contractor L3Harris for the failure that forced the Federal Aviation Administration to ground flights nationwide last Friday — even though investigators still have not determined the root cause.

L3Harris owns and maintains the FAA's telecommunications network. A section of old copper wire in El Dorado, Arkansas, was being replaced with fiber-optic lines when the incident happened.

Speaking on "The Morning Meeting" on Wednesday, Duffy said a contractor failed to identify and isolate the router problem quickly enough. "The monitoring of the network matters," he said. New installations of the same routers have been paused, and technicians have disabled the function linked to Friday's outage. The FAA offered no update on the investigation on Wednesday.

## A single router, a nationwide stop

Officials have said a Juniper router installed as part of the upgrade in El Dorado suddenly began sending thousands of inquiries every few seconds, overwhelming the older parts of the network. That produced inconsistent radar and communications signals at 17 of the FAA's 22 key air traffic control centers, and the agency grounded planes. It was only the second nationwide ground stop since the September 11, 2001, terrorist attacks.

It is still a mystery why this device behaved differently from the 68 previous copper-to-fiber upgrades across the FAA's network, none of which caused a problem. Officials have suggested the router may have been configured incorrectly, but Bedford said the technician was a highly trained Air Force veteran who had done the same job dozens of times before.

## "Blame me"

Bedford, speaking at a separate event hosted by The Hill, pushed back on the idea that the contractor alone should carry the blame. He said his agency is ultimately responsible for what happened, even if L3Harris was doing the work.

"I am the chief accountability officer. I take full responsibility for it," Bedford said. "I wish people would lay off the secretary. He's got a lot of work to do, but I'm the guy they hired to get this done. So, blame me."

L3Harris has not commented since a Saturday statement pledging to support the investigation, and declined to say more on Wednesday.

## The $50 billion question

The FAA nearly doubled its funding request to Congress to $50 billion after Friday's ground stop, to pay for the rest of the overhaul of the nation's air traffic control system. The agency received $12.5 billion last year to start the project. Only about $10 billion of the new request would go to air traffic control technology; the rest would fund runways, airport towers and aging control centers built in the 1960s.

Several key lawmakers said on Tuesday that Congress is unlikely to approve the full amount right now, since the FAA has not spent all of last year's money. Bedford said he understands why members of Congress might be having "some indigestion associated with that."

Airlines for America president Chris Sununu praised Bedford for briefing airline chief executives within half an hour of the stop and staying on the phone through the night. He warned this might not be the last technical problem during the project, because the work is akin to trying to get an iPhone to talk to a flip phone.
