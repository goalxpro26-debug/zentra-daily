Researchers at Curtin University say they have found the clearest evidence yet for when plate tectonics — the slow engine that builds continents, opens oceans and helps steady Earth's climate — began working much as it does today: about 2.5 billion years ago.

Pinning down that date has long frustrated geologists. Plate tectonics destroys much of its own evidence, dragging old crust deep into the planet and recycling it. The team got around that problem by looking for something subtler: a chemical fingerprint left behind when new crust forms by melting older rock.

## A fingerprint that vanishes

Lead author Dr. Janne Liebmann, a research fellow in Curtin's Timescales of Mineral Systems Group, and colleagues searched for the fingerprint in thousands of rocks from around the world, spanning almost 4 billion years of Earth's history.

They found it consistently in rocks older than 2.5 billion years, showing those rocks were derived from a pool of ancient crust that formed about 4 billion years ago. But after 2.5 billion years ago, the fingerprint disappears from the geological record — and despite examining thousands of rocks, the team never sees it again.

"That tells us something fundamental changed in the way Earth's crust was being formed and transformed," Liebmann said, "marking a transition toward the dynamic tectonic system we see today."

The study, "Loss of ancient crustal reservoirs marks a shift in Earth's geodynamic regime," was published in the *Proceedings of the Royal Society A*.

## From reworking in place to recycling on a grand scale

Before the shift, Earth's early crust was repeatedly melted and reworked more or less where it sat. After it, the planet became capable of moving and recycling crust on a much larger scale — the kind of conveyor-belt system that defines modern plate tectonics, with old crust sinking at plate boundaries and new crust forming elsewhere.

Study co-author Professor Chris Kirkland, also of Curtin's Timescales of Mineral Systems Group, said the finding helps explain when Earth acquired one of the features that sets it apart from tectonically quiet neighbours such as Venus and Mars.

"Plate tectonics is fundamental to the way Earth has evolved over billions of years," Kirkland said, "continually reshaping the continents and oceans and influencing the planet's atmosphere and climate."

## Why the timing matters

The date matters beyond geology. Plate tectonics regulates the carbon cycle, shapes the magnetic field that shields the surface from solar radiation, and builds the continents where most life lives. Knowing when the modern system switched on gives scientists a firmer starting point for understanding how Earth became habitable — and stayed that way.

## What is still unknown

The study identifies when the transition happened, not what caused it. The precise trigger for the change remains an open question, and the onset of plate tectonics is still actively debated — other studies have argued for some form of plate motion earlier in Earth's history. What this work adds is a sharp, globally consistent marker in the rock record: a 4-billion-year-old fingerprint that is everywhere before 2.5 billion years ago, and nowhere after.
