The Dutch government is on the brink of collapse less than nine months after taking office, after a row over wealth taxes split Prime Minister Rob Jetten's three-party minority coalition.

Jetten will meet his coalition partners on Friday in an attempt to hold the government together, a day after parliament narrowly passed a 2027 tax plan that one of the governing parties had voted against.

The dispute centres on a deal struck on Wednesday between Jetten's centrist D66 party and the Christian Democrats, the CDA, and left-wing opposition parties. In exchange for opposition support for the annual tax package, the two governing parties agreed to a small increase in wealth taxes — a shift of part of the tax burden from income to wealth.

The right-wing, pro-business People's Party for Freedom and Democracy, the VVD, the third member of the coalition, opposed the change and ultimately voted against its own government's tax plan. The plan still passed with a narrow majority, carried by opposition votes.

## The "millionaire tax"

The most contested element is a levy on the wealthy that critics have dubbed the "millionaire tax". It would tax annual returns on assets above €60,000 at 38.5 per cent, one of the highest rates in the euro area, while the rate on returns below that threshold would fall to 35.5 per cent.

Supporters say the package raises taxes on the very wealthy while cutting them for people on lower incomes. The VVD called it a bad deal for the Netherlands and a breach of the coalition agreement the three parties negotiated earlier this year.

VVD parliamentary leader Ruben Brekelmans described the result of the vote as "disillusioning" and said his party would have to seriously reconsider its position. The party had threatened to quit the coalition if the amendment passed.

## A government without a majority

Jetten, 38, became the youngest prime minister in Dutch history when he took office in February, after his D66 party unexpectedly won last year's election on a campaign promising a break from the divisive previous government. But his coalition has never held a majority in either house of parliament, and has needed opposition support to pass every proposal.

He skipped a summit of European Union leaders in Ireland on Thursday to attend the parliamentary debate. After the vote, he said he would do all he could to hold the coalition together and would seek "a new start" at Friday's meeting with the leaders of the VVD and the Christian Democrats.

If the VVD walks out, the government would almost certainly fall. A crisis could trigger new elections — which would be the fourth in the Netherlands in five years — although parties could also try to form a new government from the current parliament. That route would probably produce another minority government, an option the left-wing opposition party Pro has already ruled out.
