Nvidia has stopped shipping its licensed Hopper artificial intelligence chips to China, the world's leading AI chip supplier said on Tuesday — a decision that closes off a market Washington had only recently cleared it to serve.

Colette Kress, the company's interim chief executive, told analysts on an earnings call that Nvidia's outlook contains no licensed Hopper data centre products for China and that shipments of those chips have ended. The announcement came even though the US government gave Nvidia permission last year to sell a version of the Hopper model into China under licence.

The move draws a line under a fraught chapter for the chipmaker. Nvidia sold $2.4 billion worth of licensed Hopper products to China in the third quarter, showing that demand in the country remained strong even as the political ground shifted beneath the business. That revenue will now fall away from future guidance.

Nvidia's position in China has been squeezed from both directions. In Washington, the company's access to the Chinese market has become a political flashpoint: the administration's decision to allow licensed sales drew criticism, and the loopholes that let Chinese firms reach advanced chips through overseas data centres have drawn scrutiny in Congress. In Beijing, meanwhile, officials have pushed domestic buyers toward homegrown alternatives, leaving Nvidia's licensed products in an increasingly narrow lane.

The decision also lands in the middle of a leadership transition at Nvidia. Kress is running the company on an interim basis after the departure of Jensen Huang, the co-founder who built Nvidia into the dominant supplier of the chips powering the AI boom. The board is weighing who should lead the company next, and the China question is one of the most consequential files on the new chief executive's desk.

For the wider chip industry, Nvidia's exit from licensed Hopper sales in China sharpens a divide that has been widening for years: a US-led AI hardware ecosystem and a Chinese one, each increasingly sealed off from the other. Chinese customers will now have to rely on domestic suppliers or on older, less capable hardware, while Nvidia gives up one of its largest single markets in the name of a cleaner position with Washington.

What remains unclear is whether the halt is permanent or a pause while the company and the government settle on new terms. Kress gave no indication of a timeline for resuming sales, and Nvidia did not say whether it is seeking approval for a different, more restricted chip design for China.

Investors will be watching the company's next set of results for the full cost of the decision — and whether the booming demand for AI hardware elsewhere is enough to absorb the loss of $2.4 billion a quarter in Chinese sales.
