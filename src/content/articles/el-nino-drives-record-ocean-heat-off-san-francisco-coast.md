The water off the San Francisco coast is warmer than it has ever been this late in the year — by a wide margin. Near the Farallon Islands, normally the centre of Northern California's famously cold upwelling zone, sea temperatures have passed 66°F, roughly 10 degrees above what is normal for November.

The warmth is not a local quirk. Climate scientist Daniel Swain of UCLA reports that water within 20 to 30 miles of shore is running more than 10°F above average in one unbroken stretch from central Baja California north to Sonoma County, with 5- to 8-degree anomalies reaching the Oregon border. The readings are the warmest on record for the date along nearly the entire California coast — and in many places they would break the record for any week of the cool season, November through April, in the satellite era that began in 1981.

The cause is a pile-up of warming forces: an extreme El Niño, already among the strongest ever measured, building on a marine heatwave amplified by long-term ocean warming. NOAA puts the chance of a "historic" El Niño this winter at about 60%.

## Seas running a foot high

Warm water takes up more space, and the effect is already visible in sea level. Swain estimates that thermal expansion accounts for about 12 inches of a persistent 12- to 18-inch rise in coastal ocean height, with more to come as coastally trapped Kelvin waves arrive through the season. That leaves the coast exposed: king tides, the year's highest astronomical tides, fall on November 23–25, December 23–25 and January 21–22, and even a moderate storm during one of those windows could push water a further foot or two higher.

The warming reaches well below the surface. Researchers at the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute recorded 64.72°F at a depth of 200 feet in Monterey Bay on October 26 — the warmest reading at that depth in nearly 40 years of monitoring, beating a record set during the powerful 1997 El Niño. The temperature there was more than 7°C above the long-term average, and tropical species are already appearing where they have never been seen.

For now, the immediate consequence is weather. The same warm water is feeding extra moisture and energy into a run of Pacific storms now arriving on the coast. Forecasters expect three or four systems over the coming days, with the first significant rain of the season reaching Northern California on Friday. San Francisco could see one to five inches of rain over two weeks — against a November average of about 2.6 inches for the whole month.

Swain's seasonal outlook suggests the extreme warmth is likely to persist at least into January or February. Splitting the contributions — El Niño, the background marine heatwave, human-caused warming — will take attribution studies. What is already clear is that the ocean off California has never been this warm, this late, in the modern record.
