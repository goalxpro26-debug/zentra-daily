Close your eyes and cover your ears. Much of the world disappears, but the feeling of being present — of being you — stays. Two researchers at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem now argue that this basic feeling does not come from where most neuroscientists have looked for it.

Their paper, "Brainstem Theory of Consciousness," published in the journal *The Neuroscientist*, proposes that raw conscious experience — what the authors call the "me inside" — is generated by the brain stem, the stalk-like structure at the base of the brain that connects the upper brain to the spinal cord. Until now, the dominant view has placed conscious experience in the cerebral cortex, the folded outer layer responsible for language, recognition and planning.

The authors — veteran pain researcher Prof. Marshall Devor and neuroscientist Dr. Mark Baron, of the Hebrew University and Harvard Medical School's Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center — build their case on pain, brain injury and anaesthesia.

Pain is central to it. Detecting a harmful stimulus and feeling it hurt are different things, Devor told *The Jerusalem Post*: the nervous system can trigger an automatic withdrawal reflex, but pain also involves a subjective experience — it hurts. That feeling, they argue, does not depend on the cortex the way complex sights and sounds do. Extensive damage to the cortex can wipe out vision or hearing, yet usually leaves the experience of pain intact, or even stronger. Damage to particular regions of the brain stem, by contrast, consistently sends a person into a coma.

They point to one part of the brain stem, the mesopontine tegmentum, where earlier research located circuits involved in coma after brain injury and in losing consciousness under anaesthesia. In a 2001 rat experiment, tiny doses of anaesthetic delivered into one small node there rapidly and reversibly switched off pain responses and other signs of consciousness, while injections placed even slightly off target had no effect.

Under the theory, the cortex does the heavy computing — recognising a face, judging whether an animal might attack — while the brain stem acts as an adjudicator, filtering the flood of sensations and deciding which signals deserve conscious attention. "The cortex provides detailed information about the world, and brain stem mechanisms shine the light of subjective experience on those aspects that deserve conscious attention," Devor said.

The authors stress what they have not shown: the paper synthesises existing findings and clinical observations, proposing a framework for further investigation — not a proven mechanism. The precise mechanism remains unresolved, they say, though it is surely biological.

If the theory holds up, it could change how doctors assess consciousness in patients with severe brain injuries, coma or disorders of consciousness. Devor also suggests the capacity to feel pain arose early in evolution, before the mammalian cortex existed — perhaps in reptiles, even fish — though whether they have subjective experiences, he said, "we don't know."
