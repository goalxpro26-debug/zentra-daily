South Korea's education minister resigned on Sunday after last-minute rule changes meant to stop AI-assisted cheating on the country's college entrance exam left students and teachers in confusion.

Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin offered to step down, saying he took responsibility for the disruption. President Lee Jae Myung accepted the resignation about an hour later, presidential spokesperson Kang Yu-jung said.

"As the minister in charge, I believe I cannot escape criticism that we failed to prepare in advance," Choi said in a statement released by the ministry. "Now that the exam has concluded, I feel a deep sense of responsibility and am offering my resignation."

The exam at the centre of the row is the College Scholastic Ability Test, known in Korea as the Suneung. Held on Thursday, the annual nine-hour test is widely seen as a defining moment, with university admission heavily shaping career prospects.

The crisis began four days before the exam, when the top scorer on the national certified tax accountant exam admitted to cheating with a miniature camera, a smartphone and generative AI. The ministry had initially focused on smart glasses, then rushed out new rules in the final days before the test.

Two days before the exam, the government announced 8,400 extra staff, including about 1,800 to detect smart devices. The day before, the rules changed again: test-takers could bring only pencils into exam rooms, and marking pens, correction tape and erasers were banned. Students were also required to cover the buttons on their clothing with stickers, to block miniature cameras. During the exam, the authorities sent three emergency disaster alerts to mobile phones nationwide to help identify test-takers who had failed to hand in their phones.

The education ministry said on Friday that 111 suspected cheating cases involving electronic devices were detected during the exam. Its examination misconduct committee is reviewing the cases and will decide on penalties before scores are released on 11 December.

Choi also drew criticism for remarks he made in parliament ahead of the test. Asked by a lawmaker whether he was 100 percent confident that AI-assisted cheating could be detected, the 72-year-old replied: "I don't think I could, because of my age."

President Lee wrote on social media shortly after the resignation that concerns over AI-assisted cheating were spreading beyond the CSAT to essay-based admission exams and school tests, and that "the fairness of exams is a principle that can never be compromised." He said he would order a comprehensive inspection of exam management, and a review of marking question papers so their source can be traced if leaked.

The government and the ruling party have suggested extending the ban for AI-assisted cheating to 10 years, potentially applying it retroactively to this year's exam. Separately, a man who earned a perfect score on this year's law school entrance exam was found dead on Thursday after facing cheating allegations.
