Strict bans on mobile phones in schools are linked to small but measurable improvements in pupils' test scores, according to a large new study — though the researchers themselves, and outside experts, warn the picture is far from settled.

The study, a joint effort by Harvard University's Center for Education Policy Research, Dartmouth College and the testing organisation NWEA, looked at more than 3 million elementary, middle and high school students over the past five school years. It compared student-level maths and reading scores at 844 schools that use Yondr lockable phone pouches with similar schools that did not adopt them. More than 3,300 schools across the United States began using the pouches between the 2019–20 and 2025–26 school years.

The researchers found that the strictest bans — phones locked away in pouches for the whole school day — were associated with gains equivalent to one to two weeks of extra learning per year. Co-author Tom Kane of Harvard framed the result as plugging about half of the estimated two to three weeks of learning lost each year since around 2013. The gains did not fade after the first year, the study found, and appeared to continue for up to three years after a school adopted the pouches.

The cost is part of the pitch. Kane put the price of the pouches at roughly $25 per student — far less, he argued, than paying teachers for an extra week or two of instruction.

## The caveats

The new study has not been peer-reviewed, and it arrives alongside a very different result. An earlier peer-reviewed study published in April, by a team including Brian Jacob of the University of Michigan and Tom Dee of Stanford, pooled school-level average scores from several thousand Yondr schools and found close to zero overall impact, with no statistically significant benefit. That study did find significant gains in high school maths, but they were offset by learning losses in middle school.

The two teams used different methods: the new study worked with finer-grained student-level data from fewer schools, while the April study used school-level averages from a much larger group. Dee cautioned against extrapolating the new study's small, sensitive estimates over just a few years of bans, describing the early results as largely disappointing and saying continued study is needed. Jacob said the new findings were encouraging if the benefit persists and accumulates — but "we'll just have to wait and see."

For schools and districts weighing a ban, the evidence now points in the same direction but with different force: locked-away phones may buy a little learning back, at modest cost, yet the size of that gain — and whether it lasts — is still an open question.
