In April 2017, a magnitude 6.5 earthquake shook central Botswana, where earthquakes were not supposed to happen: the middle of the Kalahari Craton, an ancient block of crust largely unchanged for billions of years. Nearly a decade later, scientists say they finally understand why. An ancient rift system beneath Botswana, dormant for around 180 million years, appears to be stirring back to life.

Published in the journal Tectonics, the findings come from researchers in Botswana and at Columbia University's Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory, and suggest the forces slowly pulling Africa apart are reaching into ground long assumed to be unshakable.

## A failed rift

A continental rift forms when tectonic forces pull Earth's crust in opposite directions until it fractures along large faults. Given millions of years, the process can split a continent apart and open a new ocean. But not every rift gets that far: some stall, leaving a failed rift frozen in the crust.

That is what the team believes happened in Botswana. They focused on the Khurutshe Fault, a major fracture linked to a 50-mile scar in the landscape, about 50 miles south of the 2017 quake. The fault's steep scarps hinted at past movement, so the team dug trenches across it and imaged the rock below with electrical currents.

The evidence points to a rift that first opened around 180 million years ago, during the Jurassic Period, when tectonic forces were stretching the region. It stalled before it could split the continent and lay dormant for millions of years. But the fault has also displaced younger Kalahari sand laid down within the past 2.6 million years, a sign movement has resumed recently. When exactly it reawakened is still unknown.

## Why here

The researchers say it exploited an ancient seam inside the craton, an intra-cratonic suture zone formed billions of years ago when separate blocks of crust collided and joined. Even in a craton as strong as the Kalahari, such old seams stay comparatively weak, giving tectonic forces a place to grip.

"Whether rifts do propagate through cratons has been a really big question in geoscience," said Boniface Kgosidintsi, the study's lead author. His co-author Folarin Kolawole put it more plainly: "While cratons are regarded as strong old continental blocks, they do have inherent zones of weakness that can localize a new plate boundary. Maybe you need to reactivate the rift multiple times. You need to try multiple times before it actually succeeds."

It may also redraw the map of southern Africa's plates. The region south of the Okavango Rift Zone has long been treated as part of the San Plate, a microplate moving as one block. The renewed fault activity suggests it may instead be slowly breaking into two smaller plates.

Nobody is predicting a new ocean in Botswana: any breakup would unfold over millions of years. But the study shows continental breakup can reach into regions once written off as stable, and that splitting a continent may take more than one attempt.
