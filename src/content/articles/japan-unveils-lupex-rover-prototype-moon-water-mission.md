Japan's space agency has shown off a test model of the rover it plans to send to the Moon's south pole in search of water — a joint mission with India that is aiming for launch in 2028.

The Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency, JAXA, unveiled the four-legged test model to reporters in Tokyo on Wednesday, then put it through a demonstration run at its Chofu Aerospace Center. The rover moved slowly across a simulated lunar surface on caterpillar tracks fitted to its four legs, crossing rocks and a crater-shaped dip without difficulty.

The mission, known as LUPEX — Lunar Polar Exploration — is a partnership in which Japan is responsible for the rover and the H3 rocket that will carry it, while India is building the lander. The launch is planned for fiscal 2028 from the Tanegashima Space Center in Kagoshima Prefecture.

The target is water. The Moon's polar regions contain areas of permanent shadow that never see sunlight, and scientists believe water ice is likely to exist there close to the surface. Once it lands, the rover is expected to look for the most promising sites and drill up to about 1.5 metres underground to collect samples, giving scientists their first detailed data on how much water is there and in what form.

The reason goes beyond science. Water can be split into hydrogen and oxygen — the ingredients of rocket fuel — so a reliable lunar supply could one day let spacecraft refuel on the Moon instead of carrying everything from Earth. "We ultimately want to use lunar water for rocket fuel," project manager Takeshi Hoshino told reporters. He noted that more than 90 percent of the water aboard the International Space Station is recycled, but the water produced when rockets burn oxygen and hydrogen cannot be recovered — which is why producing fuel on the Moon matters for missions returning to Earth or heading onward to Mars.

The test model is being used to prove the rover can cope with the lunar surface, which is covered in fine dust called regolith. Engineers have tested it climbing slopes on a single leg, driving continuously on two legs, and moving under vacuum conditions. The flight version is still under development: JAXA's specifications put it at about 350 kilograms, roughly two metres long and 3.3 metres tall, carrying instruments including a water analyser and a drill.

The two countries agreed to pursue the mission jointly last year. India landed its Chandrayaan-3 spacecraft near the lunar south pole in 2023, and Japan made its own first soft landing on the Moon in 2024 with the SLIM probe, becoming only the fifth country to do so. Last month JAXA also launched a spacecraft to one of Mars's moons on a mission to bring back samples.

If LUPEX stays on schedule, the rover will explore the south pole for about 100 days after landing, with the results feeding into the US-led Artemis programme and longer-term plans for lunar bases.
