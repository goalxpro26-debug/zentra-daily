For nearly a year, a radio telescope in the Australian outback kept picking up the same uncanny rhythm: a burst of radio energy arriving like clockwork every eight minutes, then silence. Now astronomers say they have found what is behind it — a dead star spinning beside a star much like our own Sun.

The signal, catalogued as GPM J071507−114153, was first detected in late 2024 by the Murchison Widefield Array in Western Australia. Between December 2024 and September 2025 the telescope recorded 214 pulses from the source, and researchers classified it as a long-period transient — a recently recognised class of cosmic radio signals that switch on and off over minutes or hours.

In a study published on Thursday in the journal Nature Astronomy, the team reports the most likely source: a binary star system made up of a white dwarf — the dense, collapsed core left behind when a star dies — orbiting a G-type star similar to the Sun. It is the first time radio pulses have been detected from a white dwarf paired with a Sun-like star rather than a smaller, cooler red dwarf.

## A clockwork signal 5,000 light-years away

The system sits about 5,000 light-years from Earth. The eight-minute beat, the researchers found, is set by the white dwarf's own rotation. Because the pulses were unusually clear, the team could watch the white dwarf moving in its orbit around its companion — the first time that has been possible for this kind of object.

"This is the first time the pulses are clear enough to see the white dwarf moving in its orbit around its companion," said lead author Sam McSweeney, an astronomer at Curtin University in Australia, "so we know that the pulsations are coming from a region close to the white dwarf, or possibly even from the white dwarf itself."

The pulses did more than reveal the pair. They also let the team catch stellar flares erupting on the Sun-like companion — storms on a star thousands of light-years away, picked up through radio waves. Follow-up optical observations with the Keck Observatory in Hawaii confirmed the companion is indeed a G-type star. "It's amazing that by using radio wavelengths we can detect the storms on a star over 5,000 light-years away," said Natasha Hurley-Walker, a corresponding author of the study and an astronomer at Curtin University.

## Why it matters

Long-period transients are still a young field. The first hints of such signals appeared in 2005, but astronomers only began studying them in earnest in 2022, when a signal pulsing roughly every 18 minutes was found; another, with a 21-minute period, followed a year later. Around a dozen are now known, and at least four of them turned out to be binary systems pairing a white dwarf with a red dwarf — but it was never clear which star was producing the long radio pulses.

This discovery breaks that pattern. It shows that white dwarfs can generate these pulses while orbiting Sun-like stars too, widening the hunt: future detections of long-period transients could now help astronomers find white dwarfs that ordinary telescopes cannot see.

## What is still unknown

The exact mechanism that produces the radio pulses is not settled. The researchers believe the emission comes from near the white dwarf, but whether it is generated by the white dwarf itself or by the interaction between the two stars remains an open question — one that further observations of this system and others like it may answer.
