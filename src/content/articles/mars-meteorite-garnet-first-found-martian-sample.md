A few tiny grains of garnet have turned up inside a rock that fell to Earth from Mars — the first time the mineral has ever been found in a Martian sample. The speck is smaller than a poppy seed, but scientists say it could change what they know about the Red Planet's geology.

The discovery came from a meteorite known as NWA 8171, held in the collection of the Royal Ontario Museum. Inside a rock fragment measuring roughly 0.8 by 0.5 millimetres, researchers found a handful of grains of andradite, an iron-rich form of garnet. Unlike the deep red gemstone most people picture, this variety is yellowy-green, and it looks much like other minerals commonly found in meteorites.

That resemblance is almost why the find was missed. The team, led by planetary geologist Tanya Kizovski of Brock University in Canada, first took the unusual patch for pyroxene, a very common mineral. Its chemistry looked slightly off, so they took a second look — and follow-up analyses confirmed it was garnet.

NWA 8171 is already a rock of intense interest. It is one of 18 fragments from the same Martian meteorite fall, the best known of which is a piece nicknamed Black Beauty. It is a basaltic breccia, a rock that forms when cooling magma hardens around other blobs of minerals — a structure the researchers compare to a fruitcake, with basalt as the cake and the other minerals as the fruit and nuts.

The find matters because garnets are excellent record-keepers. They preserve snapshots of the temperature and pressure conditions under which they formed, and can help date those conditions. On Earth, garnet typically forms under intense heat, pressure or chemical alteration — and no set of conditions that could produce it has yet been identified on Mars.

That leaves an open question: did this garnet form on Mars at all? One possibility is metamorphism, in which heat and pressure transform existing rock. On Mars, that heat and pressure could have come from a meteorite striking the surface, or from magma rising into the crust. The researchers cannot yet say which process was involved, or when it happened.

They also cannot rule out that the garnet formed somewhere else entirely. Because NWA 8171 is a breccia made of several kinds of material, the fragment could in theory have landed on Mars from elsewhere and later been folded into the rock — although some of its chemistry does look Martian.

The next step is to measure isotope ratios in the mineral. If they match the ratios in other Mars minerals, that would indicate the garnet formed on Mars, opening a new window on the planet's deep history.
