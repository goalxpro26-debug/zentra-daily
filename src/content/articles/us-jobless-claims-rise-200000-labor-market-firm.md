More Americans applied for unemployment benefits last week, the Labor Department reported on Thursday, but the increase was small and claims remain at levels that have historically pointed to a tight labour market, with layoffs rare and most workers enjoying unusual job security.

Initial claims for state unemployment benefits rose 9,000 to a seasonally adjusted 200,000 for the week ended November 7, the department said. That was above the 195,000 economists polled by Reuters had forecast. The previous week's figure was revised up by 2,000 to 191,000.

The broader picture has barely changed. Claims have stayed at or below 200,000 for nine straight weeks and have not topped 230,000 in any week this year. They remain close to the 189,000 level reached in mid-July, the lowest since 1969. The four-week moving average, which smooths out week-to-week volatility, was unchanged at 195,250.

There was also a sign of steady hiring underneath the headline. Continuing claims — the number of people still receiving benefits after an initial week of aid — fell 20,000 to a seasonally adjusted 1.697 million in the week ended October 31. The insured unemployment rate held at 1.1%.

## A stable backdrop for the Fed

The figures fit with the wider jobs picture. The government reported last week that nonfarm payrolls surged in October and the unemployment rate fell to 4.1% from 4.2% in September. Economists read the claims data as consistent with a stable, or even strengthening, labour market — a backdrop that lets the Federal Reserve focus on fighting inflation rather than worrying about a wave of layoffs.

That stability matters because the claims report is the most timely read on the jobs market, arriving weekly rather than monthly. A single week's rise of 9,000 is well within the normal noise of the series, and at 200,000 the level is far below the readings that have historically signalled trouble.

## What is still unknown

Economists expect layoffs to stay low through the end of the year, but seasonal volatility around the holiday season could produce some spikes in the weekly data. Whether claims hold near current levels into December will be the next test — for now, the rise looks like a blip in a calm series rather than the start of a turn.
