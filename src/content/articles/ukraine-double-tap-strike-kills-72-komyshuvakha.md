At least 72 people were killed in the Ukrainian village of Komyshuvakha on 2 November, Ukrainian officials say, after a second strike hit a shelter where residents had fled following an initial attack on a humanitarian aid distribution. Ukraine says rescue workers were then struck repeatedly as they tried to reach the survivors.

Local people had gathered at the aid centre after a message in a local Telegram group said food and supplies would be handed out between 10:00 and 12:00. Ukrainian authorities say the first strike came at 09:45, hitting buildings above ground and killing one woman. About an hour later, a second strike hit the underground shelter where people had taken cover. Ukraine says it involved a 3,000 kg aerial bomb.

Police bodycam footage examined by BBC Verify shows people freeing a casualty after the first strike, radio warnings of a second attack growing more urgent, and people running for the shelter before a large explosion. Later footage shows bodies being recovered from a large crater above the shelter.

## Rescue workers 'hit again'

Ukraine's State Emergency Service told BBC Verify there were several Russian strikes during the rescue operation, injuring three police officers, four workers and two rescue dogs and damaging five vehicles. Work had to be suspended repeatedly because of the threat of further shelling, the service said.

An intelligence officer investigating the incident, identified only as Oleksiy, said the rescue vehicles were clearly marked and accused Russia of deliberately destroying humanitarian equipment and of terror against civilians. BBC Verify confirmed Ukrainian police videos showing rescue workers shouting warnings about a drone and running for cover the day after the attack.

Bomb disposal expert Trevor Ball and former UK National Crime Agency investigator Chris Proops both said the crater and damage were consistent with a FAB-3000, a 3,000 kg Russian bomb capable of destroying underground structures. Both also confirmed that verified photographs of munition fragments obtained from Ukrainian authorities show part of a glide-bomb guidance kit compatible with the weapon.

## What Russia has said

BBC Verify said it asked the Russian Ministry of Defence for comment specifically about the Komyshuvakha attacks and received no response. A general ministry statement on 2 November said it had targeted energy and transport infrastructure used by the Ukrainian military across the country.

The article notes there are no military or industrial facilities in Komyshuvakha, but that the village lies close to a key route used for Ukrainian troop movements, supplies and evacuations, and that it had previously been reported struck on 28 October.

International law experts Ben Saul of the University of Sydney and Samuel Moyn of Yale told BBC Verify that knowingly attacking rescue workers or civilians would be a war crime or a gross violation of international humanitarian law — stated conditionally, as an assessment of the law rather than a finding on this incident.

## What is still unknown

The death toll may still change: bodies were still being recovered from the rubble, and funerals have been held almost daily for aid workers, people collecting aid, and soldiers officials say came to help with the rescue. The casualty figure and the account of strikes during the rescue come from Ukrainian authorities, and the claim that rescuers were deliberately targeted is Ukraine's allegation, not a BBC finding. The toll would make it one of the deadliest single incidents since Russia's full-scale invasion began in February 2022.
