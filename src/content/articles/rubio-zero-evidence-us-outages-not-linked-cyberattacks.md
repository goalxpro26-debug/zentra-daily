Secretary of State Marco Rubio moved on Tuesday to calm a jittery public, saying the United States has found no evidence that a string of outages across the country is connected — or the work of hackers or a foreign state.

"We have zero evidence or any reason to believe that any of these isolated or individual instances have anything to do with nefarious actors, with cyber hackers, with criminals, with another nation state, with a malign actor of any kind," Rubio told reporters in Manila, where he is travelling with President Donald Trump ahead of meetings with Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin in Shenzhen.

Rubio, also Trump's national security adviser, was blunt: the failures are not linked to each other. "We have no indication, no evidence, and no reason to believe at all that any of these incidents are related to each other," he said. "In essence, the Microsoft incident has nothing to do with what we saw with the FAA. The FAA has nothing to do with what we saw with the banks a few days ago." "We are not under systemic attack," he added.

The reassurance follows a remarkable run of disruptions. It began on Thursday, when Microsoft's email and other workplace tools went down for millions of users; the company blamed a network capacity upgrade. On Friday, flights were grounded nationwide in the longest ground stop since 9/11; the Federal Aviation Administration blamed a router upgrade. By Monday, customers at several major banks reported trouble reaching online accounts after a technical issue at JPMorgan Chase rippled through the system. Chase called it a software bug, "not cyber-related." United Airlines fixed an internal systems outage the same day. On Tuesday, an internal National Security Agency network used for classified communications went offline during what the agency called routine maintenance.

Officials point to a common theme rather than a common culprit: security and capacity upgrades hitting unexpected problems. Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton said the government was "looking very diligently across the landscape" and "not seeing this as a cyber attack incident." The FAA's preliminary investigation into the ground stop likewise found no indication of a cyberattack, and Senator Mark Warner, the Senate Intelligence Committee's vice chairman, also said there was no sign of a coordinated cyberattack or foreign interference.

Not everyone is satisfied. Trump was dismissive in Asia, saying of the Chase outage that "things like that happen." Former press secretary Karoline Leavitt advised the White House that "transparency is key" and said a senior official should explain what is known after Trump returns from Asia. Commentator Laura Loomer claimed without evidence the outages were "very clearly a coordinated cyber attack," while former Homeland Security spokesperson Tricia McLaughlin said Americans "want and deserve to know what's really going on."

For now, the administration says the country suffered unrelated technical failures, not an attack. What remains unknown is when fuller findings will be published — and whether that will settle the scepticism.
