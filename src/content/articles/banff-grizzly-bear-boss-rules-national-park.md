To the scientists and wardens of Banff National Park, he is grizzly bear 122. To almost everyone else in and around Canada's oldest national park — and to many of its 4.5 million annual visitors — he is simply the Boss.

The Boss weighs about 700 pounds and rules a territory of roughly 2,000 square kilometres across Banff and the neighbouring parks. He has been struck by a freight train without apparent ill effect and shrugged off being hit by cars. Scientists have confirmed that he killed and ate a black bear at least once, and he is believed to have fathered many, if not most, of the bears in his territory. His battles with a rival known as Split Lip are local legend — he caused the split lip. Each spring, his emergence from hibernation, usually in March, is reported by news outlets across Alberta.

When he strolls along the Trans-Canada Highway looking for dandelions, traffic stops and "bear jams" follow. Galleries in Banff and neighbouring Canmore sell giant photographs of him around the world. A local restaurant is named for him. Yet the people who know him best say his fame, not any rival, may be what ends his reign.

## A vegetarian who eats 200,000 berries a day

For all his fearsome reputation — and four-inch claws — the Boss is mostly a vegetarian. Berries, roots and other plant material make up about 90 percent of the diet of Banff's grizzlies, according to David Laskin, a Parks Canada wildlife ecologist. Keeping 700 pounds fed on plants takes serious work: the Boss can put away about 200,000 buffaloberries a day, and in 2012, while wearing a tracking collar, he travelled some 1,400 kilometres in search of food.

About 75 grizzly bears roam Banff's 6,641 square kilometres of mountains, rivers and valleys. The Boss stands out partly because his home turf overlaps with the park's main tourist gateway, so he is seen far more often than bears in remote valleys. Laskin says the danger most grizzlies pose is overstated. "Bears need to be respected," he said. "Getting close to a bear: there's a risk for you but also for the bear especially."

## Old age, and the cost of celebrity

Now in his mid-20s, the Boss is getting old for a wild grizzly, and his body is showing it. His teeth are worn, though Laskin says that is not unusual for a bear his age. Earlier this year he was found in a trap set for another study bear, sedated and examined, and fitted with a collar that soon fell off, most likely in a fight. Grizzlies typically peak physically at about 10 and then gradually shrink — but Laskin said there is no sign of that in the Boss.

Under the park's bear management plan, the Boss is classed as "habituated," meaning he does not much care about people as long as he is left alone. When trail cameras and sightings suggest he is heading toward Banff, Canmore or a campground, park workers gently steer him elsewhere and clear visitors from his path.

Paul Kalra, a gas station owner and nature photographer widely credited with naming the bear more than 20 years ago, worries the attention itself is the problem. "Give him a break," he said. "Even the movie stars that are most famous, they go incognito with sunglasses when they go to have a burger, you know? I want the same thing for the Boss."

As winter approaches, the question in Banff is whether the old bear will emerge from hibernation in the spring — and whether the crowds that love him will give him the space to keep ruling.
