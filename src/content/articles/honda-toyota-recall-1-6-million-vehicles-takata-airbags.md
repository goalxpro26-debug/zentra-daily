Honda and Toyota said on Friday they are recalling about 1.6 million cars, trucks and SUVs because the Takata airbag inflators in them could rupture when the airbag deploys.

Both carmakers said there have been no injuries or crashes linked to the vehicles being recalled, and no airbag inflator ruptures have been reported in them. The inflators will be replaced.

The recall covers 810,592 older Honda cars and SUVs in the United States and 790,000 Toyota vehicles worldwide, including 314,000 in the United States.

The Toyota recall includes the 2002–2010 Lexus SC430, the 2011 IS250, the 2017 GX460, the 2003–2008 Toyota Corolla, the 2005–2006 Tundra, the 2005–2007 Sequoia and the 2016–2017 4Runner. The Honda recall covers some 2013 Accord, 2012–2013 Civic and 2012–2013 CR-V models, along with the 2013 Acura ILX and 2013 Acura RDX.

The Honda recall applies to vehicles sold or registered in 14 states and territories known for high heat and humidity and large temperature swings: Alabama, California, Florida, Georgia, Hawaii, Louisiana, Mississippi, South Carolina and Texas, plus Puerto Rico, the U.S. Virgin Islands, the Northern Mariana Islands, Guam and American Samoa. Toyota did not specify which U.S. states its recall covers, but said it expects replacement inflators to be ready by early 2027.

What sets this recall apart from the earlier Takata recalls is the type of inflator involved. The inflators in these vehicles contain a moisture-absorbing material, unlike the millions recalled previously. Honda said long-term monitoring and analysis had revealed potential propellant degradation in certain inflators in specific regions. Toyota warned that degraded propellant could cause the inflator to rupture if the airbag deploys, causing serious injury.

The U.S. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration said analysis and inflator-degradation simulation models run by independent testing groups had allowed the carmakers to act before the risk materialised. "Honda and Toyota saw early evidence of degradation risk in certain of their vehicles and are currently conducting proactive recalls of vehicles," the agency said late on Friday, adding that other carmakers continue to run inflator analysis and testing.

The Takata airbag defect is one of the biggest safety failures in automotive history. At least 28 people have died in the United States and more than 400 have been injured from defective Takata inflators since 2009, prompting the largest auto recall in U.S. history. All of those deaths involved inflators without a drying agent, and at least 20 of them were in Honda vehicles. Over the past decade, two dozen major carmakers have recalled more than 100 million vehicles fitted with Takata inflators worldwide, including about 67 million in the United States.

The inflators can explode on deployment, sending metal fragments into the cabin and killing or injuring drivers or passengers. The new recalls are a reminder that the problem is not fully closed: even inflators fitted with a moisture-absorbing material, long thought to be the fix, are now showing signs of degradation in hot, humid climates.
