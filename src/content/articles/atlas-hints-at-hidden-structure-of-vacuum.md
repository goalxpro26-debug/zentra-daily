Empty space may not be as empty as it looks. Physicists working with the ATLAS experiment at CERN's Large Hadron Collider have reported the first hint of a phenomenon predicted by the Standard Model of particle physics about 50 years ago — and never directly observed.

The phenomenon is known as a QCD instanton process. In quantum chromodynamics, the theory of the strong force that holds quarks together inside protons and neutrons, the field that carries the force has a structure even in a vacuum. It can be pictured as twisting around itself in different ways, and through a quantum effect called tunnelling it can switch from one twist to another. An instanton is that switch happening inside a particle collision.

Theory predicts such an event would look like a firework: many low-energy particles bursting out in all directions, spreading almost evenly. The trouble is that ordinary proton collisions can produce a very similar spray, so the signal is easy to miss.

For its first search, the ATLAS Collaboration — with researchers from the University of Bonn, Oxford and Prague playing a leading role — turned to a special dataset recorded in 2015, when the collider was running with very few simultaneous collisions. The quieter conditions gave the team a clearer view of each event. They trained a neural network on the tracks left by charged particles in the detector, teaching it to separate the spherical, evenly spread patterns from ordinary collisions.

The analysis found more of those spherical events than the best available models of ordinary collisions predict. In the region most sensitive to instantons, ATLAS counted 32,789 events where about 28,800 were expected from ordinary collisions — leaving roughly 3,900 that could come from instantons. The excess stands at 3.5 standard deviations above the background-only expectation.

That is a hint, not a discovery. Physicists normally require five standard deviations before claiming a discovery, and the predicted rate of instanton events itself is uncertain by orders of magnitude, so more precise theoretical calculations are needed before the comparison can be read firmly. The researchers plan to extend the search to other kinds of collisions, including ones in which one or both protons emerge intact, and to test different analysis methods.

If the signal holds up, it would show that the twisted structure of the vacuum has measurable effects on particle collisions. It would also open a new experimental route into one of the biggest open questions in cosmology: why the universe contains more matter than antimatter. Instantons themselves do not change the balance between matter and antimatter, but related processes in the Standard Model can — and observing instantons would let physicists study in the laboratory the kind of field topology that makes those processes possible.

The results have been posted as a preprint on arXiv.

## What is still unknown

The excess could still turn out to be an artefact of how ordinary collisions are modelled, rather than a real signal. More data, better background models and independent checks will decide whether ATLAS has caught the vacuum in the act — or simply glimpsed a statistical fluctuation.
