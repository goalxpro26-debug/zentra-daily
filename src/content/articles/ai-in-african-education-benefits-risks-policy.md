Artificial intelligence is arriving in African classrooms faster than the rules for using it. A new analysis published by the World Economic Forum argues that whether it helps or harms students will depend less on the technology itself than on the policies, languages and teachers around it.

The stakes are high. More than 80 percent of students in sub-Saharan Africa cannot read by the age of 10, according to the piece, and around 1.2 billion young people in emerging and developing economies will reach working age between 2025 and 2035. Education systems need to equip them with job skills at a scale that traditional methods have struggled to reach. Technology alone, the authors note, has not done the job: a review by the Global Education Evidence Advisory Panel found that interventions focused only on infrastructure or hardware did not improve learning outcomes.

Used well, AI could change that equation. The analysis points to tools that automate routine administrative work, support teachers' professional development, and give students personalised guidance as they learn. But it warns that the technology is often oversold as a cure-all and pitched without regard for a country's norms, policies, stakeholders or culture — and when that happens, it can falter, or actively harm teaching, learning and students' sense of identity.

The risks are concrete. Large language models still perform far better in English than in the low-resource languages many African students speak, and their outputs can carry culturally irrelevant content. In Guinea-Bissau, where the government used Anthropic's Claude with World Bank Group support to draft learning-assessment questions and help with grading, Portuguese prompts returned lower-quality results than English ones, and the model used uncommon surnames and the European Portuguese word "aldeia" for village instead of the Bissau-Guinean term "tabanka". The team caught the errors and did not use the output verbatim — but the authors warn that using such a chatbot to train more than 6,000 teachers nationally could have real consequences if it kept suggesting the wrong content.

Even good tools can stall on logistics. In Seychelles, an open-source AI tutor for secondary maths and geography was piloted in schools that already had free internet, yet the team struggled to fit it into the timetable: schools had to decide what activity it would displace, who would supervise the sessions, and how participation would be sustained.

The authors' conclusion is that success depends more on policy and cultural relevance than on computing power. The Beijing Consensus has already called on countries to adjust existing regulations or adopt new ones for responsible AI in education. Evidence from South Africa points the same way: a World Bank Group programme studied 46 higher education institutions and found that about half had their own policies or guidelines for ethical AI use. Among those with policies, 78 percent were integrating AI into the curriculum — roughly three times the rate of institutions without one. The study's authors caution that this does not prove fragmented policies alone can mainstream AI, and that more research is needed on how policy, institutional size and resources interact.

The message for governments and schools is to treat AI as one tool in a broader toolkit, not as something above human systems. That means building policy, culture and people into every deployment — including training language models on the under-represented cultures and languages they are meant to serve.
