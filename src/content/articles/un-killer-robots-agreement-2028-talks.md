A week of United Nations talks on lethal autonomous weapons has ended with a promise to keep talking — not with the binding rules that many countries wanted.

Delegates from the 128 member states of the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons met in Geneva from Monday, and the negotiations ran past 4 a.m. on Saturday before a compromise was reached. The agreement pledges further discussions aimed at developing new rules within two years, by 2028. It does not say whether those rules will be legally binding.

Around 100 countries had backed a push for binding rules within a short timeframe, to govern weapons systems that can identify and engage targets with varying degrees of autonomy — sometimes called "killer robots". But major arms-producing nations, including the United States, Russia and Israel, opposed that approach, and the final text was watered down as a result.

Russia's opposition shaped the outcome in more than one way. Diplomats said Russian objections to the rules of procedure forced the cancellation of some formal meetings, and negotiators spent hours overnight wrangling over a handful of words before the deal was struck.

The Swiss chair of the meeting, Julien Thoeni, called the agreement a significant achievement for humanitarian disarmament after more than a decade of talks. "It shows that even in today's deeply divided world, multilateral diplomacy can still deliver concrete results," he told reporters as the session closed.

Human rights groups were less convinced. "Delaying the creation of a legally binding instrument puts civilians and humanity at risk," said Nicole van Rooijen, executive director of the campaign group Stop Killer Robots, speaking on the sidelines of the talks. One diplomat from a country supporting new laws described the outcome as "disappointing but could have been much worse".

The urgency behind the talks is not theoretical. Alarm has grown over the use of lethal autonomous weapons systems in conflicts including Ukraine, Sudan and the Middle East. The UN secretary-general, the pope and the Red Cross have all called for strong and legally binding rules, and campaigners warn that technology is advancing faster than diplomacy.

That gap is now the central question. Because the current UN framework requires all 128 state parties to agree by consensus, some observers and former military officials say states should be prepared to consider a new process outside it. For now, though, the path chosen in Geneva is to keep negotiating — with the deadline for rules set two years out, and the question of whether they will bind anyone still open.
