President Donald Trump heads to Asia this week for a three-day trip built around a first-ever trilateral meeting with Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin in Shenzhen on Thursday, November 19.

The White House confirmed on Friday that Trump will meet Xi and Putin together, and China confirmed the meeting on Monday. It will take place on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit, which Xi is hosting in Shenzhen on November 18–19. It is the first time the three leaders have met together.

Before China, Trump travels to the Philippines, where he will attend the ASEAN summit in Manila, meet the other leaders of the Quad — India, Japan and Australia — and sign a trade deal with Thailand. The Manila stop is the region's first look at the president since his party lost control of Congress in the midterm elections, a result that could weaken his bargaining position.

Trump has said he wants to use the trilateral meeting to discuss the war in Iran and reducing nuclear weapons, but expectations for a concrete outcome are low. A White House official said no agreement on denuclearisation is expected, calling the meeting an "entry point" for talks on cutting nuclear stockpiles. China has long resisted three-way nuclear discussions with the United States and Russia, and US officials say Ukraine is not intended to come up. There is no plan for a separate meeting between Trump and Putin.

The Shenzhen meeting is Trump's second visit to China this year and his second meeting with Xi on the sidelines of APEC. Last year's meeting in Busan, South Korea, produced a truce: Washington lowered some tariffs on Chinese goods and Beijing agreed to loosen exports of rare earths. The execution has been uneven, but the agreement is expected to be renewed past its January expiry, either in Shenzhen or at the G20 summit Trump is hosting in Miami later this year.

The trip is also shadowed by the resignation last week of Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang, who admitted to an extramarital affair. The New York Times reported that US intelligence officials believe the woman involved was recruited by China's top spy agency; NPR has not independently confirmed those reports. A White House official said Trump is not expected to raise the matter with Xi; the focus is trade. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick has agreed to appear before the Senate Banking Committee to answer questions, and Trump has continued to defend Huang. Tech billionaire Elon Musk is joining Trump in Shenzhen.

With the last US–Russia nuclear arms treaty having expired in February and no deal expected from the meeting itself, the summit's significance may lie mostly in the image it produces: the leaders of the world's three largest nuclear powers, together in one room, for the first time. Commentators have already drawn comparisons with the 1945 Yalta Conference — a parallel that underlines the symbolism of the moment and the unease among smaller countries left outside the room.
