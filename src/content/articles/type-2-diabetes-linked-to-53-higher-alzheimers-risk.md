People living with type 2 diabetes face a 53 percent higher risk of developing Alzheimer's disease, according to a major review pooling data from more than 27 million participants.

The Curtin University-led review brought together 40 studies in a systematic review and meta-analysis. It found that for every 1,000 people with type 2 diabetes followed for a year, about 4.7 would develop Alzheimer's disease. After accounting for other factors, the adjusted relative risk was 53 percent higher than for people without the condition.

The researchers screened 9,431 scientific articles before selecting the 40 studies that met their criteria. The work was published on November 9 in the journal Alzheimer's Research and Therapy.

The findings sharpen a connection scientists have suspected for years. Earlier reviews often grouped different types of diabetes together, or lumped Alzheimer's in with other forms of dementia, which blurred the picture. This analysis looked specifically at type 2 diabetes and Alzheimer's disease, and measured both how much the risk rises and how often new cases occur.

"While many earlier reviews combined different types of diabetes or dementia, this review focused specifically on type 2 diabetes and Alzheimer's disease and estimated how often new cases occurred," said study author Dr. Emmanuel Adewuyi, an investigator fellow at the Curtin School of Population Health. "Relative risk tells us how two groups compare, while incidence tells us how often new cases occur. Putting those numbers together gives us a clearer picture of the burden of Alzheimer's disease among people living with type 2 diabetes."

The numbers describe an association, not proof that diabetes causes Alzheimer's. The review cannot say why the two conditions travel together. Possible explanations include damage to small blood vessels, chronic inflammation, and how the body handles insulin, which also plays a role in the brain. None of those mechanisms was tested here.

What the study offers is a firmer basis for prevention. The authors argue that the results support building cognitive risk checks into routine diabetes care, so memory problems are spotted earlier and patients get support sooner. For people living with type 2 diabetes, the conversation with a doctor may increasingly cover brain health as well as blood sugar.

The absolute risk remains small for any one person in a given year. About five new cases per 1,000 people with type 2 diabetes annually is modest on its own, but across a condition affecting hundreds of millions of people worldwide, it adds up to many cases. That is why the authors say the burden matters for health systems, not only for individuals.

More work is needed to pin down who with diabetes is most at risk, and whether tighter control of blood sugar or blood pressure lowers the chance of developing Alzheimer's. For now, the review gives doctors and patients a clearer number to work with, and a reason to treat brain health as part of diabetes care.
