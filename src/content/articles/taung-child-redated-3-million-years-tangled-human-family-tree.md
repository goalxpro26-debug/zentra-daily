A century after its discovery changed the story of human origins, the Taung Child has a firm new age — and the result complicates the family tree it once seemed to anchor.

The fossil skull of a young early human relative, found in a South African lime quarry in 1924, is now dated to between 2.96 and 3.03 million years old, according to a study published on November 5 in the journal *npj Palaeoecosystems*. The work was led by Gabriele Macho of the Vrije Universiteit Brussel, and announced by the university on November 9.

Dating the child has been difficult because its exact position in the quarry was lost during mining, leaving earlier estimates scattered from under a million years to 3.03 million. The team dated a sheet of mineral rock known as flowstone, lying above the fossil-bearing deposit, using uranium-lead dating. That gave a minimum age of 2.962 million years, give or take 59,000. Combined with a maximum age from earlier magnetic evidence, it pins the child to 2.96–3.03 million years ago, firmly in the Pliocene.

The child is the type specimen — the reference fossil — for *Australopithecus africanus*, the species named from it in 1925. It provided the first concrete evidence that human origins lay in Africa. Yet no clear criteria were ever set for sorting later discoveries into the species, and the child remains the only fossil that can be assigned to it without dispute.

That is the problem the new study presses on. Comparing the child's teeth and brain development during growth with adult fossils, the researchers found that several adults traditionally grouped under *Australopithecus africanus* do not fit the child's developmental trajectory. Differences in brain development were especially telling, suggesting that more than one hominin species lived in the Sterkfontein region, either side by side or one after another. The authors describe *Australopithecus africanus* as a poorly defined taxon.

The team ties the variety to a changing climate. Fossilised pollen shows the child lived in a much wetter, more wooded landscape than the region's semi-arid savanna today, with trees that now grow hundreds of kilometres to the north-east. Between about 4 and 2.5 million years ago, wet and dry cycles repeatedly reshaped southern Africa's habitats. As populations followed the shifting landscape, they were split apart and brought back together — diverging, and sometimes interbreeding, along the way.

"For over 100 years, we assumed the Taung Child was just one of many similar fossils from the era," Macho said. "Our findings turn that on its head. The human family tree wasn't a neat, straight trunk — it was a chaotic, tangled bush driven by climate shifts."

The study does not name a new species or redraw the tree. The authors write that no clear picture yet emerges of the child's relationship to other hominins, and that the Taung Child must stay at the centre of future work on how the diverse populations of three million years ago eventually gave rise to our own genus, *Homo*.
