Mortgage rates are climbing again, ending a brief stretch of relief for American homebuyers after touching a three-year high.

The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate rose to 7.28% this week, up from 7.11% last week, according to Freddie Mac. It is the largest one-week jump since the week of October 1, and it ends four weeks of declines that had briefly eased borrowing costs.

The increase also widens the gap with a year ago. At this time last year, the average 30-year rate stood at 6.26% — more than a full percentage point lower than today.

Mortgage rates tend to follow the 10-year Treasury yield, which moved higher recently after a hotter-than-expected inflation report raised the odds that the Federal Reserve could raise its benchmark interest rate at its December policy meeting. When investors expect the Fed to tighten, long-term borrowing costs usually rise with it.

Rates have now stayed above 7% since late September. Outside of a single month in 2023, that is the highest they have been at any time since 2000.

The higher costs are weighing on an already stalled housing market. Pending home sales — deals that have been agreed but have not yet closed — collapsed in October to a record low, according to a report released Wednesday by the National Association of Realtors. Pending deals fell 4.7% between September and October, and nearly 17% compared with a year earlier.

"It is somewhat puzzling that home sales would fall to levels last seen three decades ago, even as the U.S. population has grown by roughly 75 million," Lawrence Yun, the association's chief economist, said in a statement. "Either sizable housing demand is building in the background, or the dream of homeownership is increasingly out of reach for many Americans."

For would-be buyers, the turn in rates is a reminder of how quickly affordability can shift. A four-week slide had raised hopes that borrowing costs were finally easing after years of elevated rates. This week's jump shows that relief is far from assured — and that the path of inflation, and the Fed's response to it, will decide what happens next.
