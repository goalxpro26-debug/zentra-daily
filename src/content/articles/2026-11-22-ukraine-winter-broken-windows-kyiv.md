For weeks after a missile exploded in front of her apartment block, Oleksandra Horban kept finding pieces of glass in her small flat in Kyiv. She threw out her two-year-old son's soft toys, which were covered in shards. Outside her ground-floor home, mangled window frames were piled onto a mound of rubble, with glass mixed into the fallen leaves on the muddy ground.

"Even now we still find glass," she said.

As winter sets in, Ukraine is facing a severe shortage of replacement windows, with Russian missile and drone strikes continuing to damage buildings and leave residents exposed to the cold. Temperatures in Kyiv fell to minus 20 degrees Celsius (minus 4 Fahrenheit) last winter. Government help can take months to arrive, skilled installers are in short supply, and replacement costs have soared — hitting low-income districts near industrial zones hardest.

Horban lives in a Soviet-era "groundscraper", an enormous horizontal apartment block in the Ukrainian capital. A missile blast in October damaged a vertical section of the building and shattered hundreds of facade windows. Most broken windows have since been covered over with fibreboard or tarps. Some boards have small openings fitted with Plexiglas, to let a little daylight into the flats behind them.

Replacing the windows in Horban's flat would cost about $1,700 — more than three times the country's average salary. Government compensation can take three to four months. Many residents cannot afford to wait: without proper windows, flats are left open to cold, damp and mould, above all during power cuts.

Housing damage is among the largest costs of the war. An estimated 3 million housing units were destroyed or badly damaged by the end of last year, adding $90 billion to Ukraine's reconstruction needs, according to the World Bank.

Ukraine's window manufacturers can keep up with demand. The bottleneck is the people to fit them. Factories can produce a window in about 10 working days, but installation can be delayed far longer while customers wait for a crew to become available. Mykhailo Orlenko, executive director of the Association of Ukrainian Manufacturers of Transparent Structures, said businesses had been unable to exempt their employees from military service, including many installers. "The market lost a large number of these specialists," he said.

The industry is now investing in training a new generation. A vocational college in Kyiv opened a European Union-funded facility in March, where 26 tenth-graders have started a three-year programme to become window, door and facade installers. The programme was set up after industry associations lobbied the government to formally recognise window installation as a profession, allowing the industry to access grants and run structured training courses.
