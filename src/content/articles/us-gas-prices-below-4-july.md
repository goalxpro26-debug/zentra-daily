The average price of regular gasoline in the United States fell below $4 a gallon on Tuesday, the first time it has done so since July 20, according to data from motor club AAA. The national average stood at $3.97 a gallon, down from $4.14 on Election Day a week earlier.

The drop caps a slide that began in late September, when the national average peaked near $4.48, and it follows a sharp fall in crude oil. Brent crude, the global benchmark, plunged 13% last week — its steepest one-week decline since April 2020 — then slid a further 5% on Monday to its lowest point since August 31. On Tuesday it briefly traded below $90 a barrel.

Two forces are pushing prices down. The first is seasonal: the summer driving season has ended and the clocks have gone back, both of which cut demand for gasoline. The second is geopolitical. Oil tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz has picked up, easing constraints on global supply, and markets are betting that the midterm election results increase pressure for a diplomatic end to the war in Iran. President Donald Trump said on Monday that the war was "effectively over" militarily, and his administration is meeting mediators over Tehran's nuclear programme.

## Relief — with limits

For drivers, the change is real but partial. Gas prices remain more than 30% above where they stood before the United States and Israel launched strikes on Iran on February 28, and roughly 40% higher than at the start of the year. Diesel, used by heavy trucks and farm equipment, is still expensive: it fell from $6.28 a gallon on Election Day to $6.15, but remains above $6.

The picture also varies sharply by state. Twenty-six states already average below $4 a gallon, with North Carolina the cheapest at $3.26. At the other end, Nevada, California and Hawaii are still averaging close to $6.

## What happens next

Analysts expect further declines in the near term. Patrick De Haan, head of petroleum analysis at GasBuddy, expects the national average to reach about $3.75 by Thanksgiving, and AAA notes that prices typically fall by 10 to 30 cents a gallon over the winter months. Whether the sub-$4 level holds into spring will depend largely on whether negotiations in the Middle East continue — and on whether the recent easing in oil markets lasts.
