United Nations climate talks in Antalya have stretched into a second day of overtime, with a standoff over fossil fuels threatening to derail any final deal at the COP31 summit in Türkiye.

The two-week summit was due to end on Friday. Instead, negotiators spent Saturday night in hasty meetings, bracing for another long stretch on Sunday as the presidency tried to bridge the conference's defining divide: whether the final agreement will mention coal, oil and gas at all.

Late on Saturday, COP31 President Murat Kurum told a plenary session that a third and final text would be put to delegates. "We believe that we have put forward a text which all the parties can agree upon," he said. Delegations had until 03:00 GMT on Sunday to raise objections, with a session at 06:00 GMT to begin approving the deal.

The dispute began with the draft agreement. An opening draft from Australia, which is leading the talks, called on Monday for a faster phase-out of fossil fuels. A slimmer version released on Thursday removed every mention of them, after Saudi Arabia and other major oil and gas producers objected. The omission drew protests inside the venue and threats from several countries to walk away.

More than 130 countries want phase-out language restored. France and Panama led a late pushback on Friday; Panama's environment minister, Juan Carlos Navarro, said it was "not acceptable to have a COP or a cover decision that does not even mention fossil fuels, and we simply will not put up with it." The European Union warned the draft "risks turning this climate COP into a fossil summit."

Other issues remain unresolved, including industrial carbon capture and storage, whether a push to raise electricity's share of final energy use to 35 percent by 2035 should rely on renewables alone, and how much money richer countries will provide to help poorer ones adapt. An earlier draft dropped a $120 billion target for adaptation finance.

Pressure from outside the negotiating rooms has been blunt. On Saturday, 100 civil society groups wrote to the co-hosts warning they were "on course to preside over one of the most disappointing COPs" the United Nations has ever held, with only hours left "to save this COP from becoming a low point of three decades of climate negotiations."

The stakes are plain. The United Nations said before the talks that the world would likely blow past the Paris Agreement goal of limiting warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial temperatures, and October 2026 was 1.8 degrees warmer than the mid-1800s. For the first time, the agreement — the Antalya Accords — could acknowledge the 1.5-degree goal has been missed.

Expectations were already low amid an oil price shock and tensions over the war in Iran. If negotiators cannot agree, the summit could end with no deal at all and have to be suspended — leaving the question of fossil fuels, the main driver of climate change, unanswered for another year.
