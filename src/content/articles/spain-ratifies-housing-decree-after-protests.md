Spain's interim lower house ratified a package of housing measures on Thursday, a day after a general strike and major street protests over the housing cost crisis. The vote was decided by a single ballot: 35 in favour, 34 against.

The measures extend eviction protections for tenants judged by social services to be in a precarious position, as long as regional authorities cannot offer an alternative home. That protection runs until 2030. The package also freezes rent increases above the government's reference price for the neighbourhood, and caps other annual rises at 2 percent until the end of 2027.

It also bars property companies from buying homes below 70 percent of their value, to stop cheap wholesale purchases resold at a markup, and adds a 10 percent tax on tourist apartments, tax incentives for leasing, and government-backed loans for buyers' down payments.

## A second decree falls

The government had split its plan into two decrees, expecting one to fail. The second — automatic renewal of residential leases, with landlords who refused owing a year's rent in compensation — was rejected 34-35, after a centre-right Basque party that backed the first decree switched sides. Many lawmakers argued automatic renewal would push owners to pull homes from the rental market and drive prices higher.

The measures were first attempted in October, after anger over soaring rents erupted following the televised eviction — and subsequent death — of an 87-year-old woman in Madrid. Conservative parties blocked them, and Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez responded days later by calling an early election. The government then waited to try again with the smaller interim parliament, where it and its allies held a slightly more favourable balance. That interim lower house has 69 members, against 350 in the regular parliament, dissolved when the election was called.

## Everything now turns on November 29

How long they last will likely depend on the national election on November 29. The leading opposition party, the conservative Popular Party, says it will repeal the measures if it takes power — which polls suggest is a real possibility — and has instead pledged one million new homes plus cuts to taxes and red tape for developers.

"Yesterday, the right to housing won on the street and in workplaces, and I hope it will win again at the ballot box," interim Housing Minister Isabel Rodríguez told lawmakers.

The stakes are high: average rents have almost doubled in a decade, and the average monthly rent of €1,176 now equals about 98.7 percent of a young person's average monthly salary, according to the Youth Council of Spain. The central bank puts the housing shortfall at roughly 750,000 homes, expected to pass one million by 2028.

## What is still unknown

The decree is now validated law, but a change of government on November 29 could repeal it. The Popular Party has not said how quickly it would act, or what it would offer tenants covered by the new protections instead.
