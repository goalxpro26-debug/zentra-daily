A federal jury in Brooklyn has convicted Huawei of stealing trade secrets and defrauding banks, ending an eight-year criminal case against one of China's most prominent technology companies — while clearing it of the most serious charge prosecutors brought.

Jurors on Tuesday found Huawei and three affiliates guilty on 10 of the 12 counts they faced, including conspiracy to steal trade secrets, bank fraud, wire fraud and violating United States sanctions. They acquitted the company of racketeering conspiracy, the charge that it operated as a criminal enterprise, and of conspiracy to obstruct justice. The trial lasted more than two months, with prosecutors calling more than 60 witnesses and the defence calling 15.

## A two-decade pattern, prosecutors said

The case began in 2018, when US prosecutors alleged that Huawei misled HSBC, Standard Chartered and BNP Paribas about its business in Iran, allowing it to move money in and out of the country in violation of American sanctions. Charges added in 2020 accused the company of conspiring to steal trade secrets from US technology firms over two decades.

Prosecutors told jurors that theft was part of how Huawei grew into one of the world's largest suppliers of telecoms equipment. They presented evidence that it took source code from Cisco for its routers, antenna technology from the wireless firm Quintel, and hired a professor to gain access to a circuit board developed by the semiconductor company CNEX. Jurors also saw video prosecutors said showed an employee slipping part of a T-Mobile testing robot into a backpack.

## The defence: ordinary business, not conspiracy

Huawei's lawyers argued the government was criminalising normal competition. The banks knew about its business in Iran and were competing for its custom, they said, and prosecutors had cherry-picked unrelated incidents to support a claim of a 20-year conspiracy.

"It's about competition, not conspiracy; innovation, not theft; ordinary business dealings, not criminal misconduct," defence lawyer Brian Heberlig said, arguing Huawei had succeeded through "hard work, innovation, and investment in research and development."

The company welcomed the racketeering acquittal, saying it "confirms that these allegations lack factual basis and vindicates our consistent position over the past eight years." It added that the remaining charges lack legal or factual merit.

## A case that began with an arrest in Canada

The prosecution emerged in December 2018, when Meng Wanzhou, Huawei's chief financial officer and daughter of its founder, was detained in Canada on a US warrant. She had been secretly indicted for allegedly misleading an HSBC banker about Huawei's Iran business. She later entered a deferred prosecution agreement acknowledging the company had illegally conducted business in Iran, and returned to China. The charges against her were dropped.

US authorities have targeted Huawei for years, saying the company poses national security risks, and in 2019 placed it on a trade blacklist restricting its access to US technology. Experts say the case has nevertheless not undermined its business, as the company has diversified and become a key player in China's push into artificial intelligence.
