Japan will pay its full 2027 contribution to the International Criminal Court by the end of this year, Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi told the court's president on Tuesday, moving months ahead of schedule to shield the tribunal from American sanctions.

Takaichi informed ICC President Tomoko Akane of the decision during a 15-minute meeting at the Prime Minister's Office in Tokyo, and announced it in a post on X. "Japan has consistently supported the ICC and will continue to do so," she wrote. Akane, who had asked Tokyo for the early payment, thanked the prime minister afterwards and said the money would go toward staff salaries and keeping the court running.

Japan is the court's largest financial backer, covering about 15 percent of its budget — 4.6 billion yen, or roughly $28.9 million, in 2026 — followed by Germany, France, Britain, Italy, South Korea and Canada. Its 2027 contribution is expected to be roughly the same amount. Normally Tokyo sends its payment in April or later; paying in December hands the court its money before the sanctions bite.

The timing matters because the United States is pressing the court from two directions. Washington sanctioned Akane herself — the first Japanese national to lead the court — freezing her US assets and barring her entry in August, and broader American sanctions on the tribunal take full effect in April 2027. Reuters reported that the US is pushing to dismantle the body and encouraging member states to leave it.

The move puts Japan, one of Washington's closest security allies, publicly at odds with the American campaign against the court. It comes five days after Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin used their first three-way summit in Shenzhen to declare that the court "must not abuse" its jurisdiction — a statement aimed at an institution that has an arrest warrant outstanding for Putin.

Analysts say Tokyo's early payment could set an example. If other large contributors follow suit and pay early, the court could build a meaningful cash buffer before the sanctions take full effect, buying time while it fights for its funding and its future.
