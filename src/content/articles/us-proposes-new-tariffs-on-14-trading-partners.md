The Trump administration has proposed a new round of tariffs on 14 major trading partners, including China and the European Union, in its latest attempt to rebuild the sweeping import taxes the US Supreme Court struck down earlier this year.

The Office of the US Trade Representative (USTR) announced the duties on Wednesday after an eight-month investigation under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, a law that lets Washington penalise countries it finds are treating American businesses unfairly. The inquiry, which began in March, examined what the administration calls "structural excess capacity" — factories abroad producing far more steel, cars, computer chips and solar panels than their home markets can absorb, then selling the surplus cheaply overseas.

China faces the steepest charges. The US proposes a 20% excess-capacity tariff on Chinese goods, on top of roughly 25% in duties imposed during President Donald Trump's first term. That rate could rise to 40% if the trade truce between Washington and Beijing, due to expire on 10 January, falls apart. Vietnam faces up to 35% if its talks with Washington fail, or 20% if a deal is reached.

The other partners face lower rates, capped by trade deals already agreed with the administration. The European Union, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Switzerland would be capped at 15%; Cambodia, Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand and Bangladesh at 18%; India at 17% if a deal is finalised, or 25% if talks fail; and Singapore at 10%. Mexico and Norway were not targeted in this round. The USTR also proposed a 10% tariff on Brazilian Embraer commercial aircraft and higher duties on Brazilian sugar above import quotas.

The duties will not take effect immediately. They are subject to a public notice and comment period, with a hearing scheduled for 25 January, and are not expected to come into force until next year. US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said the measures would "level the playing field for American workers and chart a new, more sustainable course for the world economy."

The European Commission rejected the move, pointing out that tariffs on EU goods are limited to the 15% already agreed. The announcement also comes as the tariffs face growing political pressure at home: they have contributed to higher prices for American consumers, and a Reuters/Ipsos poll conducted after the Republicans' midterm losses found 62% of respondents supported efforts to repeal them.

The new measures mark the administration's most determined effort yet to restore the "Liberation Day" tariffs it announced in April 2025, which the Supreme Court ruled illegal in February because they had been imposed under emergency powers. Section 301, the route chosen this time, is widely seen as harder to challenge in court — though the caps agreed in existing trade deals mean many countries may see only a modest rise in the cost of trading with the United States.
