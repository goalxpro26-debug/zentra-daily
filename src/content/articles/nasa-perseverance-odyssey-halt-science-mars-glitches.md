Two of NASA's Mars explorers have paused their science work after being hit by separate technical problems, the agency announced on Thursday. Engineers hope both spacecraft can return to normal operations by the end of November.

The Perseverance rover ran into trouble with its main radio link home. On October 30, the rover's main UHF downlink radio — the system it uses to send data back to Earth through Mars orbiters — stopped sending its usual "heartbeat" signals to the onboard computer. The rover switched to its backup radio on its own and entered a protective safe mode.

After the rover recovered from safe mode, engineers found the backup radio was transmitting at only about half its expected power, so they stopped using that one too. Perseverance is now talking directly to Earth through its high-gain antenna and NASA's Deep Space Network, a link with much lower data rates. With the team prioritising diagnostic data, science work — including camera observations — is on hold, and no new raw images have appeared on the mission website since November 7. NASA said the rover is stable and communicating.

The timing is a sharp turn of fortune. The glitch appeared just 19 days after Perseverance set a new off-Earth driving record on October 11, covering 28.10 miles (45.22 km) in a single drive and passing the mark of 28.06 miles set by NASA's Opportunity rover.

Meanwhile, the Mars Odyssey orbiter lost the use of one of its four reaction wheels, which keep the spacecraft pointed in the right direction. Three working wheels are required, and Odyssey had already lost one in 2013, making this its second wheel failure. The problem appeared on November 4, when the orbiter entered safe mode; it stayed there until November 6 while engineers diagnosed the fault.

Testing has at one point got the faulty wheel moving on command again, and further tests are under way to confirm whether it can be recovered. Although Odyssey left safe mode about two weeks ago, it has not yet resumed science or data-relay work. NASA's goal is for it to return to both in the last week of November.

If the wheel cannot be recovered, the team would have to steer the orbiter using thrusters alone — and Odyssey has only about one to two months of hydrazine fuel left for that. Odyssey arrived at Mars in October 2001 and is the longest-operating spacecraft at the Red Planet; among its contributions, it produced the first global maps of the Martian surface's composition.

Two unrelated problems striking both spacecraft at once is an unlucky coincidence for NASA's Mars fleet, but engineers say both vehicles are stable while the teams work through diagnostics and recovery.
