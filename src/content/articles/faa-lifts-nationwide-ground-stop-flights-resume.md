The Federal Aviation Administration lifted its nationwide ground stop on Friday evening, allowing departing flights to resume after a communications failure kept planes on the ground across the United States for about five hours.

The agency announced at 8:15 p.m. Eastern time that the order had been lifted, after issuing it at 3:54 p.m. The stop was the first nationwide grounding since January 2023, when a key FAA computer system failed, and officials described it as the longest such halt in 25 years.

The disruption began when communications problems hit all 22 of the FAA's air route traffic control centers, which guide aircraft traveling between airports. The agency said there had been a temporary telecommunications outage and halted all departures out of an abundance of caution. Flights already in the air were allowed to continue to their destinations and land, and officials said every flight had been accounted for. Flights within Alaska and Hawaii were not affected.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said the FAA had restored full functionality at its air traffic control centers and reopened the airspace, but warned that it would take time to resume normal operations. Administration officials said new equipment being added to the air traffic control system had overwhelmed older hardware, causing intermittent communications and radar gaps. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin told reporters there was no indication the failure was triggered by a threat to the country, and the FBI does not believe a cyberattack or criminal activity caused it.

## A system out of position

Even with departures resuming, airlines could not return to normal schedules immediately. Thousands of aircraft and crews were left out of position by the halt, and officials said the backlog could take days to clear. They said they expected the system to be back to normal in time for the Thanksgiving travel rush.

The scale of the disruption was large. FlightAware data cited by CBS News showed more than 10,000 flights delayed and over 4,000 canceled. About 4,042 aircraft were in the air and heading toward U.S. airports when the ground stop was ordered, according to Flightradar24. Aviation analytics firm Cirium reported that at least 827 flights had been diverted by early evening, with most sent back to the airport they departed from.

Airports moved quickly to restart operations once the order was lifted. Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport, the world's busiest, said on Friday night that its flight operations were resuming. Delta Air Lines issued a travel waiver for its passengers, and airlines urged travelers to keep checking with their carrier for the latest information.

## What is still unknown

Officials have not given a detailed account of exactly which piece of equipment failed, or why the new technology overloaded the older systems. Duffy said he could not guarantee the problem would not happen again. The FAA said its technicians were investigating.
