Italy's data protection authority has fined Meta €805 million (about $887 million) after finding that the Facebook app on Android phones accessed users' photographs and videos without their specific consent.

The regulator, known as the Garante, said on Saturday that the app had reached into the photo galleries of Italian Android users, taking in pictures, videos and the information attached to them, without properly informing them or obtaining the consent the law requires. About 20 million people in Italy may have been affected.

At the centre of the case is a Facebook feature called "Get suggestions from gallery when browsing Facebook", which offers pictures and videos from a phone's gallery as ideas for posts while the user is browsing. The authority said the feature breached the Italian law that implements Europe's ePrivacy rules, which protect information held on a person's own device. Consent had been collected through a single, generic app-wide authorisation, and the feature was switched on by default.

The fine is the largest the Italian regulator has ever imposed, and amounts to roughly 0.47 percent of Meta's global revenue. The authority said Meta's lack of cooperation during the proceedings and an earlier violation, which drew a fine in 2019, were among the factors behind the size of the penalty.

Meta now has 60 days to switch the feature off by default and to ask users for separate, specific consent before reading their galleries. The company had not publicly commented by Saturday afternoon and has the right to appeal.

The investigation began in July 2025, after a complaint from a consumer protection centre in Bolzano, and included a technical analysis of the Facebook app. The Garante also rejected Meta's argument that the case should have been handled by another European regulator, saying the ePrivacy rules do not provide for the European Union's usual "one-stop-shop" mechanism, under which a company answers mainly to the authority in the country where it is based.

The proceedings did not examine whether any of the material was used to train artificial intelligence models — a practice Meta denies. The authority said it reserved the right to look separately at a newer feature, launched in 2026, that allows content from users' galleries to be processed in the cloud.

The ruling lands as regulators across Europe continue to test the limits of how much access apps can take to the most personal material on a phone, and what clear consent has to look like before they take it.
