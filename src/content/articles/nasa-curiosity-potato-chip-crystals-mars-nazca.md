NASA's Curiosity rover has found something odd in the rocks of Mars: crystal forms shaped like potato chips. The rover drilled into the bedrock at a site nicknamed "Nazca" to collect samples, and scientists are now working out what the shapes are made of — and what they reveal about the planet's watery past.

The find was reported in NASA's Curiosity mission blog on November 11. The site is an area of bedrock "containing disk-shaped (or, in a liberal interpretation, potato-chip shaped) crystal forms," the blog said. One of the mission's key objectives, it added, is to "better characterize these chip-shaped features."

The formations are small — close-up images taken by the rover's hand-lens camera from just inches away show irregular, curled shapes embedded in the rock. Early thinking among the team is that they may be pseudomorphs: later minerals that replaced the original crystals while keeping their shape. How those first crystals grew, and in what kind of environment, is still an open question.

## Drilling in for chemistry

Curiosity spent last week completing its drill campaign at Nazca, on the slopes of Mount Sharp, the mountain it has been climbing since 2014. Samples from the drill hole were delivered to the rover's onboard laboratory, the Sample Analysis at Mars (SAM) instrument, which can read the chemical makeup of Martian rock.

The rover also aimed its laser instrument, ChemCam, at crystal formations nicknamed "Colca" and "Ahi Veremos" to probe their chemistry from a distance, and photographed the shapes at night with its hand-lens camera under white-light and ultraviolet lamps.

The shapes matter because crystal forms can record history. Disk-shaped minerals often grow where water once flowed, so the chips may hold clues to a wetter Mars — but scientists are waiting for the SAM results before claiming more.

## Polygons up ahead

Curiosity has already moved on. In its latest update, published November 17, the mission team described nearby rocks crisscrossed with small, regular polygons ringed by raised ridges. Patterns like these can form when minerals lose water, when temperatures cycle, or when buried rock is compacted and then exposed again — each a clue to how Mars' habitability changed over time.

The rover, which has been exploring Mars since 2012, will keep climbing Mount Sharp, documenting the chemistry and textures of the rocks it meets. From orbit, the layers ahead look much like the polygon fields it has just crossed. As the mission's deputy project scientist put it, after more than 5,000 Martian days of exploring, "Mars is full of surprises."
