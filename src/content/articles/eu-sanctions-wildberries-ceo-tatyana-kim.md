The European Union has added Tatyana Kim, the founder and chief executive of Wildberries, Russia's largest online retailer, to its sanctions list, in a move that takes the bloc's measures against Moscow beyond banks and energy into everyday e-commerce.

The listing appeared in the EU's Official Journal on Monday. It freezes any assets Kim holds in the EU and bars her from travelling to the bloc or carrying out transactions there.

Brussels said it had targeted Kim because Wildberries is "one of the largest taxpayers" in Russia and "operates as a supply network for the Russian military by facilitating the sale of military and dual-use goods" — items that can serve both civilian and military purposes.

Ukraine has been attacking Wildberries facilities since mid-July. Kyiv says the platform sells items such as night-vision goggles, ammunition pouches and helmets alongside ordinary clothing, cosmetics and electronics, and argues that makes it part of Russia's war effort.

Kim pushed back on that claim in July, saying the site offered nothing that was not also available on major global e-commerce platforms such as Amazon or Alibaba. The Kremlin denied that Wildberries warehouses were used for military purposes.

Wildberries said on Monday that the sanctions would not affect its operations, because it holds no assets in the EU and does no business there, and that the company and other group businesses would continue to operate as usual.

Kim is ranked by Forbes as Russia's richest woman, with about $5 billion in assets. Born in Grozny in 1975 to a family of ethnic Koreans, she worked as an English teacher before founding Wildberries with her then-husband, Vladislav Bakalchuk, in 2004. The couple started out working from their apartment outside Moscow and rode the e-commerce boom of the 2010s to build Russia's biggest online retailer.

The listing was part of a much wider round of sanctions adopted by EU foreign ministers on Monday. In total, the Council added 113 names to its Russia lists — 12 individuals and 101 entities — aimed at sectors it says fund the war, including nearly 50 Russian banks and financial institutions, oil refineries and companies linked to Russia's shadow fleet of tankers. Alexander Chachava, the largest shareholder in rival marketplace Ozon, was listed in the same round. A further 32 listings were added under separate measures covering interference and cyberattacks.

The decision matters because it extends the sanctions net to a household consumer brand rather than a bank, an oil company or a defence firm. It signals that Brussels is willing to treat large commercial platforms as part of the war economy when it believes their goods reach the front line — and it puts other big Russian retailers on notice that their supply chains are now part of the argument. What remains unclear is whether the measures will bite in practice: Wildberries says it has nothing in the EU to freeze, and the company has shown no sign of changing what it sells.
