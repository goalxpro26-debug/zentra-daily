The US Securities and Exchange Commission is examining Nvidia's accounting practices, according to two people briefed on the matter, adding a regulatory investigation to the legal troubles already facing the chip giant.

The Wall Street Journal reported on Tuesday that the SEC was looking into Nvidia, without establishing the focus. Reuters is the first to report that the focus is accounting.

A spokesperson for the SEC declined to comment, and Nvidia did not immediately respond to requests for comment. It is not yet clear when the inquiry opened, or exactly which accounting issues are being examined. An investigation is not evidence of wrongdoing, and many close without enforcement action.

The probe lands as Nvidia, a linchpin of the artificial-intelligence boom, reels from a crisis that has sent its shares tumbling by more than a third.

At the centre of that crisis is the ousting of chief executive Jensen Huang, following revelations about his extramarital relationships, including one with Alice Huang, whom US authorities suspect of being a Chinese spy — a claim China has denied.

Alice Huang led Megaspeed, a Singapore-based company that was historically a large customer for Nvidia chips. US authorities have been investigating Megaspeed since 2024 over possible violations of export controls related to alleged shipments of Nvidia AI chips to China, the Justice and Commerce departments said this week.

One area now drawing renewed attention is how Nvidia reports where its sales come from. Last year the company changed its geographic revenue reporting to use customer headquarters rather than billing addresses, a change that removed Singapore from its geographical breakdown. The Financial Times reported on Tuesday, citing company accounts, that Nvidia made the change even as sales in Singapore soared to almost half of its global revenue.

Analysts and investors have also grown concerned about circular financing deals in the AI industry, in which chipmakers have used complex arrangements to help customers pay for their chips and to fund the data centres that use them. Critics say such deals can make it harder for investors to judge the quality of a chipmaker's revenue and its true financial exposure.

On the company's earnings call on Tuesday, interim chief executive Colette Kress — previously Nvidia's chief financial officer — said the company would be far more selective going forward about financing deals in the sector. The Justice Department has also been reviewing Nvidia's $12.9-billion deal to buy the open-source platform Hugging Face, alongside a broader antitrust investigation into the company.

The SEC's enforcement chief, David Woodcock, said last month that the agency was examining AI companies' corporate disclosures to make sure they give investors the full picture of potential risks. Under President Donald Trump the SEC has pulled back on enforcement more broadly, saying it will concentrate on bread-and-butter securities fraud, including accounting violations.

What the SEC finds remains unknown. For now, the accounting focus adds a new front to a crisis that has already cost Nvidia its chief executive.
