A wooden boat carrying about 100 migrants capsized off the coast of Senegal in the early hours of Wednesday, leaving dozens of people missing and prompting a search that stretched into a second day.

The pirogue went down near the village of Yenne, south of the capital Dakar, at around 02:00 local time, according to a representative of the village chief. A survivor questioned by police said about 100 people had been on board, including five women. The badly damaged boat later washed ashore on a beach near Yenne, where identity documents, several mobile phones and a school enrolment document were found among the belongings left behind.

Initial reports said one person had died, but some local media reported that at least four bodies had been recovered. Dozens remained unaccounted for as search operations continued through Wednesday and were due to resume on Thursday. "Cries could be heard coming from the pirogue that were audible even from the beach," Oumar Sow, the village chief's representative, told the AFP news agency.

The sinking came less than two weeks after a shipwreck off the north-western town of Thieppe killed at least 39 people, in what was described as one of Senegal's deadliest migration disasters. The two tragedies underline a renewed surge in dangerous sea crossings from West Africa, with migrants increasingly setting out in overloaded wooden pirogues bound for Spain's Canary Islands.

At a cabinet meeting on Wednesday, President Diomaye Faye expressed concern about the renewed increase in irregular departures by migrants hoping to reach Europe. He instructed the interior ministry to prepare a report examining the factors driving the dangerous journeys and proposing measures to tackle the problem. After the earlier tragedy, Faye had urged Senegalese people not to risk their lives attempting the crossings.

The scale of the danger is stark. The UN migration agency, the International Organization for Migration, said last month that it had recorded at least 454 deaths and disappearances off the West African coast this year alone — and warned the true figure was likely higher, because many boats vanish without survivors or any trace.

The pressure at the other end of the route is also rising. On 7 November, El Hierro, the smallest of Spain's Canary Islands, declared a state of emergency after more than 3,300 people arrived in about 30 boats since the beginning of the month. For those who set out from Senegal's beaches in the dark, the journey remains one of the most perilous migration routes in the world.
