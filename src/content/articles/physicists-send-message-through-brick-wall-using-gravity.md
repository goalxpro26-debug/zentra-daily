Physicists at the University of Queensland have sent a message straight through a solid brick wall — not with radio waves, light or cables, but with gravity itself. The message was a single word: "Gravity."

The experiment, published in the journal Science Advances, used an unlikely transmitter: a 1950s elevator in the university's Parnell physics building. The lift's counterweight weighs about 1.8 tonnes. By moving the elevator between two floors, the researchers shifted that mass between two positions — one standing for the binary digit 0, the other for 1.

On the other side of the wall, in an adjacent bathroom, sat a gravimeter, an instrument sensitive enough to detect minute changes in gravitational pull. The counterweight came within about 70 centimetres of the detector, separated from it by brick. Each shift in position produced a distinct reading, and the team decoded the readings back into letters.

The signal was extraordinarily faint — roughly five-billionths of Earth's gravitational acceleration — but it was measurable, and every bit arrived intact. The word "Gravity" took 49 bits to encode, and at one bit per minute the transmission took about an hour. "Our lift was snail-paced — it's from the 1950s," physicist Andrew White said in a University of Queensland statement.

The researchers were not generating gravitational waves, the ripples produced by colliding black holes. They were nudging the local gravitational field — the same everyday force that keeps feet on the ground. Because no known material can block that field, the message is, in principle, unblockable: surround the receiver with steel or bury it in concrete and the signal would still get through.

The idea grew out of an earlier project to weigh passing trucks by measuring their gravitational pull without touching them. If a moving truck could produce a readable signal, the team reasoned, a deliberately moved mass could carry a message.

Practical uses are distant. Gravity is weak, and its pull fades fast with distance. The researchers calculate that faster machinery and better detectors could reach around 15 bits per minute, and that quantum sensors with heavier moving masses could in theory extend the range to about 500 metres. Possible niche uses include reaching sealed bunkers or facilities where ordinary wireless signals cannot penetrate. Study co-author Matthew Davis was candid about the limits, telling The Guardian: "At the moment, it's very hard to imagine how it could be practically useful."

For now, the point is the proof. With an old elevator, a bathroom and an hour of patience, the team showed that gravity itself can carry information — slowly, faintly, and through anything in its way.
