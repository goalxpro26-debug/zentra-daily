The United States sees no need to resume major military operations against Iran, Secretary of State Marco Rubio said on Tuesday, arguing that sanctions and a naval blockade are doing the work that airstrikes would otherwise do.

"The economic campaign against Iran is so devastating that right now there is no need for major combat operations," Rubio told reporters in Paris after two days of meetings with foreign ministers from the Group of Seven. "That's what I told our allies here today."

Rubio said President Donald Trump had reached the same conclusion: the United States can achieve its objectives "by economic means" for the time being, and choosing not to strike is not the same as accepting a stalemate. "We don't need to conduct military strikes if we're achieving the same objective through economic means and economic pressure. That's not stalemate," he said.

## A pause, not a settlement

The remarks mark a shift in tone after eight months of war between the United States and Iran. Hours earlier, Trump had spoken of "positive momentum" toward resolving the conflict, and on Monday he said he would hold off on new strikes for the time being after appeals from countries in the region, including Qatar's prime minister during talks at the White House.

But the restraint comes with a warning. Rubio said that if Iran crossed the "red lines" set by Washington, the U.S. military "stands ready" and Tehran would pay a "terrible price." The pressure campaign itself has escalated beyond sanctions: Washington has imposed a counter-blockade on Iranian ports and in August announced what it called an "economic asphyxiation" campaign aimed at financially strangling Iran, already battered by years of sanctions.

The war has also carried a political cost at home. Trump has acknowledged that the Iran conflict was part of the reason Republicans were humbled in last week's midterm elections, with the economic fallout from the fighting weighing on voters.

## The Hormuz question

At the centre of the standoff is the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow exit route for Gulf energy exports. Iran has been blockading the strait, sending oil prices soaring, and Tehran says its offer to reopen the waterway within two days — if Washington agrees to a raft of conditions — remains on the table.

Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, has said the conflict "will not be resolved through military means, nor by intensifying sanctions," and has pressed Washington to address Tehran's demands. Iran also maintains that no direct negotiations are under way, only contacts through intermediaries such as Qatar and Pakistan.

For now, Washington's message is that economic pressure is compounding daily and the clock is on its side. Whether that patience holds will depend on what happens at Hormuz — and on whether either side's red lines are tested.
