Scientists have succeeded in growing nutritious potatoes in simulated lunar soil that are safe to eat, bringing a future moon base one step closer to feeding itself.

The research, supported by NASA and led by Oregon State University plant biologist Aymeric Goyer, was published on Thursday in the journal npj Microgravity. The team grew Modoc, an early-maturing potato variety with red skin and white flesh, in a greenhouse under sunlight and artificial light, at 72 degrees Fahrenheit (22 degrees Celsius) during the day and 64 F (18 C) at night.

The soil on the moon's surface, properly called lunar regolith, is crushed rock and dust — often sharp and glass-like — formed over billions of years by meteorite impacts. Unlike Earth soil, it contains no organic material and no nitrogen. "Lunar regolith does not contain any nitrogen, so no crop would grow without supplementing it," Goyer said.

The researchers mixed the simulated regolith with vermicompost, a nutrient-rich substance produced when earthworms break down organic waste. Potatoes planted in regolith with no vermicompost produced almost no roots or tubers. A mixture of just 5% vermicompost produced some roots and tubers, and at 30% vermicompost the yield was identical to potatoes grown under the same conditions in Earth soil — specifically soil from Hermiston, Oregon, a region known for impressive potato yields.

The compost also solved a second problem. Lunar regolith carries high levels of heavy metals such as copper, and without organic matter the potatoes took up too much of them. "Organic matter helps make heavy metals less available to the plants, so plants do not overaccumulate them," Goyer said.

There are limits to the result. The plants were grown in small pots, so the potatoes they produced were small — generally less than a third of an ounce (10 grams), even in Earth soil. A second study using larger pots has already produced potatoes of the size typically seen in stores, Goyer said. And nobody has tasted the lunar-grown crop yet: the harvest was used for laboratory analyses.

The work matters because both the United States and China intend to send astronauts to the lunar surface in the coming years, with plans for long-term bases. A local food source is considered vital, since transporting all needed food from Earth is impractical. Potatoes are a strong candidate: they provide more calories per land area than many other crops, are rich in vitamin C and potassium, and have a high satiety index.

Other crops are being tested too. A study published in March documented chickpeas grown in lunar regolith supplemented with vermicompost, and researchers are also working with tomatoes, lettuce and legumes such as alfalfa.

The research may also pay off on Earth. Study co-author David Handy, an Oregon State space biologist, said the techniques could help produce food in regions of low agricultural output that rely on shipments, such as inner cities and desert communities, and could help remediate contaminated soils.
