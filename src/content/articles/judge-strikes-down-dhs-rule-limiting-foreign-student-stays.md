A federal judge in Boston has struck down a Trump administration rule that would have put a fixed time limit on how long international students, exchange visitors and foreign journalists can stay in the United States.

U.S. District Judge F. Dennis Saylor IV issued the final ruling on Monday, November 16, 2026, vacating the Department of Homeland Security rule in full. He described the policy as "arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, and otherwise not in accordance with law," and granted the plaintiffs' motion for summary judgment while denying the government's.

Published in the Federal Register on July 17, 2026, the rule would have ended "duration of status" — the system under which international students have been admitted for decades for as long as they pursue their programme and maintain their status. In its place, F-1 students and J-1 exchange visitors would have been admitted for fixed periods of no more than four years, and would have had to apply to U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services for more time. The grace period after a programme ends would have fallen from 60 days to 30, and foreign journalists on I visas would have faced shorter stays.

The rule was scheduled to take effect on September 15, 2026, but Judge Saylor blocked it one day earlier, on September 14, finding that the department had failed to show a rational connection between the restrictions and their stated objectives. Monday's ruling turns that temporary pause into a final judgment on the merits.

The lawsuit was brought by a coalition of universities, educators and unions, including the Presidents' Alliance on Higher Education and Immigration, NAFSA and the American Federation of Teachers.

The judge found that the department had not properly assessed the rule's costs and benefits, had not produced enough evidence that it was needed to prevent fraud or protect national security, and had not seriously explored less restrictive alternatives — including greater use of the existing Student and Exchange Visitor Information System (SEVIS), which already tracks students through their schools.

The court also rejected the department's claim of broad discretion to define a course of study, noting it would have barred extended programmes, including PhDs, joint degrees and changes of concentration. "Put bluntly, DHS is seeking to prevent or discourage the very activity — providing educational opportunities for foreign students in the United States — that Congress sought to permit and encourage," the judge wrote, adding that "the statute simply does not grant DHS such broad and open-ended authority to undermine the F-1 visa program."

The decision restores the status quo for students and scholars already in the country: admission for the length of their programme, with the 60-day grace period intact.

## What is still unknown

The dispute is not necessarily over. The government's appeal of the September preliminary injunction remains pending before the U.S. Court of Appeals for the First Circuit, and the administration can appeal Monday's final ruling as well. As of publication, the Department of Homeland Security had not said whether it would do so.
