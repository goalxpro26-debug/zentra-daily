A robot has assembled part of a structure in orbit for the first time, working entirely on its own — no astronaut had a hand in the build.

The system, called ASTRA, was developed by researchers at the California Institute of Technology with funding from the U.S. Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA). It spent the past six months in space assembling sections of a reflector antenna, testing whether large hardware can be built where it will be used.

Until now, there have been only two ways to get a large structure into orbit: fold it up for launch and unfurl it in space, or send it up in pieces and have astronauts bolt it together over several missions. Both hit a hard limit — the size of a rocket's cargo fairing. ASTRA points to a third option: ship the parts and let a machine do the assembly.

## How the build worked

ASTRA, short for Autonomous In-Space Tensioned Reflector Assembly, is a robotic manipulator that picks up struts and joints from motorised dispensers and joins them into identical sections, or bays, of a perimeter truss. The target is a reflector antenna 1.4 metres (4.6 feet) across, made from composite fibre tubes, aluminium joints and a Kapton reflective surface.

The roughly one-cubic-metre payload launched on March 30, 2026, from Vandenberg Space Force Base aboard a SpaceX Transporter-16 mission, riding on Momentus Inc.'s Vigoride 7 vehicle. It carried two truss bays assembled on Earth, along with the parts for the rest.

In orbit, controllers released the launch restraints, pushed out the two prebuilt bays, and built a third bay from scratch. Photographs from the payload's 11 cameras confirmed each step. When problems appeared, the team tested fixes first on a twin of the system kept in a Caltech laboratory.

"This is the first time that a structure has been autonomously constructed in space," said Sergio Pellegrino, the Caltech professor who leads the ASTRA team.

## An unfinished antenna — and why that still counts

The demonstration did not complete the full antenna. The finished design calls for 11 connected bays; time limits and unexpected difficulties in the space environment meant the team built only the initial section. With the mission due to end in December, finishing all 11 bays now looks unlikely.

The researchers say the partial result still proves the point, and the lessons are shaping a simpler, refined design. Communication was part of the challenge: the ground team gets only seven or eight windows of about 15 minutes each day to talk to the system.

ASTRA is one of only two projects to reach the third phase of DARPA's NOM4D programme, which aims to enable structures "hundreds of meters or kilometers in scale," says programme manager Andrew Detor.

Structures built in orbit could serve as enormous telescope mirrors, giant radio antennas, or solar arrays beaming power back to Earth. "Once you get to the size limit where structures cannot be prebuilt on the ground and unfolded, you have to think about how to build them in space," Pellegrino said.
