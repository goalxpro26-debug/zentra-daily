Alleged Russian sabotage across Europe rose 16 percent in the year to September 2026, and the operatives behind it are becoming more skilled, the International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS) has said in a new report — warning that the campaign now carries a risk of a mass-casualty event.

The report, released on Thursday, says most operations are still carried out by unskilled recruits. But a semi-skilled tier has emerged that uses more complex methods, including improvised explosive devices fitted with timed and remote detonators. The use of explosives and arson roughly doubled over the year, according to the think tank's findings.

More than half of the recorded incidents between October 2025 and September 2026 targeted critical infrastructure — specifically military-industrial sites and transport networks. Germany and Poland, whose logistics and industry play the largest role in supplying Ukraine, remain the most targeted states, the IISS said.

Poland alone recorded 29 Moscow-linked incidents in that period, including an explosive charge in November 2025 that damaged a rail line linking Warsaw to the Ukrainian border.

One of the most serious episodes came on August 4 at Leipzig airport, a cargo and military logistics hub used by NATO and Ukraine's Antonov Airlines. A drone laden with explosives was discovered near a Ukrainian Antonov cargo aircraft used for military supplies, and investigators suspect a second drone collided with a DHL cargo plane soon after the airport suspended operations.

The warning comes as Britain on Tuesday told defence companies working with Ukraine's military that they face a heightened sabotage threat from Russia. Last year, a group of British men were jailed for an arson attack on a Ukraine-linked business that British authorities said was carried out at Moscow's behest, and in September a man was charged with passing details of a drone factory to Russian intelligence.

Ukraine's allies have accused Moscow of waging a Europe-wide campaign of drone and cyber attacks aimed at disrupting their support for Kyiv. Moscow has rejected the accusations, and Russia's embassy in London said this week that "Russia has no plans, reasons or intentions to attack the UK."

The IISS warned that a disaster causing mass casualties could bring calls for retaliation — and possibly divide European governments over how to respond. The institute said arrests of proxies remove individual operatives but are "highly unlikely to deter" the Russian intelligence services directing them.
