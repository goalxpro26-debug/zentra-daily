For the first time, scientists have found a signal in the blood that points to advanced chronic traumatic encephalopathy, or CTE, in living people. The disease, linked to repeated blows to the head, can today only be confirmed by examining the brain after death, leaving former athletes, soldiers and their families without a diagnosis while alive.

The clue is a protein fragment called eMTBR-tau243. Researchers at Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis were studying it as a marker of Alzheimer's disease, where a protein called tau builds into toxic tangles inside brain cells; the fragment's levels in blood had already been shown to track those tangles. Testing autopsy-confirmed samples, the team found one patient with unexpectedly high levels — but no Alzheimer's. That person's records showed the brain had advanced CTE instead.

The team then tested stored blood from 11 people whose CTE had been confirmed after death. Levels were higher than in healthy people, rose with disease severity, and distinguished advanced CTE from healthy participants with high accuracy. The study, published Friday in the journal JAMA, drew on 229 participants, with collaborators at the University of California, San Francisco.

## Why it matters

If larger studies confirm the finding, clinicians could one day use a blood test to monitor people at risk — former football players, boxers and combat veterans — and drug developers would finally have a way to tell whether a treatment is working. Co-senior author Kanta Horie said the work could "potentially open a new door for diagnosing and treating CTE in living patients."

## The limits are large

The result is very preliminary. The CTE finding rests on just 11 autopsy-confirmed cases, and the test could not reliably identify early-stage disease. Levels in CTE were also much lower than in Alzheimer's, which makes the measurement harder, and the fragment rises in Alzheimer's and other diseases involving the same toxic protein — so on its own it cannot tell CTE apart from them, a real problem since the two often occur together.

Outside experts urged caution. Dr. Rudolph Castellani, a neuropathologist at Northwestern University who was not involved in the research, called it an interesting but very preliminary finding that needs far larger studies. The university has licensed the measurement technology to C2N Diagnostics, a university startup, but the test has not been validated for diagnosing CTE and is not available to patients.

For now, nothing changes for people worried about CTE: no clinic can test for it until large validation studies are done. What the study offers is a direction — a measurable clue, found almost by accident, that a disease long visible only after death may one day be seen in life.
