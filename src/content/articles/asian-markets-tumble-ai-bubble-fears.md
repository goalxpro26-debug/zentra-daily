Asian stock markets opened the week with sharp losses on Monday, as doubts about the artificial intelligence boom deepened and last week's sell-off on Wall Street spilled into a new trading day.

South Korea bore the brunt. The Kospi index in Seoul slid as much as 5.8 percent in early trading before paring losses to close 3.3 percent lower. Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, the world's two largest memory chip makers and central players in the AI build-out, fell 3.1 percent and 2.5 percent respectively.

Elsewhere the picture was mixed. Hong Kong's Hang Seng Index dipped 0.1 percent after falling nearly 2 percent earlier in the session. In mainland China, the CSI 300 edged up 0.1 percent, reversing a decline of more than 1 percent. Taiwan's Taiex recovered from an early fall to close 2.2 percent higher. Japanese markets were closed for Labour Thanksgiving Day.

The losses extend a rout that began on Friday. Wall Street's benchmark S&P 500 closed 3.7 percent lower, entering a correction, the term for a fall of 10 percent or more from a recent high. The tech-heavy Nasdaq Composite dropped 5.3 percent, erasing all of its gains for 2026. Across Asia on Friday, the Kospi, Japan's Nikkei 225 and Taiwan's Taiex each finished between 5.1 and 6.2 percent lower, while Hong Kong's Hang Seng fell 1.9 percent.

At the heart of the sell-off is a question investors have been circling for months: whether technology companies can sustain the vast sums they are pouring into the data centres and computing power needed to train and run AI systems. Much of that spending is financed by debt. Research firm Gartner expects global AI-related investment to reach 2.7 trillion dollars this year and 3.6 trillion dollars in 2027, figures that have fuelled comparisons with the run-up to the dot-com crash of 2000.

Rajat Agarwal, an Asia equity strategist at Societe Generale, told Al Jazeera that investors were trying to judge whether the correction had gone too far after what he called a more than three-standard-deviation event in US tech shares on Friday.

Pressure on the sector is also building from politics and regulation. The Democratic Party's sweeping midterm win in the United States is expected to slow data centre construction in some states, while an alleged espionage scandal involving chip giant Nvidia has prompted calls for tighter limits on China's access to advanced chips.

There were signs the bleeding may be slowing. US equity futures pointed to a strong rebound on Monday, with S&P 500 futures up 2.2 percent and Nasdaq futures up 2.9 percent. Khoon Goh, head of Asia research at ANZ, noted that South Korea reported very strong exports for the first 20 days of November, with semiconductor exports up 294.4 percent year on year.

Even so, Goh said AI concerns were likely to dominate trading for the rest of the week, as investors try to work out whether the bottom is near.
