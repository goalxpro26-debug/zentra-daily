A rule of chemistry taught in classrooms for generations does not hold up when atoms get heavy enough, according to new research from Brown University. Chemists there have found direct experimental evidence that Einstein's theory of relativity can reshape the structure of a triple chemical bond — blurring a distinction textbooks treat as fixed.

The study, published in the journal *Science*, looked at molecules containing carbon and bismuth, a heavy metallic element. Using a technique called photoelectron spectroscopy, the researchers measured the energy needed to knock electrons out of the molecules with laser light, revealing the underlying structure of their bonds.

In the standard textbook picture, a triple bond is made of three separate connections: one sigma bond, formed by electrons overlapping head-on along the axis between two atoms, and two pi bonds, formed by electron density extending to either side of that axis. That model works well for lighter elements. But in the carbon–bismuth molecules, the team found something different: instead of one sigma and two pi bonds, the bond showed one pi bond and two hybrid bonds that mix sigma and pi character.

"The boundary between a sigma bond and a pi bond is now sort of smeared," said Lai-Sheng Wang, a professor of chemistry at Brown and the study's corresponding author. "We still have three bonds, but we don't really strictly have a sigma or a pi anymore."

## Why relativity enters the picture

Heavy elements have nuclei packed with protons, and their strong electrical pull can accelerate nearby electrons to a substantial fraction of the speed of light. At those speeds, effects predicted by Einstein's theory of relativity start to matter. One of the most important is spin-orbit coupling, in which an electron's spin — the quantum property that gives it a magnetic character — becomes tightly linked to its orbital motion. That linkage changes how electrons take part in bonding, and in heavy atoms it can wash out the clean split between sigma and pi bonds.

The idea that relativity matters in heavy elements has been around since the 1970s, Wang said, but the new work provides direct spectroscopic evidence for it in a triple bond. Doctoral students Deniz Kahraman and Jie Hui, working with Wang and colleagues, produced the carbon–bismuth molecules and examined them at temperatures near absolute zero.

The finding could change how chemists describe bonds involving heavy elements, and it may matter beyond the textbook. Bismuth is being studied as a non-toxic alternative to lead in next-generation solar cells, and the research could also help scientists working on quantum computing and quantum materials, where controlling electron behaviour is essential. The work was supported by the U.S. National Science Foundation and the U.S. Department of Energy.

What the study does not do is overturn chemistry as a whole. The familiar sigma-and-pi picture still describes the bonds of lighter elements accurately. The result redraws the boundary instead: for the heaviest atoms, relativity is not a footnote to bonding — it is part of the bond itself.
