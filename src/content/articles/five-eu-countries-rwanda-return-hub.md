Five European countries have announced plans to open a shared "return hub" in Rwanda for migrants who have been ordered to leave but cannot be sent back to their home countries.

Germany, Denmark, Austria, Greece and the Netherlands unveiled the plan on Monday at a joint press conference in Vienna, hosted by Austrian Chancellor Christian Stocker. The facility is expected to be ready to receive its first transfers in 2027.

The hub would apply to people staying illegally in the five countries who are subject to deportation and have exhausted their legal avenues to remain. The Dutch government said it would offer an alternative for cases where a person cannot be returned to their country of origin.

Once in Rwanda, deportees would be able to integrate into Rwandan society or return to their home country, with the right of residence and access to education, healthcare, the labour market and the legal system, according to the plan.

The scheme would begin as a small pilot. Diplomatic sources cited by AFP said it was expected to host about 100 people at first, with each of the five countries possibly sending around 20. The Dutch government said only single men would be returned in the first phase, and that the Netherlands would focus on people from countries in the same region as Rwanda.

The five governments say the aim is to relieve pressure on European asylum systems, increase the number of removals and undercut traffickers who arrange dangerous crossings across the Mediterranean. Rwanda's Interior Minister, Vincent Biruta, was reported as saying his country was committed to working with European partners on a safe, legal and dignified solution, and that unequal economic opportunity — a driver of irregular migration — needed to be addressed.

The plan differs from Britain's earlier Rwanda scheme, which would have sent asylum seekers to Rwanda to have their claims processed there. That deal never took effect because of legal challenges and was abandoned by the incoming Labour government in 2024. Downing Street has not ruled out the UK joining the new scheme and said it would watch it "with interest".

Human rights bodies have raised concerns. Human Rights Watch pointed to Rwanda's record, including repression of dissent, arbitrary detention and ill-treatment in detention, and argued that paying Kigali would cause harm and hand political leverage to a government responsible for serious abuses. The EU Agency for Fundamental Rights said a host country should respect fundamental rights, including not sending people where they face serious risk of persecution, and that unaccompanied children should not be sent to a return hub — something the Dutch government confirmed would not happen. The International Organisation for Migration said it could play a supporting role and was in discussions with some EU countries about return hubs.

The announcement comes as irregular migration into the EU is falling but remains politically charged. The European Border and Coast Guard Agency recorded almost 110,000 irregular border crossings in the first ten months of 2026, down 29% on the previous year. About 18,100 crossings were detected in October — roughly 6% fewer than in October 2025, but 22% more than in September, driven largely by a sharp rise in arrivals on the Western African route to the Canary Islands.

With elections approaching in several EU countries, governments are under pressure to show they can control migration, amid concerns about security, struggling economies and disillusionment with governing parties.
