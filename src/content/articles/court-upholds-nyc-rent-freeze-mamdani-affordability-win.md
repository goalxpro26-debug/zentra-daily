A Manhattan judge on Wednesday upheld New York City's rent freeze, dismissing a lawsuit from landlords who argued that the city's signature affordability policy was the product of a rigged process. The ruling keeps the freeze in place for roughly one million rent-stabilized apartments — but the judge paired the victory for Mayor Zohran Mamdani with a blunt warning about how he campaigned on it.

Justice Brendan Lantry of the New York State Supreme Court ruled that the landlords had failed to show the freeze was unreasonable, arbitrary or capricious. In a 20-page decision, he found that the city's Rent Guidelines Board acted within its authority when it voted in late June to freeze rents on both one-year and two-year leases — the first time the board has frozen both lease types at once.

The freeze applies to rent-stabilized apartments, which account for about one million of the city's 2.3 million rental units, and covers leases up for renewal between October 1, 2026, and September 30, 2027. It has already been in effect for weeks, and the mayor's office says it affects more than two million New Yorkers.

The landlords, led by Kenilworth Holdings LLC, sued in July, calling the board's decision a "sham process." They argued that Mamdani had effectively dictated the outcome by appointing board members who shared his pledge to freeze rents, and that the board relied on manipulated data. Lantry rejected those claims. "Elections have consequences, a premise that is the backbone of our democracy," he wrote, noting that Mamdani was fully empowered to appoint board members of his choosing and that their shared political views "does not indicate any level of collusion."

But the judge did not let the mayor off the hook. He called Mamdani's campaign promise to freeze rents "reckless and irresponsible," writing that the mayor of New York has no authority to raise, freeze or otherwise adjust the rents of stabilized units — that power belongs to the board alone. He also described communications between the board's chair and City Hall as "troubling," saying they cast "a dark shadow" on the board's claimed independence.

Mamdani welcomed the decision as "a victory for tenants, for affordability and for the principle that working New Yorkers deserve a city they can afford to live in." In a statement, he added: "Housing is not a luxury. It is a basic necessity. This holiday weekend, more than two million New Yorkers can breathe a little easier."

The landlords' lawyer, Randy Mastro, said the group was reviewing the ruling and considering its next steps, including an appeal. He noted that the judge had acknowledged the freeze could have "devastating consequences for small landlords" across the city.

The decision is Mamdani's second court victory this month, after an appeals court revived the city's tax on second homes valued at $5 million or more. The rent freeze sits at the centre of his affordability agenda, alongside a plan to build 200,000 affordable homes and preserve 200,000 more over the next decade. For tenants, the ruling means the freeze survives its first legal test; for the mayor, it comes with a judicial reminder that the promise was never his to make alone.
