President Donald Trump said on Monday that the war with Iran could end "fairly soon" and that further U.S. military action now looks unnecessary, in his clearest signal yet that Washington is turning toward a diplomatic end to the nine-month conflict.

Speaking to reporters at the White House after meeting Qatari Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani, Trump said Tehran was "talking very politely" with Washington and that momentum was building toward an end to the fighting. "We're seeing momentum toward the end of this conflict. I think it's going to be ending fairly soon," he said.

Trump described Iran as defeated in military terms, pointing to the destruction of its navy and an economy hollowed out by surging inflation. "Iran militarily is effectively over," he said. "We're just keeping it the status quo."

## Strikes still on the table

The president was careful to keep the military option open. He said the United States was "locked and loaded" and ready to resume strikes if circumstances changed, and that a renewed campaign could be far larger than the ones already carried out. At this point, however, he said that looked "quite unlikely."

In a message posted on Truth Social after the meeting, Trump said regional governments including the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, Bahrain, Kuwait and Qatar all favoured maintaining the current approach — keeping the U.S. naval blockade of Iran in place — rather than launching further attacks.

## A deal, with conditions

Trump said he wanted to give negotiations a chance and that there was a "good chance" of a deal, but warned he could reverse course if Tehran did not treat the talks seriously. He repeated that Iran would not be allowed to obtain a nuclear weapon, calling that point non-negotiable.

The U.S. naval blockade remains firmly in place, and a new American task force known as Operation Iron Gate, announced on November 7, is tasked with restricting Iran's overland trade in weapons, dual-use technology and sanctioned materials.

Iran, for its part, has pushed back. Its top security official, Mohsen Rezaei, called on the United States to accept Tehran's seven conditions for ending the conflict, accusing Washington of spreading "fake news" about imaginary talks.

The conflict began on February 28, when the United States and Israel launched missile strikes on Tehran, killing several of Iran's top leaders, including Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei. It has since driven a surge in fuel prices felt across the global economy.

No ceasefire or formal agreement has been announced, and it remains unclear what terms either side would accept. For now, the war appears to be on hold — with the blockade and the threat of renewed strikes both still in force.
