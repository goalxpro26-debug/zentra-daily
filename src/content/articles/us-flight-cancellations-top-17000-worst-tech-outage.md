More than 17,000 flights across the United States were cancelled over the weekend after a four-hour nationwide ground stop on Friday, making the disruption the worst technology-related blow to American air travel in 35 years.

The tally, compiled by aviation data providers Flighty and Cirium, passed the 16,900 cancellations caused by Southwest Airlines' systems meltdown over the winter holidays in 2022. It also surpassed the disruption of the 2024 CrowdStrike IT failure. The Federal Aviation Administration ordered every departure in the country halted on Friday, only the third nationwide ground stop in American history and the most severe since the September 2001 terror attacks.

The cause, officials say, was mundane rather than malicious. Crews were replacing outdated copper wiring with fibre-optic cable at a remote FAA facility in Arkansas when a router began flooding the air traffic control system with connection attempts, overwhelming communications and radar feeds. No evidence of a cyberattack has been found, and the precise chain of failure remains under investigation.

The effects rippled through the weekend. American Airlines was the hardest hit in absolute terms, with more than 5,300 flights scrapped since Friday, while JetBlue suffered the worst proportional impact, according to Cirium. More than 1.5 million passengers had been scheduled to depart on Friday alone.

Cancellations began to ease on Sunday, with just over 2,220 flights affected by late evening New York time, according to FlightAware. But the recovery was far from complete: more than 10,100 services were delayed on Sunday as airlines struggled to get aircraft and crews back into position. Major carriers including Delta Air Lines and Alaska Air Group said they expected a more normal service, or sharply reduced cancellations, on Monday.

The FAA said on Sunday that its system was stable, and agency administrator Bryan Bedford had said airlines expected to restore normal schedules by the end of the weekend. For passengers, the test will be Monday's operations — and whether a system built on ageing infrastructure can hold through the Thanksgiving travel rush now approaching.

The political fallout is already under way. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy has asked Congress for $50 billion to rebuild the air traffic control system, a request Democrats are resisting, arguing the agency already has money it has not spent. That fight now sits alongside the practical question the weekend exposed: how a single piece of equipment at a remote site could bring the busiest airspace in the world to a standstill.
