Russia's oil refineries are processing crude at their lowest rate in more than two decades, according to new estimates, as Ukraine's sustained campaign of strikes on energy infrastructure takes an increasing toll on the country's fuel supply.

Russian refineries were processing between 2.6 million and 2.7 million barrels of oil a day as of Monday, according to Rystad Energy, a Norwegian energy research firm. That is roughly half the level recorded on October 30, and below the previous multi-year lows seen during earlier waves of attacks this year.

The figures come from Viktor Kurilov, a senior oil analyst at Rystad, and were first reported by Bloomberg. Ukraine launched a new wave of strikes on Russian refineries at the end of last month, hitting plants almost daily. According to a Bloomberg tally, at least 18 refineries have been attacked since late October — though that does not mean all of their capacity has been knocked out. Some plants kept running at reduced rates after earlier strikes, while others restored production quickly.

The decline is already showing up at Russian petrol stations. Average retail gasoline prices rose 0.5% in the week from November 17 to 23, according to Russia's Federal State Statistics Service — the largest weekly increase since early September. The domestic fuel market has faced repeated shortages since the summer, and Moscow has responded by restricting gasoline and jet-fuel exports, allowing fuel imports, and redirecting supplies to the regions hit hardest.

Measuring the damage is difficult. Since launching its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, Russia has classified nearly all data on its oil industry. Rystad builds its estimates from oil supply chains, tanker movements, satellite imagery, and information from industry sources. Its latest figure does not yet include the effect of strikes on Wednesday against two refineries in the Samara region.

The squeeze on refining has had a knock-on effect beyond Russia's borders. Crude oil that Russian plants can no longer process is being redirected for export instead, adding to a surplus in global supply.

For Ukraine, the refinery campaign has become a central part of its strategy: by hitting the plants that turn crude into fuel, it aims to strain Russia's war economy and its domestic market at the same time. For Russian consumers, the cost is already visible in the price at the pump — and, with strikes continuing, analysts expect the pressure to persist.
