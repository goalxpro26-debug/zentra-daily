SpaceX has caught a spacecraft returning from orbit in the mechanical arms of its launch tower, a first for the company's Starship programme and a milestone in the long effort to make rockets fully reusable.

The Starship upper stage, known as Ship 42, slipped between the tower's giant "chopstick" arms above Launch Pad 2 at the company's Starbase site in Texas on Saturday afternoon, after spending more than 22 hours in space. It had lifted off on Friday for the 15th test flight of the world's largest rocket system, circled Earth 14 times and deployed 26 Starlink satellites before beginning its return.

The catch itself took seconds. After surviving the heat of re-entry, the 52-metre vehicle fired its engines, flipped upright and descended slowly through cloud over Starbase. Two fittings near the top of the spacecraft hooked onto the tower's arms, which had swung closed around it, leaving the vehicle hanging above the pad. SpaceX calls the recovery system Mechazilla.

## A partial success

The flight was only a partial success. SpaceX had aimed to recover both stages of the rocket on the same mission, but the Super Heavy booster — the lower half of the vehicle — never made it back to the tower.

The booster was due to return about six and a half minutes after liftoff. Instead its engines stopped firing earlier than planned, the catch attempt was called off, and the booster was diverted out over the Gulf of Mexico, where it exploded. SpaceX said its guidance system triggered a self-destruct after detecting a steering problem, and engineers are investigating the cause.

It was a setback, but not a new technique: SpaceX had already caught the Super Heavy booster on three earlier test flights. This was the first time the company had tried to catch the upper stage itself, the part of the system that actually travels to orbit.

## Why it matters

Recovering the upper stage matters because it is the harder half of the reusability problem. Boosters return minutes after launch; a spacecraft returning from orbit must survive re-entry heating, steer itself back to a precise point and arrive slow enough to be caught rather than landed. Doing that with a vehicle the size of a 17-storey building is something no other operator has attempted at this scale.

A fully reusable Starship would sharply cut the cost of putting cargo and people into orbit, and it is central to SpaceX's plans for frequent launches, satellite deployment and deep-space missions. NASA is counting on a version of Starship to serve as the lander for its Artemis Moon missions later this decade.

What remains unproven is the next step: turning both stages around quickly and flying them again. Saturday's catch shows the vehicle can come home in one piece. Whether it can be readied and relaunched on a short timetable is the test still to come.
