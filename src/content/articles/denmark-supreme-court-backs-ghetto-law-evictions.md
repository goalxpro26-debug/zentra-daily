Denmark's Supreme Court has ruled that the eviction of "non-Western" tenants from a social housing estate under the country's so-called "ghetto law" was not discriminatory, handing the government a major legal victory for its hard line on immigration.

The court on Monday rejected an appeal by seven families whose leases were terminated in 2022 by the housing association Boliggarden. The families were among 91 households evicted from the Nøjsomhed estate in Helsingør, north of Copenhagen, most of them classed as "non-Western". Lower courts had already dismissed their claims.

At the heart of the case was whether the law's dividing line — between immigrants and descendants from "non-Western" countries, and everyone else — is effectively a proxy for ethnicity. The court said it was not: both groups contain people of many different ethnic origins, and people of the same ethnic origin can fall into either group. No information had been presented about the ethnic background of the evicted residents, and the court found no evidence that particular ethnic groups had been put at a particular disadvantage.

## A law aimed at 'parallel societies'

The legislation, adopted in 2018 and officially known as the Parallel Society Act, targets social housing areas where more than half of residents are immigrants or descendants from "non-Western" countries, alongside at least two of four socio-economic thresholds covering unemployment, low income, low educational attainment and criminal convictions. Such areas must reduce the share of public family housing to no more than 40 percent by 2030, which can mean demolishing buildings, selling homes to private landlords or converting them — and relocating residents. Tenants' associations say some 11,000 people will be forced to leave their apartments and be rehoused elsewhere by 2030.

The government describes the law as a bid to promote integration and combat segregation in disadvantaged neighbourhoods. Denmark officially stopped referring to "ghettos" in 2021, but the nickname has stuck. Housing Minister Jacob Mark said he was satisfied with the ruling, saying the law "strengthens security, social cohesion, and integration".

The decision is not the last word on the law's compatibility with European anti-discrimination rules. The EU's top court ruled last December that the legislation could amount to discrimination on ethnic grounds, but left the final determination to Denmark's courts. The Supreme Court has now made that determination — in the government's favour.

The families' lawyer, Eddie Khawaja, said the court had focused more on the wording of the law than on how it was implemented on the ground, and that in his view "non-Western" is "a proxy for ethnic origin". One of the plaintiffs, Riad, told AFP: "We're obviously disappointed."

Denmark has pursued a tough line on immigration for more than three decades, and the housing law — widely criticised by tenants and their associations — retains broad political support. Monday's ruling removes a major legal obstacle to it, and signals that the courts will not block policies that sort residents by origin, so long as the sorting is framed in terms of nationality rather than ethnicity.
