Most materials wear out with use: bend a paper clip back and forth and it snaps. A team at Washington University in St. Louis has now built a material that breaks that rule: a sugar-based gel that gets stronger the more it is bent, and stronger in exactly the places that take the strain.

The work comes from the lab of Chenfeng Ke, a professor of chemistry, and is described in a study published in *Nature Materials*. The material is a hydrogel — a soft, water-absorbing solid — made from a biodegradable blend of sugar crystals and polyethylene glycol, a compound widely used in laxatives and other health products. It is patented and compatible with 3D printing.

Its secret is a sugar compound called cyclodextrin, which forms tiny ring-shaped structures. When the gel is stretched or bent, large clusters of these rings break apart, slide along the polymer chains, and reorganize into smaller crystalline structures as the material relaxes. The moving rings reinforce the zone under stress while the rest of the gel stays soft and pliable.

"We bent it 10,000 times, and the material performed just as we predicted," Ke said. "The polymers in the hydrogel become more uniform and organized, making it stronger in exactly the places where the strain is happening."

## A lesson borrowed from the body

The design takes its cue from biology. Collagen, the protein that gives skin its strength and elasticity, also adapts to the forces placed on it, and the new gel works on a similar principle: reinforcement arrives only where it is needed. "Some of the most impressive smart materials are found in biology," Ke said. "We used nature for inspiration."

To test the idea, the team mounted the gel on a flexible, knee-like joint and bent it repeatedly. Instead of fatiguing, the material held up and reorganized itself, with its internal network growing more ordered at the points of greatest strain.

Ke sees medical uses first. Because the gel is made of sugar and polyethylene glycol, it is fully compatible with biological tissue, which makes it a candidate for supporting artificial joints or repairing damaged tissue. In either setting, the parts of an implant that never bear load would stay soft, while the stressed regions would firm up on their own.

Ke is a chemist, not an orthopedist, and he is quick to note that surgeons and medical-device specialists may see applications he has not yet imagined. His team is now looking to work with experts at WashU Medicine and elsewhere to explore the possibilities.

For now the material exists only in small batches, tens of grams at a time. But with the concept demonstrated, the researchers believe production could scale. "We've shown it's possible to produce smart materials with a 3D printer and the right chemistry," Ke said. "We see a lot of possibilities."
