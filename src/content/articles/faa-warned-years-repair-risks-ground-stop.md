The nationwide ground stop that halted every departure in the United States on Friday did not come out of nowhere. The Federal Aviation Administration had been warned for years that the ageing network at the heart of air traffic control was at risk of failure, according to reporting by the Washington Post — and the warnings are documented in the public record.

The stop began on Friday afternoon, November 13, after communication problems struck several regional centres that guide aircraft between airports. It lasted more than four hours before departures resumed at about 8:15 p.m. Eastern time. It was the longest nationwide ground stop in 25 years, the first since January 2023, and only the second since September 11, 2001.

More than 3,000 flights already in the air landed safely, and officials said no one was in danger. On the ground, the disruption was vast: reports put it at more than 11,000 flights affected, with cancellations running into the thousands.

## What failed

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy blamed overloaded, decades-old network equipment. Contractor L3Harris Technologies was replacing sections of the system's old copper wire with new fibre when it overloaded, he said. The ageing switches and routers began feeding control centres inconsistent information, prompting the decision to stop traffic.

"We use 1980s little copper lines," Duffy said at a White House briefing on Friday night. "It's an old system that went down and went down everywhere."

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said officials had seen no evidence of a cyberattack.

## The warnings on file

The fragility of the system was no secret. A review commissioned by the FAA in 2023 called the agency's telecom network obsolete and predicted more outages "due to an underfunded migration to IP-based telecommunications." In March 2025, the air traffic controllers' union testified to Congress under the heading "The FAA's Looming Telecommunications Crisis."

Government auditors added their own. A September 2024 assessment by the Government Accountability Office identified 105 air traffic control systems as unsustainable or potentially unsustainable. And on September 14 this year, auditors warned the FAA lacked a reliable cost estimate for modernisation and had not integrated the schedules of more than 11,000 individual projects.

There is also a question about Friday's work itself. The Professional Aviation Safety Specialists, the union representing the agency's technicians, said the FAA normally asks them to assess the risks of upgrades or repairs in advance — but that did not happen in this case.

## What is still unknown

No official root cause has been announced, and the FAA has not published a detailed account of what failed or why several centres were affected at once. Duffy said he could not guarantee the problem would not happen again.

The administration is pressing Congress for money to replace the system. A formal request sent the week of the outage sought $30.1 billion for airport and air traffic modernisation, while Duffy has floated a $50 billion emergency overhaul. FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford said air travel should be back to normal by the end of Sunday: "the worst is behind us."
