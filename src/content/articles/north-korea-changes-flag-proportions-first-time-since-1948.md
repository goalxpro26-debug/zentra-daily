North Korea has changed the proportions of its national flag for the first time since the state was founded in 1948, making the flag noticeably shorter and squarer under a revised constitution published this week.

Article 166 of the revised constitution, posted on Naenara, the state's external propaganda website, now sets the flag's height-to-width ratio at 1 to 1.65, replacing the 1-to-2 ratio that had stood for 78 years. The change was reported on Friday by South Korea's Yonhap news agency, citing the amended text.

Everything else about the flag stays the same. It keeps its broad red stripe across the centre, narrow white stripes above and below it, blue stripes along the top and bottom edges, and a red five-pointed star inside a white circle near the hoist. The flag is still wider than it is tall — just less stretched than before.

## A new shape, the same symbols

The constitution also restates what each element of the flag is meant to stand for. The red stripe represents the blood shed by fighters against Japanese rule during the 1910–45 colonial period. The white circle and stripes stand for loyalty to the country's leader and the ruling Workers' Party of Korea. The blue stripes represent the people's revolutionary spirit, the pursuit of peace and friendship, and national sovereignty.

The proportions rule had survived untouched in the law until now, including through a constitutional revision in September 2023. Its first change in nearly eight decades comes as leader Kim Jong Un works to put his own stamp on the state he inherited — building a distinct governing style and national identity while keeping the symbols of his predecessors in place.

## Part of a wider redefinition

Observers see the flag change as one piece of a broader redrawing of North Korean identity. The revised constitution also drops language about reunification with the South and instead defines South Korea as a separate, hostile state — a position Kim set out in late 2023 and early 2024. In 2024, the lyrics of the national anthem were also revised, replacing a phrase referring to the whole Korean Peninsula with words meaning "this world."

Analysts quoted in regional coverage described the flag revision as an attempt to craft a new national image for the Kim era: the familiar colours and the red star remain, but the specifications are now his.

What is not yet clear is how quickly the new proportions will appear on flags in everyday official use. The change exists so far in the constitutional text and on the Naenara website; Pyongyang has issued no separate public statement about it.
