El Salvador's president has released video showing four-legged robots fitted with rifles taking part in an operation to retake Haiti's national stadium from gang control, in what appears to be the first time armed robot dogs have been used in a live security operation in the Americas.

The footage, posted by President Nayib Bukele on Thursday, shows the machines moving down a hallway and up a set of stairs inside the Sylvio Cator Stadium in Port-au-Prince, rifles mounted on their backs. Soldiers are seen in the same video sweeping the stadium's stands and fitting the weapons onto the robots on the pitch.

The stadium had been under gang control for more than two years, forcing Haiti's national team to play its home matches abroad. Under a bilateral agreement between the Haitian government and the government of El Salvador, a joint operation involving the Haitian National Police, the Haitian Armed Forces, a government task force and Salvadoran special forces took control of the ground and secured it.

## What the robots did — and did not — show

Exactly how the robot dogs assisted in the operation is not clear from the footage. Bukele did not elaborate on their role, and no robot is shown firing a weapon or engaging a target. The video also does not identify the robots' make or model, or the type of rifle mounted on them. Open-source analysts have suggested the machines resemble a commercially sold Chinese quadruped, but that identification has not been confirmed by any official source.

Bukele said "several human skulls" were found inside the stadium during the operation, a claim that has not been independently confirmed. He said forces would now establish an operational base at the stadium to advance deeper into gang-held territory, adding that his forces now have "the upper hand on the ground."

Before the operation, Bukele had demanded that gang members leave the area, warning that those who stayed to fight would face the consequences.

## Why Salvadoran troops are in Haiti

El Salvador's involvement follows an ambush in October in which Salvadoran nationals were seized. Bukele sent troops to Haiti under the bilateral security agreement, and the stadium operation is the most visible result so far. Haitian police have also claimed credit for the recapture, and the two accounts have not been fully reconciled in public reporting.

The use of armed robots raises questions that go beyond this one operation. The machines are built on commercially available platforms, and the footage shows how quickly off-the-shelf technology can be adapted for military use. Neither the manufacturer of the robots nor the rules governing their use in the field have been disclosed.

For Haiti, the stadium's recapture is a symbolic win in a much larger fight. Gangs control large parts of Port-au-Prince, and reclaiming a national landmark — one the country hopes to use again for its own matches — matters to a public that has watched the state lose ground for years. Whether the stadium base becomes a springboard for a wider offensive, as Bukele promises, remains to be seen.
