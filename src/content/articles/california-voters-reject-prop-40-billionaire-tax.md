California voters rejected Proposition 40, a proposed one-time 5% tax on the wealth of the state's billionaires, the Associated Press projected on Monday, ending one of the most expensive ballot fights in the state's history.

The measure was trailing 52.8% to 47.2% when the AP called the race. It would have been the first modern tax on wealth in the United States, where taxes are almost always assessed on income rather than on assets.

## What the measure would have done

Proposition 40 would have imposed a one-time 5% tax on the net assets of California residents worth more than $1 billion, with residency counted as of January 1, 2026. Roughly 200 people would have been affected.

Supporters, led by the Service Employees International Union-United Healthcare Workers West, estimated the tax would raise about $100 billion over five years, with at least 90% of the money going to health care. The union said the revenue was needed to offset federal Medicaid cuts signed into law last year. The state's nonpartisan Legislative Analyst's Office offered a more cautious estimate, in the tens of billions, and warned the measure could cost the state up to $1 billion a year in income-tax revenue if wealthy residents left.

## A record-breaking campaign

Opponents outspent supporters by more than ten to one. Billionaires and business groups poured more than $333 million into defeating the measure, including $102 million from Google co-founder Sergey Brin — the largest single contribution. Supporters raised roughly $30 million.

Opponents also placed two competing measures on the ballot designed to blunt the tax. One, Proposition 42, would bar the state from taxing personal property and financial assets, effectively banning wealth taxes. Its outcome remained too close to call late Monday.

## A split among Democrats

The result exposed a divide inside California's Democratic Party, which backed the measure, along with Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Ro Khanna. Governor Gavin Newsom opposed it, arguing that a wealth tax should be national rather than imposed by a single state.

The union behind the measure vowed to put a billionaire tax back on the ballot in 2028 if the state legislature does not come up with another way to fund the health care cuts by then.

The defeat does not amount to a broad rejection of higher taxes on the wealthy. In the same election, about 60% of voters approved a separate measure making the state's higher income-tax rates permanent for top earners, rather than letting them expire in 2031.
