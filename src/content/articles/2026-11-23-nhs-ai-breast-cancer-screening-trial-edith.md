The NHS has begun recruiting for the largest trial anywhere in the world of artificial intelligence in breast cancer screening, with nearly 700,000 women expected to take part across the United Kingdom.

The study, known as EDITH, will test whether AI can help specialists find cancers sooner and review screening images more efficiently. Recruitment has started at Cambridge University Hospitals, the first of around 30 NHS screening sites that will join over the coming months.

Breast cancer is the most common cancer in women, with around 55,000 people diagnosed each year in the UK. The NHS screening programme invites women aged 50 to 71 for a mammogram every three years, carrying out about 2.1 million screenings a year and preventing an estimated 1,300 deaths annually.

At present, two specialists examine each mammogram for signs of cancer. The trial will test whether an AI-assisted tool working alongside a specialist can spot cancers too small to see or feel, so that most images need to be checked by only one specialist. If it works, the change could free hundreds of radiologists and other specialists to see more patients who need cancer tests and treatment, and help cut waiting lists.

The £10 million study is funded by the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR) and led by the University of Warwick, the University of Cambridge and Cambridge University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust, working with NHS England.

Health Secretary Yvette Cooper, who visited the trial team at Addenbrooke's Hospital in Cambridge, called it the biggest AI breast cancer trial in the world. She said the technology could help women get a diagnosis and start treatment much more quickly, while stressing that proper safeguards must be in place so AI is used safely and effectively.

Professor Fiona Gilbert of the University of Cambridge, a co-chief investigator of the trial, noted that millions of women have a mammogram each year and fewer than one in a hundred will have cancer. Technology that makes reviewing those images more efficient, she said, would let specialist breast radiologists spend more time caring for patients with cancer.

Researchers will also track how many cancers the AI-assisted approach detects, how often women are called back for further tests, and whether screening quality and safety are maintained throughout. The trial was first announced in 2025; results will take several years to gather and will be published in a scientific journal.
