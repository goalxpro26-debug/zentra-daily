NASA's Voyager 1 is about to set a distance record no human-made object has ever reached. On Wednesday, November 18, the spacecraft will sit one light-day from Earth — 16.09 billion miles (25.9 billion kilometres) away, the exact distance light travels in 24 hours.

The crossing is expected at 5:16:07 a.m. Eastern time. From that moment, a radio signal sent from Earth will take precisely a full day to reach the probe, and its reply will take another day to come back. Every exchange with the spacecraft will now take about 48 hours.

Voyager 1 launched from Cape Canaveral on September 5, 1977, on what was planned as a five-year mission to Jupiter and Saturn. It flew past both planets in 1979 and 1980, then kept going. In August 2012 it crossed the heliopause — the boundary where the Sun's wind gives way to the space between stars — becoming the first human-made object to enter interstellar space.

It is still travelling at roughly 38,000 miles per hour (61,000 km/h), and it has held the title of most distant human-made object since 1998, when it overtook Pioneer 10. Its twin, Voyager 2, launched two weeks earlier but on a slower trajectory, will not reach the one-light-day mark until 2035.

## A yardstick, not a border

Nothing on the spacecraft will change when it crosses the line. The milestone is symbolic — a way of picturing a distance that is otherwise hard to grasp. A light-day is about 173 times the distance between Earth and the Sun. At its current speed, Voyager 1 would need around 18,000 years to cover a full light-year, and more than twice that before it comes near another star.

The probe's longevity is itself the story. Its computers hold about 69 kilobytes of memory, its transmitter puts out roughly 23 watts, and it still sends science data home from beyond the solar system. To keep it alive, NASA has switched off instruments over the years, most recently the low-energy charged particle instrument in April 2026, leaving the magnetometer and the plasma wave instrument still operating.

Project manager Suzanne Dodd, who leads the mission at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, called the spacecraft "a national treasure" and "really extraordinary" — a machine launched 49 years ago that is still working, still talking, and still setting records.

## How NASA is marking the day

NASA is inviting the public to take part. After sunset on Tuesday, November 17, people around the world are asked to shine a light toward the sky, flashing "VGR" — the mission's call sign — in Morse code as part of an event called "Shine Bright for Voyager's Light-Day." On Thursday, November 19, the Jet Propulsion Laboratory will host a moderated discussion with mission members about the twin probes and the challenge of keeping them operating.

Voyager 1's most famous moment came in 1990, when it turned its camera back toward home and took the "Pale Blue Dot" photograph — Earth as a speck of light seen from beyond Neptune, the most distant picture of our planet ever taken. Thirty-six years later, the same spacecraft is still moving away, one light-day at a time.
