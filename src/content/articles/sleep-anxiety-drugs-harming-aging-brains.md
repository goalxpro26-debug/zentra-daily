Older adults living with dementia or other cognitive disorders are especially vulnerable to the harmful effects of common anxiety and sleep medicines — and a new American study finds that nearly one in three of them are still taking drugs that could make their thinking worse.

The research, from the University at Buffalo and published in the *Journal of the American Geriatrics Society*, tracked the medication use of 2,796 older adults with cognitive disorders who lived independently. Using data from the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey, the team examined a sample representing about 29.7 million older Americans between 2011 and 2022.

The news was encouraging, up to a point. Exposure to potentially inappropriate medicines fell from 39.3% in 2011 to 29.3% in 2022 — a steady, decade-long improvement. But the researchers were clear that the remaining share is still too high. "One-third of our target population is still exposed to these harmful medications, which isn't good," said first author Haowen Hsu, a postdoctoral fellow in pharmacy practice at the university.

The drugs in question act on the central nervous system: benzodiazepines such as Xanax, prescribed for anxiety, and sleep medicines such as Ambien. In older adults whose nervous systems are already compromised by cognitive disorders, they can impair memory and thinking, as well as balance and coordination. The potential consequences include more emergency-room visits, longer and more frequent hospital stays, declining mental health, and an increased risk of premature death.

To identify the risky medicines, the team used the American Geriatrics Society's Beers Criteria, a list of roughly 100 drugs that may pose risks to older adults. They then focused on those that present additional dangers for people with diagnosed cognitive disorders.

The answer, the researchers say, is not to stop medicines abruptly but to review them carefully. Older adults who have taken these drugs for years may benefit from a medically supervised reduction in dose, or gradual discontinuation — a process the researchers call "deprescribing." In some cases a milder alternative can be substituted. They also suggest that care teams use tools such as the Drug Burden Index, which measures the combined effect of medicines acting on the central nervous system.

The work is part of Team Alice, an interdisciplinary initiative at the University at Buffalo dedicated to medication safety. It is named for Alice Brennan, who died in 2009 after being prescribed a muscle relaxant flagged as potentially inappropriate under the Beers Criteria. The prescribing error went unrecognised, and she died after six weeks of preventable complications. Her daughter, Mary Brennan-Taylor, shared the story with university researchers in the hope of driving systemic change.

The findings land as the world's population ages rapidly. According to a recent US Census Bureau report, adults aged 65 and older now outnumber children from birth through age five worldwide for the first time in human history — and nearly 75% of those older adults live with at least two chronic health conditions, which often means taking more medicines.

The study did not examine what caused the decade-long decline in risky prescribing, though Hsu points to growing awareness among researchers and clinicians. Further research is still needed to establish whether better medication use improves quality of life and reduces hospital admissions for older adults with cognitive difficulties. For now, the researchers say, thorough medication reviews at routine appointments are the simplest safeguard — because the patients least able to tolerate these drugs are the ones still most likely to receive them.
