The United States Supreme Court on Monday, November 9, allowed the Trump administration to move ahead with border wall construction in the remote Big Bend region of West Texas, lifting a lower court's order that had halted the work for the past month.

The court granted the government's emergency request to pause an injunction issued on October 2 by U.S. District Judge Kathleen Cardone. That injunction had blocked construction of barriers and roads across the Big Bend sector while a lawsuit against the project moved through the courts. The Supreme Court's action lets construction resume while the case continues — it is not a final ruling on whether the project is lawful.

In an unsigned order, the court said Congress has expressed a "clear preference for expeditious border construction," and that the government's interest in border security outweighs "any aesthetic, recreational, and environmental harm" claimed by the challengers. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson was the only justice to publicly dissent.

## The legal fight

The lawsuit was brought by the Center for Biological Diversity, a Big Bend river guide and landowner, and Friends of the Ruidosa Church, a group working to preserve a historic adobe church in Ruidosa, Texas, that it says is threatened by the construction.

At the centre of the dispute are waivers issued by the Department of Homeland Security that set aside a broad set of environmental and conservation laws — including the Endangered Species Act, the National Wild and Scenic Rivers Act, and the National Park Service Organic Act — to allow the building to proceed. It was the first time such waivers had been applied inside a national park.

The court said the government was "exceedingly likely to prevail" on its argument that a 1996 federal law limits lower courts' ability to review border barrier decisions, meaning Judge Cardone likely lacked jurisdiction to block the work.

## A vast, remote stretch

The Big Bend sector spans 517 miles along the Rio Grande — more than a quarter of the entire U.S.-Mexico border — and includes Big Bend National Park and Big Bend Ranch State Park. It is rugged, sparsely populated terrain, and it records the lowest number of migrant apprehensions of any Southwest border sector. Judge Cardone noted that the sector accounts for only 0.3 to 2.2 percent of apprehensions, despite its size.

The administration says most of the construction, spanning more than 300 miles, will take place on privately owned land, though work is also planned inside the national park. The waivers were issued between February and July by Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin and his predecessor Kristi Noem, who designated the region an area of high illegal entry.

The plan has drawn bipartisan criticism, including from lawmakers who object to building through a protected landscape known for its canyons and desert wilderness.

## What happens next

The case itself remains alive. The challengers can still press their claims in the district court, and the Supreme Court's order does not decide whether the waivers themselves are lawful. But with the injunction lifted, construction can proceed in the meantime — and environmental groups say the work could cause permanent damage to protected wilderness before the courts reach a final answer.
