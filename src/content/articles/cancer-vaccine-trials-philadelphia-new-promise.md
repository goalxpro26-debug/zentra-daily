After decades of disappointment, cancer vaccines are showing real signs of progress — and two of the most closely watched trials are running in Philadelphia, at Jefferson Health and the University of Pennsylvania.

At Jefferson, researchers are testing a virus-delivered vaccine that trains the immune system to recognise GUCY2C, a protein commonly found on gastrointestinal tumour cells. It is given after tumours have been surgically removed. In a trial of 46 patients it was found safe, according to an abstract presented at the American Society of Clinical Oncology meeting in late May, and it produced strong immune responses at the highest dose. Among 21 colorectal cancer patients in the analysis, higher doses were associated with lower recurrence two years on.

The results come with clear limits: they are preliminary and not yet peer-reviewed, and the trial was neither randomised nor controlled, so it cannot prove the vaccine works. A follow-up Phase 2b trial is being prepared, funded by a $2 million grant from the Pennsylvania Department of Health. The work is led by oncologist Babar Bashir and Jefferson scientist Adam Snook, who developed the technology.

The University of Pennsylvania is taking a different approach: trying to prevent cancer before it starts. A trial led by Susan Domchek is testing a vaccine in people with BRCA mutations, which sharply raise breast and ovarian cancer risk. It targets three features common to many cancers — a telomerase subunit found in larger amounts than usual in 95% of human cancers, plus the proteins WT1 and PSMA. In an early Phase 1b trial of 44 participants it was found safe, with findings reported at ASCO in 2025. The next step is to check whether it produced an adequate immune response, before any trial can test whether it prevents cancer.

The Philadelphia work sits inside a wider shift. In August, a personalised mRNA vaccine from Merck and Moderna became the first of its kind to succeed in a late-stage trial: in 1,137 melanoma patients whose tumours had been fully removed, adding the custom-made vaccine to the immunotherapy drug pembrolizumab cut the risk of recurrence or death by 42% compared with pembrolizumab alone. This month, SELLAS Life Sciences reported positive results from an advanced leukaemia-vaccine trial involving 126 patients.

The history explains the caution: scientists have chased cancer vaccines for decades, and only one — sipuleucel-T for advanced prostate cancer, approved in 2010 — has ever won full approval from the US Food and Drug Administration. Experts say vaccines are most likely to work when cancer is caught early or after tumours have been removed, and it will take years to prove the new generation safe and effective. For now, the promise is real — but so is the wait.
