More than 1,300 Delta Air Lines flights were delayed across the United States on Sunday after the airline's automated system for calculating aircraft weight and balance failed, snarling travel at its Atlanta hub just days before the Thanksgiving holiday rush.

The system, known as ALIS, went down on Sunday morning. It performs the load calculations crews must complete before every departure, working out how passengers and cargo are distributed so an aircraft stays within safe limits and keeps a stable centre of gravity. Without it, Delta staff fell back on manual calculations, and departures slowed to the pace of work done by hand.

By early afternoon, more than 1,300 of Delta's mainline flights had been delayed, at least 40 percent of the airline's planned operation for the day, according to figures from the tracking service FlightAware. The heaviest disruption was at Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport, the carrier's home base and the world's busiest airport, where FlightAware counted 1,044 delayed flights by the afternoon. Around 45 percent of Delta flights out of Atlanta were running late as of 1:30 p.m. ET.

The Federal Aviation Administration briefly halted Delta departures bound for Atlanta on Sunday morning before easing the restriction to a ground delay programme. The agency said the problem was confined to Delta's own technology and was not connected to federal air traffic systems.

"Delta teams are using manual workarounds this morning to safely get flights departed while work continues to restore automation," the airline said in a statement. Delta later reported that its automated systems were working again at about 3:30 p.m. ET, and apologised to customers for the delays.

Not all of the disruption at Atlanta came from the outage. Delta said about 70 percent of the airport's delays were caused by the system failure, with the rest linked to bad weather and air traffic adjustments in New York City and the Northeast.

The timing adds to the pressure on travellers. Thanksgiving week is one of the busiest periods in American aviation, and a day of manual operations leaves aircraft and crews out of position, meaning knock-on delays can linger into Monday even after the system itself is fixed. Delta advised passengers to check their flight status in the airline's app or on its website before heading to the airport.

Sunday's failure is the latest in a run of technology problems to hit American aviation. On November 13, a failure in the FAA's own communication systems grounded flights nationwide for about four hours, and a separate issue affected only United Airlines last Monday. The FAA stressed that Sunday's Delta disruption was unrelated to its systems.

Delta has not said what caused its weight-and-balance system to fail, and it gave no detailed account of the fault while restoration work was under way.
