Four possible lava tunnels lie hidden beneath the surface of the Moon, each potentially more than a kilometre wide and buried under about 100 metres of rock, according to a new analysis of the Moon's gravitational field.

The four candidates were identified by geoscientists Juan Losarcos and Andrew Dombard of the University of Illinois Chicago, working with a higher-resolution model of the Moon's gravity field that NASA released in May 2025. Their findings are published in the journal Icarus.

The method turns on a simple idea: a hollow tunnel contains less mass than the same volume of solid rock, so the Moon's gravitational pull is very slightly weaker above it. The pair mapped tiny dips in the gravity field, accounting for the shape of the surface, and compared them with simulations of the signatures that hollow tunnels of different sizes would produce. They then checked the surface geology for features that could support an underground cavity.

Four spots stood out: one near Vallis Schröteri, the Moon's largest sinuous rille; one at Rima Mairan; and two near the Wollaston crater. In each case, the gravity signal was consistent with a tunnel less than two kilometres wide, buried beneath around 100 metres of rock.

Lava tubes form when the surface of a flowing river of lava cools and hardens into a roof, while molten rock keeps moving underneath. When the eruption ends and the remaining lava drains away, a long hollow tunnel is left behind. The same structures exist on Earth, but on the Moon — with its weaker gravity, higher eruption rates and freer-flowing lava — they could be far larger.

Earlier work pointed to bigger prizes. Using data from NASA's twin GRAIL spacecraft, which mapped the Moon's gravity field in 2011 and 2012, studies in 2017 and 2024 identified possible tunnels up to nine kilometres wide. Later studies found that tunnels that wide would probably be unable to support the weight of their own roofs — which is what makes the new, narrower candidates more plausible.

The four may also have formed differently from the classic picture. Rather than a roof forming over a vast open river of lava, they may have started as much smaller underground channels, gradually enlarged as molten rock flowing through them melted and wore away the surrounding material.

For now, all four are candidates rather than confirmed detections. Other variations in the density of lunar rock could produce similar gravitational signals, and gravity data alone cannot measure the size of a cavity directly. Future radar observations and more detailed geological mapping could settle the question — though fully intact tubes, with no collapsed sections in their roofs, would be especially hard to spot.

If any of the tunnels are real, they matter twice over. They would preserve evidence of the Moon's volcanic and thermal history — and intact ones could one day shelter explorers from the radiation and extreme temperatures on the lunar surface.
