A federal judge has ordered Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins to stop sending religious messages to employees from her official account, finding that her holiday emails likely violated the First Amendment's ban on government establishment of religion. U.S. District Judge Trina Thompson of the Northern District of California issued the preliminary injunction on Thursday, November 19, writing that "the Secretary must recognize certain boundaries when communicating religious messages" — and expressing no view on what Rollins may do in her personal capacity.

The ruling focused on two messages sent to all department employees: a Christmas email on December 23, 2025, that included a video calling Jesus Christ "our Savior," and an Easter email on April 5, 2026, carrying an illustration of a stone rolled away from Jesus' tomb with the words "Christ is Risen." Thompson found that both went too far.

At the heart of the case was not the secretary's faith, but her authority. Her messages reached every employee through an official account that staff are expected to read, and used language such as "we," "us" and "our" that assumed every recipient shared her Christian beliefs. No comparable messages marked other religions' holidays. Even without singling out anyone who disagreed, the judge found, the "immense social pressure" of such messages from a superior could amount to coercion.

The lawsuit was filed in May 2026 by the National Federation of Federal Employees, a union representing more than 100,000 federal workers, along with six individual USDA employees. They said the messages favored employees who shared the secretary's beliefs and pressured others to hide their own — a Buddhist employee who no longer felt safe discussing a holiday she celebrates, an atheist who felt silenced, and a Pagan employee who felt pressure to appear Christian to advance. They are represented by Americans United for Separation of Church and State, Democracy Forward and Bryan Schwartz Law.

The department had argued the emails were not coercive because Rollins was sharing her personal views on holidays, not compelling conformity. Thompson rejected that framing for messages sent in an official capacity, and ordered Rollins to follow the department's own guidance on religious expression at work: stop if a colleague asks, do not create a hostile work environment, and do not use official authority to pressure subordinates.

The injunction is preliminary, not a final ruling — it holds while the case continues, and the government can ask an appeals court to lift it. The administration is considering an appeal, according to reports. Thompson also dismissed the plaintiffs' Administrative Procedure Act claims, finding the emails did not amount to an official agency rule.

The order arrives just weeks before Christmas, when the next round of seasonal messages would ordinarily go out. It does not restrict Rollins's private religious expression. As the judge put it, the Founders understood that independence requires freedom of conscience — and the Establishment Clause bars the government from using its authority to sway how a person practices their faith.
