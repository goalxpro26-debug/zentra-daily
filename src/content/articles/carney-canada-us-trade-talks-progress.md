Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney said on Saturday that Canada and the United States are making progress toward a trade deal, after negotiations broke down in August. He made the remarks to reporters in Manila, where he is attending a summit of Southeast Asian leaders.

"I had quite a long, substantive conversation with President Trump just before leaving for this trip," Carney said. "We are making progress. There is the possibility of a deal, a bilateral deal. But we're not yet there."

U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer and White House chief of staff Stephen Miller also took part in the call, Carney said.

## The auto industry is the sticking point

Carney drew a clear line around Canada's car industry, which he called one of the most difficult issues still on the table. He said his government would not accept an agreement that undermines the sector, which has been hit hard by U.S. tariffs.

Canada walked away from trade talks in August after what Ottawa described as last-minute American demands it considered unacceptable. Washington then imposed 50 per cent tariffs on about $20 billion worth of Canadian goods, and Canada answered with retaliatory tariffs in September. The two sides have since returned to the table.

Canada and the United States share one of the largest trading relationships in the world, and a deal could lower tariffs on key sectors including steel and aluminum. President Donald Trump said before the U.S. midterm elections that the two countries were "very close" to an agreement, while Carney said Washington had dropped demands touching on Canadian culture, French-language protections and Canada's freedom to strike trade deals with other countries.

## Carney looks to Asia as talks continue

Carney used the Manila visit to announce progress on a second front. Canada has substantially concluded free-trade negotiations with the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, a bloc of nearly 700 million people, and has completed separate negotiations with the Philippines. Both agreements are expected to be signed next year.

Ottawa estimates the ASEAN agreement could add nearly 2 billion Canadian dollars (about US$1.4 billion) to the Canadian economy and create almost 14,000 jobs, mostly in agriculture and manufacturing. The Philippines deal covers more than 90 per cent of tariff lines, and the Canadian government said it could help triple Canadian exports to the Philippines by 2035.

The announcements fit Carney's stated goal of doubling Canadian exports to countries other than the United States by 2035, reducing Ottawa's dependence on its southern neighbour during the trade war.

## What is still unknown

No timetable for a Canada–U.S. deal has been given, and the auto issue remains unresolved. It is also unclear how far U.S. tariffs would fall under any agreement: Carney has acknowledged that Canada faces a choice between accepting a deal that cuts some tariffs but leaves others in place, or holding out for freer trade while the U.S. Congress prepares to challenge the tariffs.
