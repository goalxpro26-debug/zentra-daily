The James Webb Space Telescope has picked up a whisper of light that physicists have been chasing for four decades — the faint rotational glow of the trihydrogen ion, a molecule so central to interstellar chemistry that it had never been seen shining, anywhere in the Universe or in a laboratory.

The detection was made with Webb's Mid-Infrared Instrument, trained on a turbulent patch of sky known as Herbig-Haro 168, or HH 168, in the star-forming region Cepheus A. There, a jet from a young star slams into the surrounding gas, heating it in a violent shock. Buried in the infrared light from that hot gas, a team led by David Neufeld of Johns Hopkins University found a series of extraordinarily faint spectral lines — 26 of them, each matching the wavelengths predicted for the trihydrogen cation, H₃⁺. The strongest line registered at 72 sigma, leaving virtually no doubt about the detection.

## A prediction from 1986

The signal was predicted in 1986, when astrophysicists Fu-Shih Pan and Takeshi Oka of the University of Chicago calculated that the trihydrogen ion should emit a very faint amount of light as it rotates. The transitions involved are so weak that physicists call them "forbidden" — not impossible, but so improbable that the light they produce is vanishingly faint.

For forty years, that prediction stayed unconfirmed. The signal was too faint to catch, even in laboratory experiments designed to look for it. Webb's sensitivity finally changed that, and the wavelengths the team measured matched Pan and Oka's forty-year-old calculations.

It was not a one-off. When the researchers examined archival Webb observations of another Herbig-Haro object, HH 211, they found the same telltale emission there too — a second confirmation that the signal is real and not a quirk of one patch of sky.

## Why a faint glow matters

H₃⁺ is one of the most important ions in astrochemistry. It forms readily in cold molecular clouds and drives much of the chemistry that builds more complex molecules in space. Its newly detected rotational lines give astronomers a fresh way to measure ionisation in shock-heated gas — the kind of gas that surrounds newborn stars — and to test how those violent regions evolve.

There are limits to what the result can yet say. The work is available as a preprint on arXiv and has been accepted for publication in The Astrophysical Journal Letters, but the interpretation still rests on incomplete knowledge of how energetic collisions excite H₃⁺. Models of the shock in HH 168 do not yet fully match the observed brightness, so the physics behind the glow remains partly open.

What is settled is the detection itself: after forty years, a signal once thought too faint to ever see has finally been caught — twice, in two different stellar nurseries.
