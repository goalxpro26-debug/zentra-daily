Astronomers using NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory have solved a mystery that has puzzled them for 15 years: a giant, unstable star that keeps exploding like a supernova — without ever dying.

The star, known as AT 2016blu, sits in the spiral galaxy NGC 4559, 30 million light-years from Earth. It is a luminous blue variable, a class of hot, massive stars known for dramatic swings in brightness. Its surges are so bright they resemble a supernova, the violent death of a massive star — but the star survives each blast, which is why astronomers call it a "supernova impostor."

Between 2012 and 2026, telescopes recorded at least 27 bursts of visible light from the star, arriving roughly every 113 days. The regularity was the clue that something was driving the eruptions on a schedule — but for years, nobody could say what.

## A companion hiding in the glare

The answer, published Monday in The Astrophysical Journal, is a hidden companion orbiting the giant star: either a stellar-mass black hole or a neutron star, the dense core left when a massive star dies. The pair travels an oval orbit, swinging close together every 113 days. At closest approach, the companion pulls material off the giant star; the captured gas whips around near light speed, heating until it shines in X-rays, and that energy heats the surrounding gas until it glows in visible light.

"For 15 years, astronomers have been watching this star get really bright and then dim again on a regular basis," said Mojgan Aghakhanloo of the University of Virginia, who led the study. "Until we were able to get Chandra data at the right time, we couldn't figure out what was making this happen."

Catching the star at the right moment was the hard part. The team timed a Chandra observation for a predicted outburst in early 2026, confirming the flare with optical telescopes — the Katzman Automatic Imaging Telescope, the Las Cumbres Observatory network and more than 30 amateur astronomers. Chandra observed in March 2026 and found the star at least 100 times brighter in X-rays than in 2001 and 2002, before the outbursts began.

## A first on two counts

It is the first X-ray detection of this class of erupting star, and the first found with a black hole or neutron star companion. AT 2016blu is also bright enough in X-rays to qualify as a candidate ultra-luminous X-ray source, a rare and poorly understood class of bright X-ray objects.

The discovery may reach beyond one strange star. Astronomers suspect many stars that brighten before exploding as supernovae sit in similar systems — a massive star orbiting a compact object or another massive star. The hidden companion may be a common culprit behind some of the sky's most dramatic behaviour.

What remains unknown is which kind of companion it is: the observations confirm a compact object but cannot yet say whether it is a black hole or a neutron star. That will need further observations — and the next outburst is never far away.
