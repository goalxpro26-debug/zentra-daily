SpaceX caught its Starship spacecraft with the mechanical arms of its launch tower in Texas on Saturday, completing the first-ever recovery of the rocket's upper stage after a trip to orbit.

The spacecraft, known simply as the Ship, descended through the atmosphere after circling Earth 14 times over about 22 hours, then eased between the tower's two "chopstick" arms at the company's Starbase site. Crowds watching from a nearby beach cheered as it settled into its cradle high above the ground.

"We caught a Starship and now, everything changes," SpaceX spokesman Dan Huot said after the catch.

## A day in orbit

Flight 15 lifted off on Friday afternoon, November 13, at 4:47 p.m. Central time. The Super Heavy booster separated about two minutes into the flight and sent the Ship on toward orbit, where it deployed 26 of SpaceX's newer Starlink V3 internet satellites.

It was only the second time a Starship had reached full orbit. This time the spacecraft kept going, completing 14 laps of the planet at about 165 miles (265 kilometres) up, with live views of itself and Earth streaming down via Starlink. SpaceX said everything on board, including the vital heat shield, appeared to work well.

The return began on Saturday afternoon. The Ship fell back through the atmosphere, performed its sideways "belly flop" descent, then flipped upright, fired its engines for a landing burn and came to rest in the arms of the Mechazilla tower at about 3:20 p.m. Central time.

## Only half the plan

The catch was one half of an ambitious goal. SpaceX had hoped Flight 15 would achieve a first double catch: the booster returning to the tower minutes after launch, followed a day later by the Ship.

The booster part failed. After separation, the Super Heavy was initially cleared to return to the launch site, but it diverted instead toward the Gulf of Mexico, where its flight termination system triggered and it exploded before hitting the water. SpaceX said early signs pointed to the booster's boostback burn ending early because of a low fuel mass alarm, and that the root cause was still under investigation.

SpaceX had caught Super Heavy boosters three times before. But catching the Ship was far harder: it falls much faster and from much higher than a booster does, after surviving the heat of re-entry.

## Why it matters

Recovering both stages is the core of SpaceX's plan to make Starship a fully reusable rocket — a goal the company says would cut the cost of reaching space sharply and allow far more frequent launches. The same system is central to NASA's plans to return astronauts to the Moon and, eventually, to send people to Mars. NASA welcomed Saturday's news.

Starship is the largest and most powerful rocket ever flown, standing 407 feet (124 metres) tall. Saturday's flight was its 15th full-scale test since 2023. SpaceX expects to begin launching Starships from Florida's Kennedy Space Center within the next few months.
