A heart attack or stroke can feel sudden, but the warning signs may start appearing three decades before the event, according to a large international study published in JAMA Network Open on November 23.

Researchers led by the University of Edinburgh, with funding from the British Heart Foundation, pooled records from more than 113,000 adults in the United States, Norway and the United Kingdom, drawn from four long-running population studies. None had a history of heart attack or stroke at entry. Across 316,719 check-ups between 1985 and 2019, 16,917 of them went on to have a heart attack, a stroke or a fatal cardiovascular event.

Working backwards from each event, the team found that people who later fell ill had measurably different risk-factor patterns from those who stayed healthy — and the gap opened up to 30 years beforehand.

Blood pressure gave the clearest early signal. Among women, those who later had an event averaged a systolic reading — the top number — of 121 mmHg three decades before it, against 117 mmHg in women who remained event-free. By the time of the event, the gap had widened to 137 against 130. Similar long-term differences appeared in men and for other risk factors, including cholesterol, diabetes, body mass index, physical activity and kidney function. People whose events came earlier in life were more likely to have had diabetes and a higher body mass index in the decades before.

Blood tests added a second layer of clues. Differences in circulating biomarkers — including C-reactive protein, cardiac troponins and NT-proBNP, a protein fragment used to assess heart failure — began to separate one to three decades before an event, with NT-proBNP changes detectable about 10 years beforehand.

The pattern was not the same for everyone: younger women generally had lower blood pressure than younger men, but that reversed around the age of 65.

The researchers say the findings point to prevention starting much earlier in life, rather than waiting until risk becomes obvious in older age. "Modifiable risk factors begin to diverge 30 years before a cardiovascular event occurs," said Dr Dorien Kimenai, a British Heart Foundation research fellow at the University of Edinburgh's Usher Institute who worked on the study. Professor Bryan Williams, the foundation's chief scientific and medical officer, added that many heart attacks and strokes are preventable, but people often do not realise they are at risk until much later — and that simple checks of blood pressure and cholesterol could help people understand their future risk sooner.

## What is still unknown

The study describes patterns across large groups of people, not a test that can tell any one person whether or when they will have a heart attack or stroke. More work is needed before these long-term patterns can be used for individual prediction, and tracking risk-factor trajectories over time — rather than relying on a single reading — is the next step for doctors weighing earlier intervention.
