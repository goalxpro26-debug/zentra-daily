Washington has moved to close one of the last open routes by which Chinese firms gain access to advanced American chips, announcing new interim rules that put data-centre operators across South East Asia and Brazil under US licensing controls.

Under the rules, firms in Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil will need a US licence before buying advanced chips from Nvidia or other American chipmakers. Licences will be decided case by case, the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security said, warning the five countries present a higher-than-normal risk of diversion to China.

The controls go beyond physical exports to target access to computing power itself: Chinese companies have increasingly rented capacity in overseas data centres to train AI models, after earlier US export bans cut off direct supplies. The Bureau said the measures aim to stop sensitive US technology being circumvented through remote computing arrangements in third countries.

The announcement lands as the Megaspeed affair continues to reverberate. The Singaporean data-centre firm, the biggest buyer of Nvidia chips in South East Asia, is accused of smuggling chips to Chinese companies. Megaspeed denies the allegations, calls its business legitimate and lawful, and says it has hired external lawyers to investigate. Its former director Alice Huang, suspected by US officials of passing information to China, left the firm last year. The scandal intensified after Nvidia chief Jensen Huang admitted an affair with her and resigned.

The stakes are large. The US-based Institute for Progress projects that at least 30 percent of China's advanced AI computing power will come from rented overseas capacity or smuggled chips in 2027, and estimates the best American AI models lead their Chinese rivals by only about six and a half months.

Reactions split along familiar lines. China's foreign ministry called the rules "economic coercion" and said Beijing would take the necessary steps to protect its interests. Singapore said it was talking to US authorities to understand the rules and keep trusted access for local firms. Brazil said it was closely monitoring developments, while Malaysia has said it will not allow itself to be used to get around export controls. The BBC said it had contacted Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand for comment.

Analysts cautioned that the region's data-centre boom is unlikely to stop. Trade expert Naoise McDonagh of Edith Cowan University said Malaysia and Thailand, where the build-out has been heavily driven by Chinese buyers, could be hit hardest. Reema Bhattacharya of Verisk Maplecroft said investment backed by major tech firms should stay relatively resilient — but the rules have taken away one of the more practical routes Chinese AI developers had to frontier computing power.

The rules come just as President Donald Trump heads to China to meet President Xi Jinping in Shenzhen this week. Tighter controls may also push Chinese firms further toward domestic semiconductors, a field Beijing has made a national priority. No start date for the new licensing regime has been stated.
