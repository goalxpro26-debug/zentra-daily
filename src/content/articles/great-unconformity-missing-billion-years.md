More than a billion years of Earth's history is missing from the rock record, and scientists say they may finally have an explanation. A study published November 4 in the journal *Nature* traces the gap to slow movements deep inside the planet, which lifted ancient continents into the path of grinding ice sheets and then let them sink again.

The gap is known as the Great Unconformity. In a normal sequence, rock layers stack up with the oldest at the bottom and the youngest at the top. But in places including the Grand Canyon, northern Europe and northern China, sedimentary rock about 540 million years old sits directly on crystalline basement rock that is more than a billion years old — in places up to two billion. Everything that should have been laid down in between is simply absent. At the Grand Canyon, researchers estimate the missing record spans roughly 1.2 billion years, as much as a quarter of Earth's entire history.

Geologists first described the feature in the late 1800s, at the Grand Canyon. For about 150 years the cause stayed an open question. One leading idea blamed the Snowball Earth period, roughly 720 to 635 million years ago, when the planet may have been largely frozen and moving ice could have scraped the rock away. But if ice covered the globe, it should have erased the record everywhere — and the gap is not everywhere.

The new work, by Santosh Dhungana and Nicolas Flament of the University of Wollongong in Australia, tested a different suspect: the mantle, the 2,900-kilometre-thick layer of hot, slowly flowing rock between the crust and the core. Using the Gadi supercomputer, they ran six simulations of mantle convection over the past billion years. In every model, the currents lifted three ancient continental cores — Laurentia, now much of North America; Baltica, in northern Europe; and North China — from around a billion years ago until about 540 million years ago.

That uplift overlapped with the Snowball Earth freeze, and raised land erodes far faster under moving ice than flat land does under rivers. The researchers argue the ice ground away the exposed rock. Then, over the past 540 million years, the same regions slowly sank and were buried under fresh sediment, sealing the sharp boundary seen today.

The gap ends just before the Cambrian explosion, around 530 to 540 million years ago, when animal life diversified rapidly. The erosion may have released elements such as potassium, iron, manganese and carbon into the oceans, which could have helped marine life develop shells and added oxygen to the atmosphere — though that link remains a hypothesis.

The finding does not close the book: the evidence is a computer model, and the primary proof is rock that is no longer there. But it is the first quantitative test of the mantle-driven idea, and it ties two long-standing suspects — deep-Earth movement and ice — into a single sequence.
