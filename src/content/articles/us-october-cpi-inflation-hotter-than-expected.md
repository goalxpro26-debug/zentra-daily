US consumer prices rose 0.4% in October, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on Tuesday — a slower pace than September's 0.6% increase, but hotter than the 0.3% rise economists had expected, and enough to keep the Federal Reserve on course to raise interest rates at its December meeting.

The report's underlying measures were stubborn too. Core inflation, which strips out volatile food and energy prices, rose 0.3% for a third consecutive month, again above the 0.2% forecast. Housing did much of the work: shelter costs rose 0.3% and accounted for more than 30% of the month's overall increase, according to the bureau. Food prices rose 0.5%, while energy prices rose 0.7% — a sharp easing from September's 5.2% jump, but still rising.

One figure was missing entirely. The bureau did not publish an annual inflation rate for October, because last year's federal government shutdown prevented it from collecting the consumer price data for October 2025 that a year-on-year comparison requires. The last published annual reading, for September, was 3.7%.

## A rate hike now looks near-certain

The October data all but settles the question hanging over the Fed's December 9 meeting. Traders now put the odds of a rate hike at more than 98%, according to CME FedWatch, with a quarter-point move to a 4% to 4.25% range the most likely outcome. Higher rates would make borrowing more expensive for households already stretching to cover groceries, rent and other everyday costs — and real average hourly earnings were unchanged from September to October, the bureau reported separately, meaning pay did not keep pace with prices.

The report lands days after midterm elections in which frustration over the cost of living was a major issue for voters. For the central bank, the dilemma is familiar: inflation is cooling from September's pace, but it is not cooling fast enough, and the missing annual figure leaves policymakers without their usual headline yardstick.

## What is still unknown

It is not yet clear how large December's move will be, or how the Fed will frame its next steps given the gap in the annual data. The bureau is scheduled to publish the November inflation report on December 10 — one day after the Fed's decision — so the central bank will act on October's figures and its own projections rather than fresh data.
