Talks have opened at the United Nations in Geneva on whether to set rules for lethal autonomous weapons — machines that can identify, select and strike a target without a human giving the order. Critics call them "killer robots", and the question before delegates this week is whether to start formal negotiations on a binding agreement to control them.

The discussion is taking place at the seventh review conference of the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons, a treaty that restricts weapons considered excessively injurious or indiscriminate. The conference runs from November 16 to 20 and brings together delegates from more than 120 member states, with 128 countries party to the convention.

UN Secretary-General António Guterres opened the meeting by urging delegates to move towards negotiating a legally binding instrument with "clear prohibitions and restrictions". He has set a deadline of this year for talks to begin, arguing that the nature of warfare is changing and that even war has limits.

The push has momentum. Last month, seven countries — Austria, Belgium, Brazil, Ireland, Mexico, New Zealand and Panama — submitted a formal proposal to launch negotiations on a new legally binding agreement. The advocacy group Stop Killer Robots says 130 countries now support new international rules, and 163 states backed a UN General Assembly resolution last month calling for negotiations to begin.

But agreement is far from certain. Some of the world's biggest military powers are hesitant or opposed. The United States and Russia are expected to resist a move to binding talks, and Russia has described it as premature to argue that restrictive measures are needed. Around a dozen countries, including the United States, Israel, Russia and India, do not want a legally binding instrument.

The debate is framed by its supporters as a question of human control. Pope Leo XIV, the UN chief and campaign groups have cast it as an existential issue: whether machines should be allowed to kill, and whether humans must keep control of — and accountability for — the use of lethal force. The UN human rights chief, Volker Türk, called on Monday for nations to "urgently" agree on binding rules.

In a joint statement in August, Guterres and Mirjana Spoljaric, president of the International Committee of the Red Cross, warned that the world was "dangerously close to crossing a moral red line: the autonomous targeting of humans by machines."

The urgency is not abstract. Reports describe autonomous weapons systems already being tested and used on the battlefield, including in Ukraine, with some systems said to be capable of selecting and killing people without human approval. Human Rights Watch, Amnesty International and Stop Killer Robots warned last week that failing to act at this conference would leave civilians dangerously exposed to rapidly advancing military technology.

Whether this week's conference produces a mandate for negotiations — or another round of inconclusive discussion — will become clear by Friday.
