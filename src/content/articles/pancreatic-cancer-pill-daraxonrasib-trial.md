A daily pill taken alongside chemotherapy has produced striking early results against one of the deadliest cancers, with tumours shrinking or disappearing on scans in six out of ten patients in a small trial.

The drug, daraxonrasib, was tested with two standard chemotherapy drugs — gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel — in 61 patients whose pancreatic cancer had spread to other organs and who had not yet received treatment for the advanced disease. The results were presented at a cancer therapeutics symposium in Barcelona by the trial's lead researcher, Dr Wungki Park of Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center in New York.

Across the group, tumours shrank significantly or became undetectable in 60.7 percent of patients. In 8.2 percent — about one in twelve — tumours vanished entirely from scans, a result known as a complete response. Overall, the treatment stopped tumours growing in 90.2 percent of patients.

The benefits also appeared to last. Patients went a median of 12.8 months before their cancer started growing again — roughly double the five to six months typically seen with chemotherapy alone. After a year, 76.6 percent of patients were still alive, compared with the roughly one in four pancreatic cancer patients who usually survive beyond twelve months.

Daraxonrasib works by switching off RAS, a family of proteins that acts like a stuck accelerator in cancer cells. More than nine in ten pancreatic cancers carry a RAS mutation, most commonly in the KRAS gene, which drives cells to divide relentlessly. The drug blocks the active form of the protein, cutting the signal that tells tumours to grow.

The trial's leaders are urging caution despite the excitement. The study was small and early-stage, with no comparison group receiving chemotherapy alone, so it cannot yet prove the pill improves survival. Side effects were in line with the known effects of the drugs involved — mainly rash, diarrhoea, fatigue and low blood counts — and five percent of patients stopped the drug because of side effects.

A larger, randomised phase III trial is now under way to test daraxonrasib, alone and with chemotherapy, directly against standard treatment. Cancer Research UK has described the findings as the biggest leap forward for pancreatic cancer patients in decades, while stressing that more trials are needed before the treatment can be considered for routine use.

For now, daraxonrasib remains experimental, but the results suggest that switching off RAS — long considered an untargetable driver of cancer — may finally be within reach.
