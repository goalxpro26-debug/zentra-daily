The American Cancer Society has changed its advice on prostate cancer screening for the first time in 16 years. The organisation now says most men should get a blood test for the disease, rather than weighing the option with a doctor.

Under the new guidance, published Thursday, men at average risk should have a prostate-specific antigen, or PSA, blood test every two years between the ages of 50 and 69. Doctors should still explain the benefits, limits and possible harms before testing begins. The previous guidance, issued in 2010, left the decision entirely to the individual after a discussion with a clinician.

## Earlier and more frequent testing for higher-risk men

The recommendations are stronger for men at higher risk. Black men, and men with a strong family history of the disease, should now be tested every year from the age of 45 through 69. Black men face a 67 percent higher incidence of prostate cancer than white men and nearly double the death rate, according to the society.

For men aged 70 to 74, the decision becomes individual, made with a clinician, because the balance of benefit and harm is less clear at that age. Routine screening is not recommended from 75 on, and should stop after 80 or when life expectancy is under 10 years — though very healthy men aged 75 to 79 with a rising PSA may choose to continue after talking with a doctor.

## A clearer path between a high reading and a biopsy

The update also tightens what happens after a test comes back high. A PSA level of 3.0 nanograms per millilitre or higher counts as elevated for men aged 45 to 69; for men aged 70 to 74, the threshold is 6.5. That is lower than the 4.0 figure many American laboratories still print as normal.

Rather than moving straight to a biopsy, the guidance calls for a second blood test to confirm the result, then an MRI scan or a validated biomarker test. The aim is to cut unnecessary biopsies and to catch slow-growing cancers that can safely be watched rather than treated at once.

## Why the society changed its mind

The shift reflects new evidence and better tools. A long-term European study published in 2025 found prostate cancer deaths were 13 percent lower among screened men. MRI scans and biomarker tests now also let doctors sort out which raised readings need a biopsy — far harder in 2010, when the test's lack of specificity led to false alarms, unnecessary procedures and treatment of tumours that would never have caused harm.

The society estimates about 333,830 American men will be diagnosed in 2026, and about 36,320 will die from it — the second most common cause of cancer death in men.

One influential body has not yet moved. The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force, whose recommendations determine which preventive services insurers must cover, still advises individual decision-making. It has announced an update is coming.
