The first picture ever taken of a black hole's shadow has survived its toughest test yet. A team of Australian researchers has reanalysed the original telescope data with a completely independent method — and produced almost exactly the same glowing ring.

When the Event Horizon Telescope collaboration released the image in 2019, it became one of the most recognisable pictures in modern science: a bright orange ring around a dark centre, the shadow of the supermassive black hole at the heart of the galaxy Messier 87, known as M87\*. But the image was a reconstruction, stitched together from radio signals collected by telescopes across the planet, and some researchers argued the ring shape might be an artefact of the way the data had been processed. One alternative analysis even produced a shapeless blob instead of a ring.

The new study, published in *Nature* on 11 November, set out to answer that doubt. The team — Samuel Lai, Nithyanandan Thyagarajan, O. I. Wong and F. Diakogiannis, from CSIRO, Australia's national science agency — built a fresh imaging pipeline that works differently from the original in almost every respect.

## A different way of combining the data

The original analysis combined measurements from pairs of telescopes. The new approach, called GenDIReCT, groups the telescopes in threes. Combining three signals at a time preserves information about the object's structure while cancelling out errors caused by the atmosphere and by the instruments themselves — errors that have troubled radio astronomers for decades.

The team also trained its machine-learning algorithm only on images unrelated to astronomy, so the software had no preconceived idea of what a black hole should look like. "Our model had no idea what a black hole looked like when we applied it to the M87 data," lead author Samuel Lai said.

Applied to the original 2017 observations — and to later data from 2018 and 2021 — the method recovered a compact ring in every epoch, in close agreement with the 2019 image. It is, the researchers say, the first truly independent validation of the landmark picture.

## Why it matters

The stakes go beyond one famous image. The ring's size and shape matched predictions from Albert Einstein's general theory of relativity, which first predicted black holes more than a century ago. A reanalysis that produced a different shape would have raised questions not only about the image but about the physics it appeared to confirm.

M87\* sits about 55 million light-years from Earth and has a mass roughly 6.5 billion times that of the Sun. What the telescopes actually capture is not the black hole itself — no light escapes it — but the glow of hot matter swirling around it, bent into a ring by the black hole's gravity.

## What is still unknown

The new result confirms the ring's shape, but it does not end the work. The Event Horizon Telescope continues to observe M87\* and the black hole at the centre of our own galaxy, Sagittarius A\*, with the aim of sharper images and, eventually, moving pictures of matter orbiting the edge of a black hole.
