Lebanon's prime minister says France is preparing a proposal for the UN Security Council that would allow peacekeepers in southern Lebanon to keep doing some of their work after their mandate expires at the end of the year.

Prime Minister Nawaf Salam told The Associated Press on Friday that the plan would let the force known as UNIFIL continue operating after December 31 while the Security Council debates what should replace it. France would introduce a resolution allowing the peacekeepers to keep carrying out functions such as clearing mines, escorting humanitarian convoys and monitoring violations during a drawdown period after the mandate ends.

"This would not resolve the problem of so-called post-UNIFIL, but it will give us some extra time to agree on a new architecture in southern Lebanon," Salam said in an exclusive interview with the AP at the government palace in Beirut.

US officials have indicated that Washington is "open to considering" such an arrangement, Salam said. France has not publicly commented on the proposal. A Western diplomat familiar with the discussions said one option under consideration was to slow the withdrawal and retain certain UNIFIL missions to avoid a power vacuum.

The proposal addresses a gap with no agreed answer. The Security Council voted last year to terminate UNIFIL's mandate at the end of 2026, bowing to demands from the United States and Israel, which argued that the force had failed to stop Hezbollah's military buildup in southern Lebanon. The roughly 7,500 peacekeepers now stationed there are expected to withdraw gradually over about six months after the mandate expires, with the force given a year to end its operations fully.

Lebanese officials have called for an extension of the mandate or, failing that, for a new UN-mandated force to be formed. Salam stressed that any force deployed in the south would have to be endorsed by the Security Council. US officials have expressed willingness for some sort of international force to remain, but not in its current form, suggesting it could be a civilian force or a private defence contractor.

The uncertainty is compounded by the situation on the ground. Much of southern Lebanon is occupied by Israeli forces after a war broke out in March between Israel and Hezbollah, and implementation of a June agreement under which Israeli forces would gradually withdraw and Hezbollah would be disarmed has stalled. Salam said formal negotiations with Israel were put on hold because of elections there and uncertainty over the next Israeli government, but denied the talks had collapsed.

Salam also said his government keeps open lines of communication with Hezbollah and Iran, but will not "allow the future of the country to be contingent on Iran's wishes." With the wider region in flux, he said, it is hard to make definitive plans: "Lebanon is not an isolated island in the Mediterranean. We are very much influenced by developments in the region."
