Hackers have broken into Cloud One, the US Air Force's flagship cloud computing platform, in an intrusion discovered during upgrade work within the past month, according to Reuters, which cited three people familiar with the matter.

The breach could put at risk a vast range of sensitive tactical, personnel and logistics data, the report said. But investigators have not yet established who the hackers were, how far inside the system they got, or whether any data was actually stolen.

Cloud One is one of the US military's biggest cloud platforms. Launched in 2017, it was built to be a one-stop shop for the Air Force's computing needs, drawing on the resources of four operators — Microsoft, Amazon, Oracle and Alphabet — to run scores of applications. Those include systems that manage personnel records and pay, logistics and aircraft maintenance, as well as two with direct operational roles: Cloud-Based Command and Control, which pulls together air defence radar and other data feeds from across North America, and the Theater Integrated Combat Munitions System, which tracks bombs and missiles at more than 200 Air Force locations worldwide.

According to one source, the intruders exploited a software vulnerability to reach the platform's control plane — the layer that manages the cloud environment itself. The source compared that access to holding a universal remote: in theory, it could have let the hackers check the operational status of military aircraft or follow the movement of spare parts. It is not clear whether they did any of that, and Reuters could not identify the specific vulnerability that was used.

The Air Force said it "does not comment on the security posture, vulnerabilities or operational status of specific networks and systems." SAIC was the platform's prime contractor until 2025, when Leidos took over; SAIC did not respond to a request for comment, and Leidos referred questions to the Air Force.

The discovery has already disrupted work across the platform. Efforts to move more back-office applications onto Cloud One — a longstanding military priority — have been paused, and updates to applications already running there have been blocked, leaving Air Force staff unable to apply fixes. A fifth source said some users had their administrator rights revoked. All five sources spoke on condition of anonymity.

Investigators have called in US Cyber Command, the military's cyber force, to assess the damage. Cyber Command did not respond to requests for comment.

The breach is the latest in a string of cyber incidents to hit the US government, including two at the FBI, a hack of Pentagon data, a leak of Department of Homeland Security personnel information, breaches affecting at least two spy agencies, and a hack at the Treasury Department.

It also stands in sharp contrast to the platform's own public record. In a 2023 commentary, Cloud One's leadership boasted of a "100% success rate" in beating back what it called a "staggering" number of attempts to penetrate the system.

Who broke in, what they saw, and what they took remain unknown.
