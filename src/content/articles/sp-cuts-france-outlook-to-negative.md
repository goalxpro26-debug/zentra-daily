S&P Global Ratings cut the outlook on France's sovereign credit rating to "negative" from "stable" on Friday, warning that a downgrade could follow if the country cannot get its budget deficit under control. The agency affirmed France's rating at A+/A-1.

The ratings agency said growing budget pressures, weak economic growth and higher interest costs were making it harder for Paris to reduce the deficit, and that it now expected larger deficits this year and next than it had previously forecast. A negative outlook means S&P sees a greater chance of a downgrade at its next scheduled review, in a few months' time, if the public finances do not improve.

The decision came on the same day France's statistics office, INSEE, confirmed the country had entered recession in the second quarter of 2026 and reported that the deficit had climbed to 5.6% of GDP in the third quarter. Under Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu, who took office in September 2025, the government had planned to cut the deficit from 5.1% to 4.7% of GDP in 2026; after parliamentary debate the target was revised to 5%.

S&P's move means all three major ratings agencies now hold a negative outlook on French sovereign debt — Moody's and Fitch got there first. S&P cut France's rating itself to A+ from AA- in 2025, so a further downgrade would push it another notch lower. The agency said a downgrade could come if high deficits do not fall significantly over the next two years, or if economic growth falls short of its forecasts over an extended period.

French government borrowing costs have risen sharply in recent months. The yield on 10-year bonds topped 5.1% in October, its highest level in 24 years, before easing to about 4.7%. A lower credit rating would typically mean France pays more to borrow when it issues new debt.

The 2027 presidential election looms over the debate. S&P cited political polarisation and a lack of consensus on how to fix the structural deficit, warning that it could hinder both deficit reduction and growth-promoting reforms even beyond the election.

## What is still unknown

It is not yet clear whether the government can pass a 2027 budget that convinces the ratings agencies the deficit is on a downward path. Markets will also be watching whether Moody's or Fitch move first, and how far French borrowing costs rise if any of the three agencies follows through with a downgrade.
