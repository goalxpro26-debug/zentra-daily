People who regularly work night shifts show small but measurable differences in the structure of their hearts and in blood markers linked to cardiovascular health, according to a study of more than 42,000 people published on November 19 in the *European Heart Journal — Cardiovascular Imaging*.

The research is the first large-scale study to combine detailed cardiac MRI scans with extensive blood-marker analysis to look at how night-shift work may relate to the heart. Around one in five workers worldwide performs regular night-shift work, and earlier research has already linked it to a higher risk of cardiovascular disease and death. Working against the body's circadian rhythm can disturb sleep, metabolism, inflammation, blood pressure and blood-vessel function, though why the link exists is not fully understood.

## What the scans showed

The team analysed 42,671 men and women from the UK Biobank, with a mean age of 52. Of those, 1,322 reported frequent night-shift work; people who already had cardiovascular disease were excluded.

Compared with day workers, frequent night-shift workers tended to have slightly thicker heart walls relative to the size of the main pumping chamber, and somewhat smaller heart chambers. There was also a subtle difference in one measure of how the heart muscle contracts. The researchers found no significant difference in ejection fraction, the standard measure of the heart's overall pumping function.

Blood tests told a similar story: lower levels of DHA, an omega-3 fatty acid that supports heart and blood-vessel health, and of some measures linked to "good" cholesterol (HDL), alongside higher levels of markers relating to triglycerides and inflammation.

## Small differences, not a diagnosis

The researchers stress that the differences they observed were small average differences at a population level. They do not mean that all night-shift workers will develop heart disease or heart failure.

The study was observational, so it cannot establish whether night-shift work directly damages the heart, and it measured the heart at a single point in time — it cannot say whether the differences grow with longer exposure or ease if night work stops. Those questions need prospective studies that track shift patterns and heart imaging over time.

There are other limits. The UK Biobank population is predominantly white European and made up of healthy volunteers, which limits how far the results generalise. Participants whose shift-work information was unavailable were excluded, and they tended to be older and to have more cardiometabolic disease, so some selection bias is possible.

Study author Dr. Muwei Li, from Zhengzhou University, said people who regularly work at night should "pay particular attention to taking care of their cardiovascular health" — not smoking, staying active, keeping a healthy diet and weight, sleeping enough on a regular schedule, and keeping blood pressure, cholesterol and blood glucose controlled. Doctors, he added, may wish to note night-shift work in a patient's history, particularly alongside established cardiovascular risk factors.
