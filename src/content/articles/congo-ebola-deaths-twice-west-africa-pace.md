Six months after Ebola was declared in the Democratic Republic of Congo, the outbreak is not slowing down — it is accelerating. Infections and deaths are rising at double the rate recorded in the first six months of the West African epidemic of 2014–16, the largest Ebola outbreak ever seen, according to government data reported by Reuters.

More than 11,000 people have been infected and more than 5,500 have died since the outbreak was declared on May 15. It is already the second-largest Ebola outbreak on record anywhere in the world, and the deadliest of the 17 that Congo has faced. The virus is the rare Bundibugyo species, for which there are no approved vaccines or treatments. Congo's previous largest outbreak, in 2018–20, ended with 3,481 cases and 2,299 deaths — figures this outbreak has already left far behind.

## Why it is not under control

The response is struggling on several fronts at once. According to the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention, 80 per cent of new cases are being detected outside health surveillance, meaning most infections are never tracked by the authorities. The virus had already been circulating undetected before the outbreak was announced: 43 deaths had been recorded by the time of the declaration in mid-May.

Insecurity is a major obstacle. Health workers, vehicles and surveillance teams have come under attack in communities that distrust outsiders, and the medical charity Médecins Sans Frontières says vital resources and supplies still are not reaching hot spots outside the original epicentre in Ituri province. Health workers have gone on strike over conditions and unpaid salaries, and some have not been paid since August, officials say. Some $2.9 billion has been pledged for the response, but less than half of it has been disbursed, according to Africa CDC.

## Children hit hardest

Children have borne a disproportionate share of the toll. According to UNICEF, children accounted for 35 per cent of deaths recorded through November 4. Children under five made up 21 per cent of all deaths, and nearly 72 per cent of infected children in that age group died. Experts say most affected children have not received specialised care, and that pre-existing conditions such as malnutrition have made them more vulnerable.

Public health experts cited by the Associated Press said there was little chance of bringing the outbreak under control within the next year. On its current course, the death toll is on track to surpass the roughly 11,000 people who died in the West African epidemic.

For the wider world, the risk remains low: Ebola is not airborne and spreads only through direct contact with the bodily fluids of a sick person. But inside Congo, six months in, the outbreak is still growing faster than any before it — and the response is still catching up.
