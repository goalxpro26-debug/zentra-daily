After eight years crossing the inner Solar System, the European-Japanese BepiColombo mission has arrived at Mercury. The European Space Agency confirmed on Saturday 21 November that the spacecraft had been captured by the planet's gravity, making it only the second mission ever to enter orbit around the Sun's closest planet.

BepiColombo is a joint mission of the European Space Agency (ESA) and the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA). It carries two orbiters travelling together as one stacked spacecraft: ESA's Mercury Planetary Orbiter (MPO) and JAXA's Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter, known as Mio. The only previous mission to orbit Mercury was NASA's MESSENGER, which circled the planet from 2011 to 2015.

The spacecraft was launched on 20 October 2018 aboard an Ariane 5 rocket from French Guiana. Its route to Mercury was deliberately indirect, using nine planetary flybys to shed speed before the final approach. An electric-propulsion problem in 2024 pushed the planned arrival back by a year, and the mission shed its transfer module in September to begin the final leg.

For the orbit insertion itself, four of the MPO's thrusters fired to slow the spacecraft as it approached Mercury's south pole, easing it into an initial elliptical orbit of 590 by 11,640 kilometres. Flight controllers in Germany then waited through several anxious hours while data travelled the roughly 152 million kilometres back to Earth before they could confirm the capture had worked.

## Two orbiters, one planet

The arrival is only the first step of a longer sequence. MPO and Mio are due to separate from each other on 9–10 December, after which BepiColombo will become the first mission to fly two spacecraft around Mercury at the same time. MPO will then fire its thrusters repeatedly over the coming months to descend to its final science orbit by March 2027. Science operations for both orbiters are set to begin in April 2027.

The pair will study Mercury from different vantage points. MPO will fly close to the surface, mapping craters, volcanic plains and mountain ridges, while Mio swings far out to sample the planet's magnetic field and how it interacts with the solar wind. Together they should help explain why Mercury, a planet barely bigger than the Moon, still has a magnetic field at all — and what its strange, dense interior reveals about how planets form close to their stars.

The mission is named after Giuseppe Colombo, the Italian mathematician who worked out how to use gravity assists to reach Mercury. It is the third spacecraft ever to visit the planet, and the first in more than a decade. For a world long dismissed as a dead rock, Mercury is about to get its closest look yet.
