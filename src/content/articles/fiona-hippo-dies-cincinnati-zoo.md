Fiona, the hippopotamus who became one of the world's most famous zoo animals after surviving a premature birth, has died at the Cincinnati Zoo at the age of nine.

The zoo announced her death on Thursday, November 19, eleven days after she swallowed a knit hat that had accidentally landed in her enclosure. "It is with extreme sadness that we share with you the devastating news that Fiona did not make it after multiple attempts from our amazing vets and keeper team to help her through what ended up being a medical emergency," the zoo said.

The chain of events was, in the zoo's words, almost impossible. On November 8, a child visitor tossed a knit cap into the air in front of Fiona's habitat. A ceiling fan caught it and flung it through a one-foot opening above the 12-foot-high viewing windows, and it landed in the enclosure. Fiona ate it.

At first she seemed fine, and the care team hoped she would pass the hat naturally. Within days, though, her appetite began to fall away. Vets gave her fluids and medication, sedated her for a colonoscopy using a nine-foot scope borrowed from the Columbus Zoo, and even placed her on a vibration plate borrowed from a horse farm in the hope of moving the object along. An ultrasound showed her intestines bunching, a sign that something was passing through her small intestine — but the scope could not reach the hat.

Surgery was never a realistic option. At more than 2,000 pounds, Fiona was a full-grown hippo, and the zoo said a long open incision "would've been a death sentence." Instead the team provided round-the-clock supportive care and waited. It was not enough.

A necropsy later found the hat lodged in the middle of Fiona's small intestine, about 25 feet beyond her final stomach compartment and 50 feet from the end of her intestines. "No treatment aside from supportive care had any chance for success," said Dr. Mike Wenninger, the zoo's director of animal health, "and the necropsy confirmed that no amount of supportive care would have worked."

Fiona's story had been followed around the world since her birth in January 2017. Born six weeks early at just 29 pounds, she could barely walk and could not stand long enough to nurse from her mother. Keepers hand-raised her, and her progress — first steps, first swims, first solid food — was shared online until she became an internet celebrity, with fans, merchandise and even a statue outside the zoo. Her younger brother, Fritz, was born at the zoo in August 2022.

"We are in shock at the moment," zoo director Dave Jenike said in a statement. "We did not expect this outcome. She fought until she couldn't anymore."

The news brought an outpouring of grief from fans who had watched Fiona grow up, and tributes from across Cincinnati, including the city's sports teams. The zoo has described the hat entering the enclosure as an accident, and the episode is a reminder for visitors to hold on to their belongings and respect the barriers around animal habitats.
