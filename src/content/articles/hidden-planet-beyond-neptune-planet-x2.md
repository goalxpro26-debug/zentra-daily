Astronomers may be closing in on a hidden planet beyond Neptune, according to new research that models the outer Solar System across its entire 4.5-billion-year history.

The proposed world, nicknamed Planet X2, would be about 1.5 to two times the mass of Earth, orbiting at least 200 times farther from the Sun than our planet at its closest approach. Despite decades of mapping the Solar System, such a world could have remained undetected in the dark region beyond Neptune, where sunlight is faint and objects move slowly.

The idea comes from a study led by Patryk Sofia Lykawka, an adjunct associate professor at the University of Southern Queensland based at Kindai University in Japan, with co-authors Pedro Bernardinelli and Jonti Horner, published in the Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society. The team ran computer simulations testing how a hidden planet of different masses and orbital paths would shape the distant Solar System over billions of years.

Their preferred model, a planet of about twice Earth's mass on a wide orbit 350 to 500 times Earth's distance from the Sun, reproduced several puzzling patterns among the icy bodies beyond Neptune. It generated the detached population of objects that drift far from Neptune's influence, built up a reservoir of highly tilted orbits, and matched the orbital niches of extreme objects such as Sedna and 2012 VP113, whose stretched, elongated paths have long defied easy explanation.

The simulations also tested an alternative idea, that Mars- to Earth-sized embryos scattered early in the Solar System's history could explain the same patterns. That scenario failed: the embryos were typically flung out of the Solar System within a few tens of millions of years, leaving results much like models with no extra planet at all.

Planet X2 is not the same as the better-known Planet Nine hypothesis, which proposes a much larger and more massive world in the outer Solar System. The new model offers a smaller, closer alternative, and it comes with testable predictions: the researchers say upcoming surveys, including the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time, should be able to confirm or rule out the planet's existence.

Caution is warranted. The planet has not been observed, and the model does not match every known observation. The researchers themselves note a remaining discrepancy in the distribution of orbital sizes, an area for further work. Until a telescope spots it, Planet X2 remains a hypothesis, albeit one that now fits the evidence better than the alternatives tested.

If it exists, its discovery would rewrite the map of our own cosmic neighbourhood, showing that a world larger than Earth hid in plain sight at the edge of the Solar System all along.
