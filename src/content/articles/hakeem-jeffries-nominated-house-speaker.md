House Democrats have formally nominated Hakeem Jeffries to be Speaker of the House, putting the New York lawmaker on course to become the first Black person to lead the chamber.

Jeffries won the nomination in a closed-door caucus vote on Wednesday, November 18, by 251 votes to 11. No other candidate stood against him, but 11 members voted against his nomination — a small group of holdouts that included newly elected members from the party's left wing. Seven incoming lawmakers issued a joint statement explaining their opposition, arguing that the party needed new leadership after its time in the minority.

The nomination is the first formal step. The full House will elect its Speaker on January 3, when the new Congress convenes and Democrats take control of the chamber after their midterm election victory. Jeffries will need 218 votes on the House floor to win the gavel. Democrats are projected to hold at least 257 seats in the new Congress — comfortably above that threshold — with seven midterm races still uncalled.

If elected, Jeffries would make history as the first Black lawmaker to serve as Speaker, one of the most powerful roles in American government and second in line to the presidency. The Congressional Black Caucus noted the potential milestone as the nomination was announced.

Jeffries, 56, represents a Brooklyn district and has led House Democrats since 2023, when he succeeded Nancy Pelosi as the party's leader in the chamber. He had the option of being nominated by acclamation, with no ballots cast and no count taken, but instead asked for a recorded vote — a choice that put the 11 dissenting votes on the record.

His election as Speaker is widely expected, though the nomination vote showed that a small part of the caucus remains unconvinced. How those members vote on the House floor in January will be closely watched, even if the outcome is not in serious doubt.

On the Republican side, House Republicans have nominated Judiciary Committee Chairman Jim Jordan as their candidate for Speaker, setting up a contest between the two parties' leaders when the new Congress convenes.
