Ukraine says it has used a laser weapon to shoot down a Russian jet-powered attack drone for the first time, a development Kyiv is presenting as a world first in combat air defence.

Serhii "Flash" Beskrestnov, a senior military adviser to President Volodymyr Zelenskyy on defence technology, announced the interception on Wednesday. He said the Blysk laser air defence system, developed and manufactured by the Ukrainian defence technology firm Carmine Sky, brought down the drone while protecting a critical infrastructure facility. No exact time or place was disclosed.

Beskrestnov described the strike as the first time anywhere in the world that a combat laser had destroyed a jet-powered drone. The claim has not been independently verified, and no Russian response has been reported.

The announcement matters because Russia's jet-powered drones have become one of the hardest threats for Ukraine to stop. Moscow began deploying them at scale earlier in 2026, and their higher speed makes them far more difficult to intercept than the propeller-driven attack drones that have pounded Ukrainian cities for years. In June, Ukraine was intercepting only about 57 percent of jet-powered drones, compared with about 90 percent of conventional Russian attack drones.

Russia has increasingly relied on its jet-powered Geran-4 and Geran-5 drones. Come Back Alive, a Ukrainian military nonprofit, told CBS News in September that Russia was likely producing some 1,200 Geran-4 and 800 Geran-5 drones in that month alone, and it projected those numbers would keep growing.

A laser works differently from the missiles and guns Ukraine normally uses. Instead of exploding, it holds a beam on one spot of the incoming drone until the parts inside overheat and fail. That makes each shot far cheaper than a missile, but tracking a fast, twisting target with a beam is difficult, and bad weather can scatter the light before it reaches the drone.

The Blysk system is modest by the standards of directed-energy weapons, with a reported output of about four kilowatts and an engagement range of up to roughly one kilometre. It is being developed under Ukraine's government-backed Private Air Defense program, which about 100 companies across the country have joined. Ukraine began testing laser air defence systems over the summer of 2026.

Beskrestnov was careful not to overstate what the single interception proves. "It is still too early to talk about serial production of the laser," he said, adding that tests are continuing while the systems defend critical sites.

For now, the claim stands as a single reported success rather than a proven capability. If the system works as described, it could give Ukraine a cheaper way to defend its cities against the fastest drones Russia sends — but the technology remains experimental, and its effectiveness in large numbers is untested.
