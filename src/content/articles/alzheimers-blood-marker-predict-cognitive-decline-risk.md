A simple blood test may one day tell a healthy older adult how likely they are to develop memory and thinking problems, according to a new Mayo Clinic study.

The research, published November 16 in JAMA Neurology, found that a blood marker called phosphorylated tau 217 — p-tau217 for short — can help estimate a cognitively healthy person's risk of developing mild cognitive impairment or dementia over the next five or ten years. The approach combines the blood measurement with age, sex and genetic risk. Higher levels of the marker and older age were both associated with greater risk.

P-tau217 is a protein fragment linked to Alzheimer's disease, in which tau proteins build into toxic tangles inside brain cells. Its level in the blood can rise as Alzheimer's-related changes take hold, years before everyday memory is affected.

The researchers studied 2,178 adults aged 60 to 90 with no cognitive impairment at the start: 1,644 in the Mayo Clinic Study of Aging and 534 in the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative. All had p-tau217 blood measurements, genetic testing and repeated cognitive assessments. The team built its risk estimates from the Mayo Clinic group, then confirmed the link between higher p-tau217 and greater risk of decline in the second group.

The numbers show how much the estimate can vary. For a 75-year-old woman who did not carry APOE e4, a common genetic risk variant, the estimated five-year risk of mild cognitive impairment was 5 percent with a relatively low p-tau217 level — and 16 percent with a higher one. Her estimated ten-year risk rose from 12 percent to 35 percent. Age mattered too: at the same high p-tau217 level, the estimated five-year risk ranged from 6 percent at age 65 to 37 percent at age 85.

The work builds on earlier Mayo Clinic research that used PET brain scans to measure amyloid, a protein that accumulates in the brain in Alzheimer's disease. A blood test is less invasive and potentially more scalable. It could matter more if therapies now being studied can prevent or delay decline before symptoms appear: two prevention trials, AHEAD 3-45 and TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 3, are testing amyloid-targeting treatments in people without symptoms and used blood biomarkers to help identify participants.

The researchers stress the test is not ready for routine use. The estimates were developed in the Mayo Clinic Study of Aging, whose participants come largely from southeastern Minnesota, and need further study in more diverse populations and with other p-tau217 blood tests. Biomarker testing is not currently recommended for people without symptoms, and there is no approved treatment for people who carry Alzheimer's-related changes but have no symptoms. A higher result does not mean dementia is certain — some people with Alzheimer's-related brain changes never develop symptoms.

"Absolute risk gives the biomarker result clinical context," said Clifford Jack Jr., a Mayo Clinic neuroradiologist and the study's senior author. "If preventive treatments become available, this kind of information could help patients and clinicians have more informed conversations about treatment."
