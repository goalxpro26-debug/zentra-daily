The White House's split over China policy broke into the open on Monday, as Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick fought publicly over how far the United States should go in cutting off China's access to advanced Nvidia chips.

Just hours after the White House ordered the Commerce Department to crack down on a loophole that allegedly allows Chinese companies to remotely access the chips, President Donald Trump softened the move, telling reporters as he left for Asia that he understood "both sides" of the debate.

"If you did do something, you could have a blow-up with China," Trump said on Monday morning.

Trump's reluctance to settle the question underscores the deep divisions inside his administration — and leaves unresolved a power struggle among his top economic advisers over how far to go in restricting Beijing's access to the technology fuelling the artificial intelligence race.

At the centre of the fight are Bessent and Lutnick, two of Trump's closest economic aides, who have sparred over the scope of export controls on Nvidia's most advanced processors. Bessent has publicly criticised the Commerce Department for not taking a tougher approach, saying other agencies pushed for restrictions but that "Commerce has the pen." Trump had previously directed Lutnick to pursue restrictions, according to The Washington Post, but the Commerce chief resisted, arguing a crackdown would risk a trade war with China. The rivalry between the two men extends back to the presidential transition.

The fight flared with a new federal rule aimed at closing the remote-access loophole — the latest flashpoint in the scandal that led to the ouster of Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang last week.

Nvidia's board is investigating whether Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort, after reports of his close relationship with Alice Huang, a former Megaspeed executive whom US intelligence officials believe passed information to China. Alice Huang is not related to Jensen Huang. Beijing has called the accusations groundless, and the Justice Department has said it has reached no conclusions.

Congress has widened its own inquiry. Three Republican House committee chairmen have asked Huang to attend a closed-door interview on December 1, with Nvidia's general counsel Tim Teter due the following day; the Senate Banking Committee has separately sought testimony from Huang and Lutnick. Democrats on the House Foreign Affairs Committee have written to Lutnick demanding a halt to advanced chip exports and answers by December 1 on what senior officials knew.

Huang's ties to Trump have coincided with several favourable policy decisions for Nvidia, including a decision in April 2025 to back off restricting exports of certain Nvidia chips to China, and a December 2025 decision to allow exports of its H200 processors with a 25 percent fee. Trump has defended Huang, saying he believes the chief executive did not know the businesswoman was a suspected spy.

Trump has not said which side he will back, and the December hearings will test how much the administration knew about Megaspeed and when.
