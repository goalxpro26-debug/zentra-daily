An unpiloted SpaceX Dragon cargo spacecraft splashed down in the Pacific Ocean near Los Angeles at 2:03 p.m. PST on Saturday, closing NASA's 35th commercial resupply mission to the International Space Station and delivering more than 6,300 pounds of science back to Earth.

Dragon undocked from the station's Harmony module at 11:05 a.m. CST on Friday, after more than five weeks docked to the orbiting laboratory. The capsule had arrived at the station on October 15, two days after launching aboard a Falcon 9 rocket from Space Launch Complex 40 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida. Its departure had been pushed back from Wednesday by unfavourable weather at the splashdown zone off the California coast.

Most of what came home is research. The returning cargo includes a blood vessel construct made with a 3D bioprinter, which scientists will use to study how blood vessels age and how cardiovascular disease develops, in space and on Earth. Frozen tumour organoids — small lab-grown clusters of cells that mimic a tumour — were treated aboard the station and frozen for the trip home, in work researchers hope will point to better cancer treatments and more personalised medicine. Scientists will also analyse frozen microbial samples to learn how bacteria that resist antibiotics survive, adapt and evolve during spaceflight.

A lighting system that spent more than three years aboard the station also returned, after researchers tested its potential to support astronauts' sleep and wellbeing on long-duration missions. The crew packed a wrist joint from the station's Canadarm2 robotic arm that failed in May, so engineers can examine it on the ground.

SpaceX said the capsule would announce its arrival with a brief sonic boom as it tore through the atmosphere faster than the speed of sound, before parachutes lowered it into the Pacific. NASA did not stream the splashdown, instead posting updates on its space station blog.

Dragon is one of the few vehicles that can bring experiments back intact, rather than leaving scientists to rely only on readings transmitted from orbit. The samples now head to laboratories on the ground, while the station's crew continues the research programme the resupply flights keep stocked.
