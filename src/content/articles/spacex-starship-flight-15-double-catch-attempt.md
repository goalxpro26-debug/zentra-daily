SpaceX's Starship Flight 15 lifted off from Texas on Friday evening with an ambitious goal: to become the first fully reusable orbital rocket by catching both of its stages at the launch tower. It got halfway there.

The 15th test flight of the world's largest rocket launched at 5:47 p.m. EST from SpaceX's Starbase site in Boca Chica, Texas. Minutes later, the plan for the Super Heavy booster began to unravel. A technical problem a little more than six minutes after liftoff prevented the booster from returning to the pad, and flight controllers diverted it to the Gulf of Mexico, where it splashed down and promptly burst into flames as residual propellant ignited.

It was a setback for a manoeuvre SpaceX had already pulled off three times. The last successful tower catch of a Super Heavy booster came in March 2025, and this flight was the first attempt with a new version of the booster — and the first catch try at the flight's launch pad.

## The ship is still flying

The upper stage, known as the Ship, fared better. After an 18-minute coast on an initially sub-orbital path, a single Raptor engine reignited for a short 10-second burn that pushed Starship into its planned orbit.

It then released its cargo: 26 upgraded Starlink V3 internet satellites, the second time a Starship flight has carried operational satellites to orbit. All 26 were deployed and confirmed in contact. Three of the satellites carry cameras to photograph the ship's heat shield in orbit, giving engineers a look at its condition before the return.

The Ship is now on a roughly 22-hour mission, circling Earth about 14 times. If it stays healthy, SpaceX will attempt something never tried before: catching an orbital upper stage at the launch tower. A single Raptor is expected to fire on Saturday afternoon to begin the de-orbit, followed by a 56-minute belly-first re-entry and a return to Starbase. If anything looks wrong, the ship will be ditched in the ocean instead.

## Why it matters

Full reusability is the core of SpaceX's case for Starship. Catching and reflying both stages would slash the cost of putting mass into orbit, which the company needs to expand its Starlink network and, eventually, to fly cargo and crew to the Moon and Mars.

Friday's flight showed both sides of that bet in a single evening: a booster lost to a fault minutes into flight, and a ship that reached orbit and delivered its payload as planned. Whether the mission ends as a partial success or a landmark depends on what happens when the Ship comes home on Saturday.
