Body-mass index, the height-and-weight formula doctors have used for decades to diagnose obesity, is missing millions of people who have the condition — and wrongly labelling millions more, according to a large international study published in the journal Nature.

The research compared BMI-only diagnosis against a newer framework across 844,108 adults in four groups covering China, the United Kingdom, the United States and Spain. BMI alone missed obesity in up to 8.6 percent of participants, while wrongly classifying up to 3.1 percent as having obesity when they did not meet the newer criteria. Scaled up, the authors say, that is millions of people with the wrong diagnosis.

BMI divides a person's weight by their height squared, and it has long been criticised as too blunt an instrument: it cannot tell muscle from fat, and it says nothing about where fat sits in the body or whether it is doing harm. The newer framework, put forward by experts in 2025, keeps BMI as a starting point but adds waist circumference, waist-to-height ratio and body fat scans, and checks for signs of organ damage or difficulty with everyday activities.

## Two kinds of obesity

A central part of the new approach is the split between clinical obesity, in which excess fat is actively damaging organs, and preclinical obesity, in which a person carries excess fat but it has not yet begun to harm bodily functions.

The study found that up to a third of obesity cases were clinical — an active disease that should be treated as such, according to Francesco Rubino, a bariatric surgery consultant at King's College London who led the work. Most people with preclinical obesity remained free of clinical obesity for a decade.

The difference matters for survival. Being clinically obese was associated with a 69 percent higher risk of dying during follow-up periods of more than a decade, compared with 19 percent for preclinical obesity — a gap that traditional BMI diagnosis would not flag.

"Preclinical obesity should therefore be neither treated automatically as active disease nor dismissed as harmless," Rubino said. "Some people may need preventive treatment, but its intensity should match their individual risk."

## What could change

If the framework is adopted more widely, the researchers say treatments such as GLP-1 weight-loss drugs could be directed to the people who genuinely need them, rather than to everyone classified as obese under BMI rules.

There are caveats. Some outside experts have questioned whether the ethnicity-specific thresholds used are the best global definition, and the study relied on diagnostic codes as stand-ins for organ damage rather than direct clinical assessments. The team is planning further studies to refine the techniques and test how the criteria work in practice.

"The marked differences in prognosis between clinical and preclinical obesity, consistently observed across countries and ethnic groups, support global adoption of the new framework," said epidemiologist An Pan of Huazhong University of Science and Technology in China, a co-author of the study.
