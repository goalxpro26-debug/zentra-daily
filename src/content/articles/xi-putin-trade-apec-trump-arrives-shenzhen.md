China's president used the opening of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit on Wednesday to make the case for open trade, hours before Donald Trump arrived in Shenzhen for a first-of-its-kind meeting of the three leaders.

Xi Jinping told business executives gathered in the southern Chinese technology and financial hub that the Asia-Pacific must pursue more open trade and equitable growth if it is to remain stable and dynamic. He promised China would cut tariffs, widen market access and push ahead with reforms to rules, regulations and standards, presenting Beijing as a steady partner for global investors.

"While pursuing its own development, China has shared opportunities with other countries and worked for common development," Xi said, adding that China had brought "stability and predictability to world economic growth."

The message landed as a deliberate contrast with Washington. Trump's "America First" approach of tariff hikes and other trade barriers runs counter to APEC's long-term aim of a Pacific Rim free trade zone, and US limits on China's access to leading-edge technology — including the Nvidia chips that power artificial intelligence — remain a constant source of strain. Xi did not mention export controls, Nvidia or the United States directly, according to analysts at the summit.

Russian President Vladimir Putin followed Xi on stage with a sharper attack on Western economic pressure. He described sanctions, tariffs and export restrictions as "instruments of economic dominance" that have "outlived their usefulness and have largely lost their intimidating effect." It was his first in-person appearance at an APEC leaders' meeting since 2017. Putin said the Asia-Pacific now accounts for more than half of Russia's total trade, and that Moscow has submitted a bid to chair APEC in 2035.

Trump arrived on Wednesday from the Philippines, where he attended the annual summit of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations. The three leaders are due to meet behind closed doors on Thursday, in a trilateral encounter that could touch on Russia's war in Ukraine, the US war in Iran, trade, artificial intelligence and nuclear weapons.

The gathering comes with real pressures behind the pageantry. China's economy is still growing at more than 4 per cent, but retail spending and investment have dragged during a prolonged property market slump, and its trade surplus is on track to exceed $1 trillion for a second straight year — a figure that has drawn backlash from trading partners. Friction with Washington is also pushing other countries to hedge: on Tuesday, Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney met Xi and the two agreed to cut tariffs on each other's goods.

The 21 APEC economies account for 60 per cent of global economic activity, half of global trade and 40 per cent of humanity. The forum takes no binding decisions, but over 37 years it has served as a place to build consensus on cooperation and more open trade — and this week's meeting will test whether that consensus still holds.
