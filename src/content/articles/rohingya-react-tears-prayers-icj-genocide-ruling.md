In the crowded refugee camps of Cox's Bazar, Bangladesh, news of the International Court of Justice's genocide ruling arrived on mobile phones, laptop screens and communal televisions — and was met with tears, prayers and celebration.

Umme Ruman had been waiting for the verdict since the afternoon, watching from her shelter in Camp 1E. Poor internet kept interrupting the live broadcast, so she opened Facebook and saw the headline: the court had confirmed that Myanmar committed genocide. Overwhelmed, she jumped up and rushed to hug her sister-in-law, breaking down in tears before she could explain what had happened.

"I celebrated with tears of joy because, for the first time, the world acknowledged what happened to us," she said.

On Thursday, the court in The Hague found Myanmar responsible for genocide against the Rohingya during brutal military operations in 2017, in which it found more than 10,000 people had been killed. The case was brought by The Gambia under the 1948 Genocide Convention, and the decision — reached by nine votes to six — was the first time in the court's history that it has held a state responsible for the crime.

Across the camps, people followed the announcement together, many of them on Facebook and YouTube. Eighteen-year-old Mohammad Rehan watched on a laptop with his community teacher and friends. "Some women were crying, while others were praying to Allah," he said. His first thought was that the verdict could open the door for the Rohingya to return home. "Just imagining that made me so happy," he said. When he told his mother, she said they had won and would be able to return to their homeland.

The celebrations came amid difficult living conditions. Rain fell over the camps on Thursday as Cyclone Akvan bore down on the Bay of Bengal — a reminder that recognition had not changed the daily realities of refugee life. Nurul Amin, a young Rohingya man, said he was happy when the court recognised the genocide, but the announcement also brought back painful memories of 2017 and the home he had to leave behind. Activist Rohimullah described the judgement as a moment of relief and renewed hope — but said it could not bring back loved ones or erase painful memories.

Nasir Uddin, an anthropology professor at the University of Chittagong who studies the crisis, called the judgement essential to establishing global justice. Michael Kugelman of the Atlantic Council said it would shape negotiations between Bangladesh and Myanmar over refugee returns, and that Dhaka would need stronger assurances of a safe return.

What the ruling does not settle is whether the refugees will actually be able to go home. The court has no police force of its own, so enforcement depends on other states and UN bodies. For the Rohingya, the hope is accountability, restored rights and citizenship, and a safe, dignified return. "We are hoping this judgement will lead to something more — that we will finally be able to return home with our rights and dignity," Nurul Amin said.
