When Yevgeny Prigozhin called Nigerian investigative journalist Philip Obaji Jr. a "cockroach" in 2022, the Wagner Group chief was threatening him for reporting on Russian mercenaries in Africa. A year later, Obaji was locked overnight in a shipping container full of insects and rats in the Central African Republic, detained by local soldiers acting on the orders of Russian mercenaries. He was released the next morning, treated for his wounds in Cameroon — and within days was back in the Central African Republic to finish the investigation.

"I did not want to go home empty-handed," Obaji, now 41, told the Kyiv Independent.

Obaji, a former marine biology student and sports television presenter, turned to reporting on human rights abuses. His interest in Russia began in 2018, when three Russian journalists were killed while investigating Wagner's operations in the Central African Republic. Reporting then for The Daily Beast alongside Russia correspondent Anna Nemtsova, he took up what they had been investigating.

Weeks before Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, Obaji learned from contacts inside Wagner that more than 200 of its fighters had been withdrawn from the Central African Republic and flown to Moscow for training ahead of deployment to Ukraine. Seeking confirmation, he contacted Prigozhin directly and received a threatening phone call. His photograph later circulated in a WhatsApp group used by Wagner fighters in the country, he said, with commanders instructing their men to arrest or kill him if spotted.

He returned more than a year after the threat to investigate Wagner's future following Prigozhin's death in a plane crash in August 2023. At the Cameroon border, where he had gone to interview people displaced by Russian paramilitary operations in gold-mining areas, four Central African soldiers attacked him and dragged him into a small container. He was freed the following morning after his driver, a motorcyclist, sought help from a senior military officer.

His reporting traced how revenue from African gold helped finance Russia's war — a finding later cited in a 2023 investigation by the anti-corruption group The Sentry. The U.S. Treasury that year sanctioned companies in the Central African Republic, the United Arab Emirates and Russia involved in illicit gold trading that financed Wagner's operations.

Africa is also a growing source of manpower for the war. Ukraine's military intelligence has identified 5,451 Africans among nearly 46,000 foreign nationals under contract with the Russian military; a United Nations commission found Russian authorities had trafficked people from 29 countries to recruit them. Recruits are often promised civilian work, then handed contracts written in Russian — and told the only option is the front line in Ukraine, Obaji said.

In the first nine months of 2026, Russia recruited 699 Burundians and 530 Zimbabweans, Ukrainian intelligence says — up from 30 and 65 in all of 2025. Kenya and Ghana have asked Moscow to stop recruiting their citizens; South Africa and Botswana have warned people against lucrative job offers in Russia. Asked about Russia's plans for 2027, Obaji said: "They definitely have to spread their tentacles across the continent."
