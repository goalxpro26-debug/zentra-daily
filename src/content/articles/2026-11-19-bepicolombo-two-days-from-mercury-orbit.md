After eight years in space, the European-Japanese BepiColombo mission is two days from entering orbit around Mercury, with the spacecraft due to be captured by the planet's gravity on Saturday, 21 November.

BepiColombo is a joint mission of the European Space Agency (ESA) and the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA). It carries two orbiters — ESA's Mercury Planetary Orbiter (MPO) and JAXA's Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter, known as Mio — still travelling together as one stacked spacecraft. It will be the first mission to orbit Mercury with two spacecraft at the same time, and only the second mission ever to enter orbit around the planet, after NASA's MESSENGER, which circled Mercury from 2011 to 2015.

The journey has been long by design. BepiColombo launched on an Ariane 5 rocket from Kourou in French Guiana on 20 October 2018, and has since travelled roughly 9.9 billion kilometres, using nine planetary flybys — one of Earth, two of Venus and six of Mercury itself — to shed speed. Mercury is the hardest planet to reach in the Solar System in one sense: falling towards the Sun, a spacecraft must lose a great deal of energy before the planet's weak gravity can capture it.

Arrival was originally planned for December 2025. In April 2024, engineers found that the transfer module's electric thrusters could not deliver full power, and ESA recalculated the route, pushing arrival back by almost a year. The Mercury Transfer Module, which had powered and steered the spacecraft for most of the journey, separated from the two orbiters on 3 September 2026, more than 200 million kilometres from Earth.

Since then, the mission team has been working through a long checklist. On 24 September, MPO fired its axial thrusters for the first time in the new configuration, an eight-minute, 42-second burn that delivered a precise 11.4 metres-per-second course correction. On 15 September, all of MPO's instruments were switched on together for the first time since the transfer module separated: the laser altimeter BELA fired its laser in space for the first time, emitting more than 700,000 pulses over 21.5 hours, and the science camera SIMBIO-SYS took its first image. On 29 October, the mission control team in Darmstadt completed its final simulation of the arrival sequence, rehearsing the separation of Mio's sunshield and interface structure, an operation scheduled for 16 December.

Saturday's orbit insertion is the pivotal moment, and it will happen largely on its own. Mercury is about 150 million kilometres from Earth — roughly eight light-minutes away — so no command from the ground can steer the burn in real time. If the thrusters fire as programmed, the two orbiters will be captured into a polar orbit together.

What follows is a months-long sequence. MPO and Mio are due to separate on 9–10 December, moving into their own science orbits: MPO into a close polar orbit, about 480 by 1,500 kilometres above the surface, to map Mercury and study its composition and interior; Mio into a much wider orbit, about 590 by 11,640 kilometres, to study the planet's magnetic field and the plasma around it. Full science operations are set to begin in April 2027.

Mercury is the least explored of the Solar System's rocky planets, and the two-orbiters-at-once approach is the point of the mission: surface and magnetic-field measurements taken simultaneously should finally explain how a planet so small sustains a magnetic field at all, and what its cratered surface reveals about the early Solar System. By coincidence, Mercury is also at its widest angle from the Sun in the morning sky this week, so the planet itself is visible low in the east before dawn on the day its new visitor arrives.

ESA notes that exact dates can still change for operational reasons. Confirmation that the capture burn worked is expected hours after the event, on the agency's arrival-updates page.
