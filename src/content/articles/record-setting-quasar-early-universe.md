Astronomers have found the most distant quasar ever seen, a blazing galactic core already shining just 630 million years after the Big Bang, when the universe was less than 5% of its current age.

Formally catalogued as J1504+6252, it appears as it was more than 13.1 billion years ago, beating the previous record holder, announced only in July, by about 40 million years — the largest single leap in the quasar record in nearly a decade.

A quasar is the fiercely bright heart of a galaxy, powered by a supermassive black hole feeding on gas and dust. As material spirals inward, it forms a disc heated to extreme temperatures, radiating so much energy that a quasar can outshine its entire host galaxy. That brilliance makes quasars visible across billions of light-years, and lets astronomers use them as beacons to study the early universe.

What startles researchers is the size as much as the distance. The black hole powering it had already grown to about 360 million times the mass of the sun — roughly 90 times the Milky Way's central black hole. "Just 630 million years after the Big Bang, this black hole had already grown to about 360 million times the mass of the sun and was shining as a quasar," said Julien Wolf of the European Southern Observatory, who led the study.

Euclid, the European Space Agency's space telescope, first spotted it among millions of candidates in about 18 months of survey observations. Follow-up work with the Hubble and James Webb space telescopes, the W. M. Keck Observatory in Hawaii and the Large Binocular Telescope in Arizona confirmed it and pinned down its distance: a redshift of 8.13, the first quasar found beyond redshift 8.

The team nicknamed it Dún Aonghasa, after a prehistoric stone fort on a cliff on Inis Mór, off Ireland's west coast, which looks out over the Atlantic much as the quasar stands at the edge of what telescopes can see.

It deepens a long-running puzzle: how did black holes grow so large so quickly? One possibility is that this one began as the remnant of one of the first stars, but it was feeding too slowly to have reached its size that way. The other is that it was born big, when a vast cloud of gas collapsed directly into a black hole. The team's calculations favour the second explanation.

It dates to the epoch when the first stars and galaxies were burning through the hydrogen fog left over from the Big Bang, making the universe transparent to light for the first time.

More may be coming: Euclid could yet find quasars even farther away, and models suggest the first quasars could date to 270 million to 470 million years after the Big Bang. "So in short: this is the furthest quasar we have ever seen, but maybe not the furthest there is to find," said study co-author Silvia Belladitta of the Max Planck Institute for Astronomy.
