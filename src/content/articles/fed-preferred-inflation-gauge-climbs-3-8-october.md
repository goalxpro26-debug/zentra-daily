US inflation picked up again in October, with the Federal Reserve's preferred gauge rising to 3.8% from a year earlier — nearly twice the central bank's 2% target — and all but guaranteeing another interest-rate increase next month.

The personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index, published by the Bureau of Economic Analysis on Wednesday, rose 0.3% on the month. The annual rate climbed from a revised 3.7% in September, matching economists' expectations. Core PCE, which strips out volatile food and energy prices and is watched most closely by the Fed, rose to 3.3% from 3.1% — its highest level in nearly three years and slightly above forecasts.

The report was the last major inflation reading before the Fed's final policy meeting of the year, on December 8–9. Traders now price in a near-certain quarter-point increase, which would lift the benchmark rate to a range of 4% to 4.25%. The odds of a further rise in January stand at roughly 56%, according to the CME FedWatch Tool.

The figures land at an awkward moment for the central bank. The Fed held rates steady in October to get a clearer picture of price trends, after raising them by a quarter point in September. Instead of cooling, inflation has moved the other way, and the question facing policymakers is no longer whether to raise rates in December, but how far to go in 2027.

Fed Governor Christopher Waller has floated raising rates by a quarter point in both December and January, or by half a point in December alone. Others on the rate-setting committee have been more cautious, and the split reflects a wider tension: borrowing costs are already biting for households and businesses, yet price pressures have not been tamed.

Markets took the data largely in stride. Treasury yields slipped after a flurry of Wednesday releases, which also included a second estimate of third-quarter economic growth. Investors appear to have accepted a December rise as a done deal; the bigger open question is whether the Fed signals more tightening to come next year, at a time when stocks have already been sliding on worries about the artificial-intelligence boom.

The next reading of the Fed's preferred gauge is due on December 23 — two weeks after policymakers wrap up their final meeting of the year. Until then, the October numbers leave the central bank with little room to pause: inflation is rising, core pressures are at a near-three-year high, and the target is still a long way off.
