U.S. air travel should be back to normal by the end of Sunday, the head of the Federal Aviation Administration said on Saturday, as airlines worked through the backlog from the longest nationwide ground stop in 25 years. FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford said carriers would "still have delays and cancellations" on Saturday, but promised that the "worst is behind us."

The ground stop lasted more than four hours on Friday, halting every departing flight in the country after air traffic control centres lost reliable radar and communications. It was the first nationwide stop since January 2023, when a computer system that sends notices to pilots failed, and only the second since the attacks of September 11, 2001. FlightAware counted nearly 25,000 delayed flights and more than 9,000 cancellations on Friday; by Saturday morning, more than 4,000 further cancellations had been recorded — about 14 percent of flights departing the United States, according to aviation data firm Cirium.

Trump administration officials said new technology being added to the air traffic control system overloaded antiquated equipment, causing intermittent gaps in communications and radar. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said a contractor had been replacing a section of the system's old copper wiring with new fibre, and that the work somehow triggered the overload. The contractor is L3Harris, a long-time FAA supplier, which was carrying out routine maintenance at a remote FAA location. Duffy said some of the routers involved were manufactured in 2001, and officials were investigating whether the fault came down to contractor error or a faulty piece of equipment. "Our contractor didn't meet the moment," Duffy said.

Bedford said there was no evidence of anything nefarious or of any AI-related problem, and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said there was no evidence the failure was an attack. The FAA has recently begun rolling out an AI-enhanced software tool known as SMART, but its maker, Air Space Intelligence, said the ground stop was not based on or a result of its product.

The disruption rippled far beyond Friday. Aviation consultant Andrew Charlton said the stoppage affected about a quarter of the roughly 45,000 flights that operate in the United States each day, and the knock-on effects — aircraft and crews out of position, passengers stranded — were expected to linger for days. Duffy said on Friday night that he expected the system to be "back up and operational as we come into the heat of travel during Thanksgiving." In a detail that underlined the department's struggling systems, his Saturday news conference began with audio troubles that forced last-minute microphone swaps.

Bedford said airlines told officials they expected "full schedule integrity by the close of business on Sunday." The FAA is still investigating the exact error that set off the cascade. L3Harris did not immediately respond to a request for comment.
