A US-led consortium headed by billionaire Todd Boehly has agreed to acquire the international energy business of Lukoil, Russia's second-largest oil producer, in a deal that gives Washington an ownership stake and ends more than a year of fraught negotiations over the sanctioned company's overseas empire.

The consortium — Boehly's investment firm Eldridge, the US International Development Finance Corporation (DFC), UAE-based Catena Investment Holding and US investment bank Xtellus Partners — announced a definitive agreement on Wednesday to buy LUKOIL International GmbH, the unit that holds the group's assets outside Russia.

No cash will change hands. Instead, the buyers will hand over Lukoil securities worth about $10 billion, bought from US and British funds and depositary banks that held them on behalf of retail investors. Those shares were frozen after Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022 and written down as near-worthless in the West. Under the deal they will be held in a restricted custodial account in the United Arab Emirates for five years, or until sanctions are lifted.

The portfolio being sold is sprawling: oil and gas operations in 11 countries producing around 350,000 barrels of oil equivalent a day, three refineries in Bulgaria, the Netherlands and Romania, some 2,000 filling stations across 16 countries in Europe, North America and Central Asia, and an international trading business. Reuters reported the package is valued at roughly $16 billion. Boehly said closing the deal could take up to a year, and that the venture would be renamed.

The DFC's participation gives the US government an equity stake — its size undisclosed — entitling Washington to a share of future profits once the private investors have recouped their capital plus an agreed return, and a role on the company's governing board.

The sale was forced by sanctions. The US Treasury sanctioned Lukoil more than a year ago in a bid to cut off revenue funding Moscow's war in Ukraine. Petras Katinas, a research fellow at the Royal United Services Institute in London, told CNN the deal showed sanctions working: a company that spent decades building an international footprint is being made to give it up, removing both future earnings and a lever of influence over energy infrastructure in Europe and beyond.

It caps a long and messy sale process. An offer from Swiss commodities trader Gunvor collapsed after failing to win Treasury approval, and private equity firm Carlyle's provisional agreement in January lapsed in July without a Treasury decision; Carlyle walked away in October.

The new agreement is not yet done. It still needs conditional approval from the Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control, which has previously rejected a similar share-swap structure, and it carries a 60-day exclusivity period to finalise terms.
