The Trump administration on Wednesday unveiled a new round of proposed tariffs covering 14 trading partners, using the threat of sharply higher duties to push countries into trade deals with Washington.

The proposals follow Section 301 investigations by the U.S. Trade Representative, which concluded that the 14 economies' policies on excess manufacturing capacity were unreasonable and burdened U.S. commerce. Headline rates would run from 20% to 40%, but duties would be capped lower for countries that have completed trade deals or are still negotiating.

China faces the steepest treatment: a capped rate of 20% on top of roughly 25% in duties imposed during President Donald Trump's first term. That rate could rise to 40% if a U.S.-China trade truce, reached last October and due to expire in January, is not extended. Vietnam would face 35% without a deal and 20% with one; India 25% or 17%. Cambodia, Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand and Bangladesh would face caps of 18%, while Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, the European Union and Switzerland would face 15%, including existing most-favoured-nation duties. Singapore's cap would be 10%.

The duties would not take effect soon. U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said they would not come into force until after a public hearing on January 25; a senior official said they could take effect as early as January, though Greer said it would likely be months. Mexico and Norway were left out because investigations into them are still under way. Separately, the USTR proposed a 10% tariff on finished Embraer aircraft from Brazil and higher duties on Brazilian sugar above quotas.

Beijing rejected the claim of excess capacity. Chinese Commerce Ministry spokesperson He Yadong called on Washington to "rectify its wrongdoing and return to the right track of resolving issues through dialogue," and urged the U.S. to implement $30 billion in reciprocal tariff cuts the two sides agreed in late September. Vietnam's trade ministry said it was working to complete a deal with the U.S. as soon as possible. Singapore said it was "very disappointed" and disagreed with the findings, noting the U.S. has run a trade surplus with the city-state for more than 20 years.

Analysts said the overall impact was likely to be limited. Mark Williams, chief Asia economist at Capital Economics, said the proposal "puts most of the targeted countries back where they were earlier in the year," after the Supreme Court struck down Trump's emergency tariffs in February. But the gap between the with-deal and without-deal rates gives India and Vietnam a strong incentive to conclude agreements, said Wendy Cutler of the Asia Society Policy Institute, who added that Beijing seemed prepared to live with the new rates provided they do not exceed 20%. Exemptions for semiconductors and other electronics should cushion South Korea and Taiwan.

Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping are expected to meet for a fourth time this year at the Group of 20 summit in Miami in December.
