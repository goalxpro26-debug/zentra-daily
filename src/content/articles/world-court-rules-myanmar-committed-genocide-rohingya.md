The International Court of Justice ruled on Thursday that Myanmar committed genocide against its Rohingya minority during a military crackdown in 2017, the first time in the court's 80-year history that it has held a state responsible for the crime.

Judges in The Hague found that Myanmar's security forces killed thousands of Rohingya civilians, carried out widespread acts of sexual violence and extreme violence against children, and attacked people as they fled in large numbers to Bangladesh. The court said the only reasonable explanation for the scale and nature of the violence was an intention to destroy a substantial part of the Rohingya population in northern Rakhine State.

The case was brought by The Gambia in November 2019 under the 1948 Genocide Convention, which obliges states to prevent and punish genocide. The court's decision, reached by nine votes to six, is final and cannot be appealed.

The ruling closes a case that has run for seven years. In August 2017, Myanmar's military launched what it called clearance operations in Rakhine State after attacks by Rohingya militants on security posts. More than 730,000 Rohingya fled across the border into Bangladesh in the weeks that followed, in one of the fastest mass displacements in modern history. Most remain in refugee camps there today.

The court acknowledged that the militant group known as the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army had attacked security outposts and civilians. But it found that the military's response went far beyond anything that could be explained as punishment or counter-insurgency, pointing in particular to indiscriminate killings, the separation and execution of men, sexual violence, and the deliberate burning of Rohingya homes.

The judgment is only the fourth time since the Second World War that an international court has found the legal threshold for genocide to have been met. Human rights groups called it a rare victory for survivors. Amnesty International said the finding gave formal legal recognition to atrocities that had been documented for years.

The ruling binds Myanmar under international law, but the court has no police force or enforcement arm of its own. Whether it changes anything on the ground will depend on what other states and United Nations bodies do next. Separate proceedings at the International Criminal Court, which deals with the criminal responsibility of individuals rather than states, are still open: in 2024 its prosecutor sought an arrest warrant for Myanmar's military leader, Min Aung Hlaing.

For the Rohingya, a stateless Muslim minority that has faced decades of discrimination in Myanmar, the judgment is the first time an international court has said plainly that what happened to them in 2017 was genocide.
