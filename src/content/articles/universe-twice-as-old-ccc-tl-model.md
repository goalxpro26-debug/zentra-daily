An alternative picture of the cosmos — one in which the universe is 26.7 billion years old and neither dark matter nor dark energy exists — has survived a series of major observational tests, according to a new study from the University of Ottawa.

The standard model of cosmology, known as ΛCDM, puts the universe at about 13.8 billion years old and holds that dark matter and dark energy make up roughly 95 percent of everything there is. Neither has ever been directly detected, and the model has struggled to explain the surprisingly large, mature galaxies the James Webb Space Telescope keeps finding in the very early universe.

The new work, published in the journal *Universe*, tests a framework called CCC+TL — short for "covarying coupling constants plus tired light" — developed by adjunct physics professor Rajendra Gupta and postdoctoral researcher Nikolaos Samaras. Instead of invisible matter and energy, the model says the effects attributed to them come from fundamental physical constants that change over time. It also rewrites the Einstein and Friedmann equations that describe gravity and cosmic expansion, so the researchers had to build new equations and computer code for every test.

## What the tests found

The team's results, gathered during 2025 and 2026, agreed with several pillars of observational cosmology. The model's predictions for the amounts of hydrogen, helium and other light elements forged shortly after the Big Bang matched those of the standard model. It also agreed with cosmic-chronometer measurements, which reconstruct the history of cosmic expansion from the ages of galaxies at different distances.

In a separate paper in *Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society*, the researchers applied the model to all 175 galaxies in the SPARC database and found it could reproduce their rotation curves — the speeds at which stars orbit galactic centres — without any dark matter at all. A study of time dilation in supernova observations also supports the model, although that paper is still under review.

The team also looked at the Bullet Cluster, where the extra gravitational effect the model predicts follows the galaxies rather than the hot, X-ray-emitting gas. The researchers describe that result as an early proof of concept that needs more study.

## More time for early galaxies

The older universe may also resolve one of the standard model's most awkward problems: galaxies that look too grown-up, too early. In the standard picture, the universe was only about 300 million years old during cosmic dawn. Under CCC+TL, it would already have been about 4.6 billion years old at the same observed redshift — giving galaxies far longer to grow. In the researchers' calculations, early galaxies come out about 12 times larger in size and mass than the standard model predicts.

"Impossible early galaxies become predictable galaxies," Gupta said. The model even predicts galaxies beyond the distances the James Webb Space Telescope has reached so far — a claim the telescope's GLIMPSE programme may soon test by searching for galaxies at redshifts that could exceed 50, where the standard model may not leave enough time for massive galaxies to form.

## What is still unproven

Passing tests is not the same as being right. The biggest remaining check — the cosmic microwave background, the afterglow of the Big Bang — has not yet been published for this model, although Gupta says the team's findings meet that challenge. The supernova result is still under peer review, and the Bullet Cluster work is preliminary.

"The success should encourage astronomers to take the new model seriously. It is challenging to understand and apply, but we believe it extends the physics of the universe beyond ΛCDM limits," Gupta said. For now, the 26.7-billion-year universe is a serious contender, not a settled fact — and the next round of telescope observations will help decide whether it stays one.
