Three days after the Federal Aviation Administration switched on a new artificial intelligence tool across the whole of American airspace, a communications failure grounded flights nationwide. Officials insist the two events are unrelated — but the timing has put the new system, and the idea of AI in air traffic control, under a sharper spotlight than the agency planned.

The tool is called SMART, short for Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes and Trajectories. The FAA expanded it to all 22 air route traffic control centres on November 10, at a reported cost of $875 million. It was built by the startup Air Space Intelligence, known as ASI, and it pulls together some 200 streams of data — weather, flight paths, traffic flow and controller staffing — into a single picture of where congestion or bad weather could cause trouble.

Crucially, SMART does not control aircraft. It makes recommendations. Specialists at a single office near Washington study its output and pass suggestions to the FAA's command centre, which relays them to controllers for consideration. Humans remain the decision-makers, and the system is not connected to the FAA's core radar and flight-data systems.

## Officials say there is no link

The nationwide ground stop came on Friday, November 13, when communications problems hit air traffic control centres. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said there was no indication the problems were linked to the new system, and senior Transportation Department and FAA officials repeated that on Saturday. ASI issued a direct denial of its own, saying the ground stop was not based on, or a result of, SMART or any of its products.

The FAA says the system proved itself in a Washington-area trial this autumn, helping reroute 180 flights and saving an average of 10 minutes and 80 gallons of fuel per flight by steering aircraft around weather. One flight from Tampa to Dallas cut 42 minutes off its trip, according to the agency.

## The questions were already being asked

The scrutiny did not start with the ground stop. Last month, House Democrats wrote to the Government Accountability Office asking for an audit of SMART, saying the front-line aviation workforce had "little to no input" into its development and limited training during deployment. Their letter raised concerns about reliability, safety guardrails, and whether an AI system could interfere with experienced controllers' decisions or "hallucinate safety-critical information."

Aviation safety specialists echo the caution. Mike O'Donnell, a former senior FAA official who now works as a safety consultant, said the agency must make sure SMART stays "just a tool in the toolbox and not the actual machine that's doing the work," adding that the FAA "cannot afford to be wrong on this."

ASI's co-founder and chief executive, Phillip Buckendorf, has made the same point from the other side: the AI does the maths, but the human operator decides. "They make the decision, they are the decider, they are the judge," he told NPR in October.

The debate also lands against a long-running shortage of air traffic controllers. The controllers' union, which represents about 14,000 controllers — roughly 3,000 of them trainees — has warned about staffing for more than a decade. Experts say no AI system can replace controllers who train for years for one of the most demanding jobs in transport.

What caused the ground stop itself is still under investigation, and no published technical finding has linked it to SMART or to any AI component. Until that investigation reports, the questions around the new tool are about trust and guardrails — not about a proven failure.
