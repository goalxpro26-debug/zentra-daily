The Federal Aviation Administration has paused the installation of new telecommunications routers across its air-traffic network while investigators determine how a routine equipment upgrade triggered Friday's nationwide ground stop.

The pause, first reported by The Wall Street Journal on Tuesday and confirmed by the FAA to CNN, halts the rollout of Juniper routers that contractor L3Harris Technologies has been installing to replace the agency's ageing copper-wire communications system. The FAA said it had also disabled a broadcasting function on the routers involved — and on more than 60 others — as a precaution.

The FAA said it believed disabling that function had addressed the initial cause of the disruption.

## Four hours on the ground

Friday's failure held departing flights across the country for more than four hours and led to more than 10,000 cancellations. It was the longest nationwide halt to US flight departures since the September 11, 2001, attacks. Pilots reported static and interference before the FAA ordered the ground stop at 3:47 p.m. Eastern.

Federal officials have traced the breakdown to a router installed by L3Harris at a radio transmitter site in El Dorado, Arkansas. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said the device flooded the FAA's communications network, overwhelming other routers and disrupting radio and radar feeds. He said the router was making about 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds. A second site, a radar facility in Amarillo, Texas, was also identified as a source of excess network traffic.

In all, the failure affected communications and radar feeds at 17 of the FAA's 22 en route air-traffic control centres.

## Defect or configuration?

Investigators have not yet determined whether the router was defective or improperly configured. Hewlett Packard Enterprise, the parent company of Juniper Networks, said its equipment was working as configured. L3Harris says it is cooperating, and the FBI said on Tuesday it was working with the Department of Transportation, the FAA and L3Harris. Duffy said investigators had found no evidence of a cyberattack or foreign interference.

The pause follows a letter on Tuesday from 13 Democratic senators on the Senate Commerce Committee, led by Maria Cantwell, asking the FAA to halt its plan to replace thousands of routers until the investigation is complete. The senators questioned whether the equipment had been adequately tested and why backup systems failed. "Modernization cannot come at the expense of operational safety," they wrote.

The scale of the programme underlines what is now on hold: L3Harris plans to convert 3,648 routers to Juniper devices, with 400 installed so far and 69 operational. More than 60 had been installed since July without major problems before Friday's daytime installation triggered disruption shortly after 3 p.m. Eastern.

Duffy is separately seeking $50 billion from Congress to overhaul the nation's air-traffic control system, a request President Donald Trump has also backed. For now, the FAA says it is assessing the configuration and performance of all the infrastructure involved.
