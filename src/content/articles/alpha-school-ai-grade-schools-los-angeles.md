A new kind of private school has opened in Los Angeles, where artificial intelligence — not a teacher — delivers the core lessons. Alpha School launched its first classes this year in Santa Monica, Playa Vista and Malibu, charging more than $75,000 a year, with no homework and no traditional teachers in the room.

The school's model compresses the academic day. Pupils spend about two hours working silently through personalised lessons on Alpha's software, called TimeBack, which adapts to each child's level and requires a score above 90 percent before they can move on. Alpha says those two hours cover what a conventional school takes six hours to teach. The rest of the day is spent on projects, field trips, coding, sport and practical workshops — building bookshelves, designing toy prototypes on 3D printers, even surfing.

The adults supervising are called guides, not teachers, and often have no formal teaching background. A University of Colorado policy centre estimated that about a third of Alpha's guides have education training or degrees. The lessons draw on AI systems from companies including Anthropic and OpenAI, although pupils do not chat directly with tools such as Claude or ChatGPT.

Alpha was founded in Austin, Texas, in 2014, and its biggest investor, tech entrepreneur Joe Liemandt, committed $1 billion to the school in 2022. The network has grown from 20 campuses to 50 this year, nine of them in California, and Alpha says its pupils learn twice as much as their peers in mainstream schools.

Education experts urge caution. They say the approach is experimental, that young children need a trained adult in the room, and that there has not yet been enough independent testing to know whether it works or is safe. "So much of learning happens collaboratively and in conversation with humans," said Anne Blackstock-Bernstein, a lecturer at UCLA. "Until we have more time with this sort of technology, it's going to be hard to know exactly what is lost when you do that with a machine." Morgan Polikoff, an associate professor at the University of Southern California, said Alpha should open itself up to rigorous external research. Alpha says it has given independent researchers access to its student data, though that research is still in its early stages.

The contrast with public schools is sharp. The Los Angeles Unified School District has placed a moratorium on generative AI on school-issued devices for this school year, and other Southern California districts bar younger pupils from consumer AI tools altogether. In the long term, Alpha hopes to bring its system into public schools, and it is planning further openings in Southern California, including Beverly Hills.

Two months into the school year, the parents who enrolled their children say they are happier — and free of homework. Whether a machine can replace a teacher at scale remains an open question, and one that educators across the country will be watching.
