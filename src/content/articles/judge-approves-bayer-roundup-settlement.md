A Missouri judge has approved Bayer's $7.25 billion settlement of lawsuits alleging that its weedkiller Roundup causes cancer, a decision that could finally resolve litigation that has weighed on the company for a decade.

St. Louis Circuit Judge Timothy Boyer granted final approval on Friday, finding the nationwide class settlement "fair, reasonable, and adequate" and describing the total as among the largest in the history of mass-tort personal-injury class actions. The order is subject to appeal.

The deal covers people who say they developed non-Hodgkin lymphoma, a form of cancer, after exposure to Roundup. Individual payouts will range from $10,000 to $165,000, depending on the severity of the illness, the age at diagnosis, and whether the exposure happened at home or at work. It applies both to people who are already ill and to those diagnosed in the future: the settlement covers exposure before February 17, 2026, and a second group of people diagnosed within 16 years after final approval. Monsanto, Bayer's subsidiary, will fund it through declining capped annual payments over up to 21 years, totalling up to $7.25 billion including money already paid.

About 65,000 claims are pending in US state and federal courts from people who say they developed cancer after using Roundup. The settlement, announced in February, aims to resolve nearly all current and future claims in the United States. Around 200 cases consolidated in an ongoing federal proceeding are excluded, and Bayer says a recent Supreme Court ruling gives it strong defences in those.

The litigation has shadowed Bayer since its 2018 purchase of Monsanto, costing the company billions of dollars and weighing on its share price. Bayer spent about $10 billion in 2020 to resolve most of the lawsuits then outstanding, but it could not win court approval for a parallel $1.25 billion deal covering future claims — the gap this new settlement is designed to close.

The legal ground shifted in Bayer's favour this year. In June, the US Supreme Court ruled 7-2 in the Durnell case that federal pesticide law pre-empts state "failure-to-warn" claims, undercutting a central argument in many of the lawsuits. Judge Boyer cited that ruling in finding the settlement adequate, and in October a Missouri jury sided with Bayer in the first Roundup verdict since the decision.

Bayer continues to deny the claims, saying decades of studies show glyphosate, Roundup's key ingredient, is safe and does not cause cancer. Lawyers for plaintiffs who support the deal say it ensures people with cancer will actually be paid at a time when the litigation has become more uncertain than ever. Opponents argued it does not offer enough money and said it would trap current and future claimants who would rather pursue their cases in court.

Bayer chief executive Bill Anderson called the approval "a decisive milestone in the company's strategy to mitigate the risks of both pending and future Roundup litigation." If the approval survives appeal, the long-running claims programme will begin paying out to tens of thousands of people — and bring the most expensive chapter in Bayer's history closer to an end.
