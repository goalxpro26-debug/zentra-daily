A nutrient the body already makes — and that most people get from protein-rich food — may play a surprisingly direct role in whether the immune system notices cancer cells and viruses. New research from Rockefeller University, published in the journal *Cell*, traces the connection to a single amino acid: arginine.

When arginine runs low, the study found, cells struggle to produce a protein called MHC-I, which acts as a display window on the cell surface. MHC-I shows fragments of what is happening inside a cell to passing T cells, the immune system's scouts. If a cell has turned cancerous or been infected by a virus, those fragments are the signal that triggers a response. Without enough arginine, the signal fades — and abnormal cells can slip past unnoticed.

## A stalled assembly line

The researchers, led by Sohail Tavazoie and first author Qiushuang Wu, traced the breakdown to the cell's protein-building machinery. MHC-I is rich in arginine, and when the amino acid is scarce, the ribosomes that assemble proteins stall partway through making it. In cell cultures deprived of arginine, 414 proteins fell to unusually low levels — but the most striking effect involved the three genes that produce MHC-I.

The team had already shown in 2023 that starving colon cancer cells of arginine caused them to accumulate more mutations. The new work suggests the deficiency also weakens the immune system's side of the equation. Arginine was the most depleted amino acid across the disease models the team examined: colon cancer, influenza, and SARS-CoV-2.

## Fewer tumors, milder infections — in mice

The findings held up in animal studies. Mice fed a low-arginine diet developed more colon tumors; mice given more arginine developed fewer. In models of influenza and SARS-CoV-2, an arginine-rich diet meant milder symptoms — and giving mice arginine after a flu infection had already started improved their outcomes, a result the researchers said they had not expected.

A moderate dose, roughly equivalent to a couple of over-the-counter tablets, was enough in laboratory experiments to restore the genes involved in MHC-I production. The researchers say that makes arginine a realistic candidate for testing alongside immunotherapy, or for high-risk groups exposed to viral infections.

## Why age may matter

The results may also help explain a familiar pattern: arginine levels naturally decline with age, and older people are more vulnerable to certain cancers and respiratory viruses. The researchers suggest that falling arginine could be one reason the immune system grows less sharp at recognizing abnormal cells as people get older — a "perfect storm," in Tavazoie's words, for the initiation of colon cancer.

One important caution: the evidence so far comes from laboratory and animal studies, not from people. Human trials would still be needed before anyone could say arginine supplements prevent cancer or treat viral infections. But because arginine is inexpensive and widely available, the researchers hope those studies can begin soon.
