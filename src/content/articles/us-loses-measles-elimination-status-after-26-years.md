The United States has lost its measles elimination status, an independent panel of experts has determined, ending a public health milestone the country has held since 2000.

The commission, which reports to the director of the Pan American Health Organization, concluded that the United States no longer qualifies as having eliminated measles, a source close to the process told CNN on Friday. The news was first reported by The Wall Street Journal. The decision had been widely expected after years of falling vaccination rates and surging case counts.

Measles was declared eliminated in the United States in 2000, meaning the virus was no longer spreading continuously within the country's borders. Outbreaks still occurred, usually started by travellers bringing the virus in from abroad, but high vaccination rates kept them small and short-lived. That changed in 2025 and 2026, when the virus found more unvaccinated families and communities to spread through.

## The evidence behind the decision

The committee considers an infection endemic when the same strain is passed from person to person for at least a year within a country. A key piece of evidence came from Utah, where scientists found the same strain had circulated for more than a year, accounting for more than 95 percent of the state's cases and causing more than 600 cases between June 2025 and June 2026. Sustained outbreaks have also been reported in South Carolina, Pennsylvania, Wisconsin and New York.

More than 5,400 measles cases have been reported in the United States this year — the highest number since 1991 — most of them linked to 43 new outbreaks. Nearly all have been in people who had not received the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine or whose vaccination status is unknown. Roughly one in nine reported cases has led to hospitalisation, according to the CDC, and at least 10 people have died, according to state reports.

## What losing the status means

"Measles elimination is a vital sign for our public health system," said Dr. Demetre Daskalakis, former director of the National Center for Immunization and Respiratory Diseases at the CDC. "Losing elimination status means that we are less prepared as a nation to address infectious and noninfectious disease challenges."

The consequences fall hardest on people who cannot be vaccinated — infants too young for the shot and people with weakened immune systems — who now face a higher risk in outbreak areas than they did two years ago. The MMR vaccine remains highly effective: two doses cut an exposed person's chances of falling ill by 97 percent compared with someone unvaccinated.

## The road back

The United States can regain its elimination status, but only after showing that outbreak transmission has been interrupted for at least 12 months. Experts say that will not be easy, pointing to diminished public trust in vaccines, rising nonmedical exemptions in schools and declining MMR coverage among kindergarten students.

The United States is not alone in slipping backwards. Canada lost its elimination status last year after a lengthy outbreak, and Mexico, which has had several large outbreaks of its own, is also in danger of losing the designation.
