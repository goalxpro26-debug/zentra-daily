November's full moon will rise on Tuesday, November 24, opening a run of four supermoons in a row that stretches into February 2027.

The full Beaver Moon reaches its fullest point at 9:53 a.m. EST on November 24, and it will look full on the nights of November 23 and 24. It is the first supermoon since January, and the second of 2026. Look for it climbing in the east around sunset, glowing near the Pleiades star cluster and the bright orange star Aldebaran in the constellation Taurus.

A supermoon happens when a full moon lines up with perigee, the point in the moon's orbit where it comes closest to Earth. November's perigee falls about 30 hours after the full moon, on November 25, close enough for the moon to appear up to about 14 percent wider and 30 percent brighter than an average full moon. The difference in size is hard to spot with the naked eye, but the extra brightness is easier to notice, especially as the moon rises near the horizon.

The streak continues with the Cold Moon in December, which will be the closest full moon of 2026 at roughly 221,700 miles from Earth. It reaches peak illumination at 8:28 p.m. EST on December 23. The Wolf Moon follows on January 22, 2027, reaching its full phase at 7:17 a.m. EST, and the Snow Moon completes the run on February 20, 2027, at 6:23 p.m. EST.

Three or four supermoons occur most years, and they always arrive in consecutive months, because the full moon drifts gradually closer to perigee and then away again over several lunar cycles.

November's full moon is traditionally called the Beaver Moon, a name tied to the time of year when beavers prepare for winter and trappers once set their traps before the waters froze. No telescope or special equipment is needed to watch it — a clear sky and a view toward the eastern horizon at sunset are enough, and the moon stays visible all night.
