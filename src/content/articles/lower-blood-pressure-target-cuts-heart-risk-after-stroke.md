Pushing blood pressure lower than the usual target cut the risk of further heart attacks, strokes and deaths in people who had already had a stroke, according to a large trial presented on November 7 at the American Heart Association's Scientific Sessions 2026 in Chicago and published the same day in the New England Journal of Medicine.

The OPTIMAL-STROKE trial enrolled 4,368 adults at 30 centres in Brazil between 2019 and 2022. All had previously had an ischemic stroke or a transient ischemic attack — a brief, stroke-like episode — and had systolic blood pressure, the top number in a reading, between 130 and 180. Half were randomly assigned to intensive treatment aiming for a systolic reading below 120; the others received standard treatment targeting below 140.

Over a median follow-up of 3.9 years, the intensive group averaged a systolic pressure of 122, against 135 in the standard group — a gap of about 13 points. Major cardiovascular events, a composite of death from cardiovascular causes, nonfatal heart attack or stroke, or hospital admission for unstable angina or heart failure, occurred in 10.9 percent of the intensive group and 13.5 percent of the standard group, a relative reduction of about 21 percent. Strokes alone fell from 6.4 percent to 4.7 percent.

The question had been open because earlier intensive-control trials, including the landmark SPRINT study, excluded people who had already had a stroke. Current American Heart Association guidance recommends systolic pressure below 130, and below 120 for people at higher cardiovascular risk; the new results give that lower target direct evidence in stroke survivors for the first time.

The benefit came with cautions. Kidney function declined more often with intensive treatment — the composite renal outcome occurred in 3.9 percent of the intensive group versus 2.2 percent — and low blood sodium was more common. Serious adverse events overall were similar between the groups, and injurious falls were slightly less frequent with intensive treatment. The researchers said tighter control needs closer monitoring, particularly of kidney function.

## What is still unknown

A twin trial in people with diabetes, OPTIMAL-DIABETES, found no significant difference between the two targets, so the benefit is not universal. Experts also noted the intensive group averaged 122 rather than actually reaching below 120, so it is not yet clear whether the specific number matters or simply getting pressure lower. Guidelines may revisit the post-stroke target, but for now the decision belongs with a patient's own doctor.
