The Solomon Islands has signed a sweeping treaty with Australia that names Canberra its primary security partner and declares that no foreign military or security base will ever be established on its soil.

Prime Minister Matthew Wale and his Australian counterpart, Anthony Albanese, signed the agreement in Canberra on Wednesday. In a joint statement, the two leaders said the treaty "reflects Solomon Islands' sovereign choice of Australia as its primary security partner" and confirms that the archipelago, about 2,000 kilometres northeast of Australia, will remain free of foreign bases.

The signing follows reports that Honiara plans to end the secretive security pact it struck with China in 2022, which brought Chinese police trainers to the capital and raised fears in Canberra and Washington of a Chinese naval foothold in the South Pacific. Wale would neither confirm nor deny those reports at the press conference, saying the Chinese agreement contains a non-disclosure clause. "We have ongoing discussions with China," he said.

Under the new treaty, Australia will support the expansion and development of the Solomon Islands police and border force, and provide funding for education, health, infrastructure, energy and climate resilience. Honiara has also agreed to consult Canberra before striking deals on critical infrastructure — including airports, ports, energy and telecommunications — with countries outside the Pacific Islands Forum.

The agreement marks a sharp turn in the island nation's foreign policy. Wale, who became prime minister in May, has sought to reset relations with Australia, which frayed under his predecessor Manasseh Sogavare, while maintaining diplomatic and trade ties with Beijing. Sogavare signed the 2022 pact with China, which was never officially published; a leaked draft showed it could be extended in five-year blocks unless either side gave six months' notice to end it.

For Australia, the treaty is the latest in a run of Pacific security deals, following similar agreements with Papua New Guinea, Vanuatu and Fiji. Canberra has spent four years trying to counter Beijing's growing influence in the region, and the Solomons deal — coupled with signs the China pact will be allowed to lapse — is seen by analysts as its most significant win yet.

The Solomon Islands parliament is expected to return in January to approve reforms tied to the treaty, including a pledge of free education. What remains unclear is how Beijing will respond, and whether the Solomons' parliament — where some members have close ties to China — will back the shift without a fight.
