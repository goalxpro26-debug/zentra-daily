After eight years in space, the European-Japanese BepiColombo mission has arrived at Mercury. The European Space Agency (ESA) announced on 21 November that the spacecraft had been captured by the planet's gravity, making it only the second mission ever to enter orbit around the Solar System's innermost planet, after NASA's MESSENGER, which circled Mercury from 2011 to 2015.

The arrival closes a journey that began with a launch on an Ariane 5 rocket from Kourou in French Guiana on 20 October 2018. Since then BepiColombo has travelled roughly 9.9 billion kilometres, using nine planetary flybys — one of Earth, two of Venus and six of Mercury itself — to shed speed. Mercury is a difficult destination: falling towards the Sun, a spacecraft must lose a great deal of energy before the planet's weak gravity can capture it, and the trip takes more energy than reaching the outer Solar System.

The mission nearly missed its window. In April 2024 engineers found that the transfer module's electric thrusters could not deliver full power, and ESA recalculated the route, pushing arrival back by almost a year from its original December 2025 date. The Mercury Transfer Module, which had powered and steered the spacecraft for most of the journey, separated from the two orbiters on 3 September 2026, more than 200 million kilometres from Earth.

For now the two orbiters remain joined together as one stacked spacecraft in an initial elliptical orbit of about 590 by 11,640 kilometres. They are due to separate on 9–10 December, when the mission becomes the first ever to fly two spacecraft around Mercury at the same time. ESA's Mercury Planetary Orbiter (MPO) will gradually descend to a close polar orbit between 400 and 1,500 kilometres above the surface by March 2027, mapping the planet and studying its composition and interior. JAXA's Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter, known as Mio, will trace wider ellipses between 500 and 11,000 kilometres, sampling the magnetic field and how it interacts with the solar wind.

The long cruise was not wasted. En route, mission scientists tested the onboard instruments, pointing the Mercury Imaging X-ray Spectrometer (MIXS) — the first X-ray imaging device sent on an interplanetary mission — at the Crab Nebula, the remnant of a supernova seen from Earth in 1054.

Mercury has long been the Solar System's oddity: a small, scorched world that nonetheless holds water ice in permanently shadowed polar craters and generates a magnetic field despite its size. MESSENGER showed its surface hides unexpected geological activity. With two orbiters working together, scientists hope to separate Mercury's own magnetic field from the short-lived fluctuations caused by solar wind and flares — a distinction a single spacecraft could not make cleanly — and to learn how a planet so close to its star formed. That matters beyond Mercury: most exoplanets found so far orbit very close to their suns.

Full science operations begin in April 2027, after months of manoeuvres to settle both orbiters into their working paths.
