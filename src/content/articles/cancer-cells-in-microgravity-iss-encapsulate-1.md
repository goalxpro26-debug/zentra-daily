A photograph taken aboard the International Space Station shows clusters of human stomach cancer cells growing in three dimensions — something they rarely do in a laboratory dish on Earth. NASA published the image as its Image of the Day on November 23, 2026. It was captured on November 19 inside a miniaturised biochip, part of an experiment called Encapsulate-1.

The experiment studies tumour samples taken from patients, grown aboard the station to learn how cancer cells move and interact when gravity is effectively switched off. On Earth, cells in a dish flatten against the surface and behave differently from how they behave inside the body. In microgravity they float, clump together and form compact, three-dimensional structures that resemble real tumours far more closely. That gives researchers a clearer window into the process they most want to understand: metastasis, the spread of cancer from its original site to other parts of the body.

That is the experiment's goal, in NASA's own framing. The results could help researchers develop further testing to predict a patient's risk of metastasis and support more personalised cancer treatments. It is early-stage research, not a treatment: no drug has come out of this experiment, and the samples must return to Earth and be analysed before any conclusions can be drawn. What the image confirms so far is that the biochip works as designed and the cells are alive, growing and forming the structures the team hoped to see.

The work is a joint effort between NASA and Encapsulate Inc., a Connecticut-based medical startup that builds the biochips. The project received a $3.63 million grant last year through NASA's In Space Production Applications programme, which funds commercial research that can only be done in orbit. "This grant represents a bold step forward in how we approach cancer research," Encapsulate co-founder and chief executive Dr. Armin Rad said when the grant was announced.

The station, orbiting more than 200 miles above Earth, has hosted more than 4,000 experiments since it launched nearly three decades ago, and its biomedical workload keeps growing. A resupply mission launching on November 29 is set to carry further cancer research, including tests of a new class of antibody-drug conjugates on patient-derived tumour organoids, alongside studies of muscle loss and lab-grown liver tissue.

For now, Encapsulate-1 remains a study of behaviour, not a cure. But if watching cancer cells in weightlessness teaches researchers to predict which tumours will spread — and which will not — the payoff could reach far beyond the station: treatment decisions tuned to each patient's actual risk, rather than to averages.
