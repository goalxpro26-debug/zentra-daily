Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei has said it would be "a bad idea" for humanity to hand the job of governing society to artificial intelligence, rejecting the suggestion that he believes people should surrender their free will to machines.

Amodei made the argument in a lengthy post on X on Wednesday, responding to an Axios report that described a growing divide among leaders of the leading AI labs over how much authority superintelligent systems should be given. The report, citing people familiar with internal discussions, said some Anthropic employees believed humanity would safely hand authority to superintelligent systems — and often should — rather than fight to keep control.

"We should never sacrifice legitimacy or self-determination on the altar of efficiency or security," Amodei wrote. "This argument against autocracy applies word for word to governance by AI, and I feel just as strongly that handing over governance to AI is a bad idea."

He called it "absurd" to suggest he held the opposite view, pointing to hundreds of pages of essays in which he has argued that AI must be governed through legitimate democratic processes. Political decisions, he said, should stay in human hands, and he rejected handing power to AI as firmly as he rejected autocracy.

Amodei drew a sharp line between governing and doing. Letting AI systems take on specific, complex tasks — driving a car, designing a cancer drug, writing software end to end — is a different matter from giving up free will in politics, he argued. That kind of automation is "clearly desirable in many cases and will save a lot of lives," he wrote, but it also has downsides, and in some areas it may not be worth it. In many cases, he added, people would simply prefer to do some activities themselves, "so they can stay deeply engaged in what matters to them, even if AI can do those activities better."

He also set out where he believes the real safety line falls: recursive self-improvement, or AI systems building successive generations of frontier models without meaningful human oversight. Anthropic does not consider it safe for its Claude models to do that fully autonomously, he said, and safety research must keep pace with improvements in AI capabilities.

The post comes as the industry's leaders argue openly about the future. Meta's chief AI officer, Alexandr Wang, has separately written that society should not accept a "handover" to AI. Amodei, meanwhile, criticised rival executives who repeat slogans about "human control and agency" while shipping products built for more and more automation.

Amodei also pointed to Anthropic's own record as evidence of where it draws lines. The company refused to allow its existing models to be used for autonomous weapons during its dispute with the Pentagon, he wrote — a refusal he said he was willing to risk Anthropic's future over, "even in the face of legal sanctions."

The debate is not abstract for Anthropic. The company went public on Nasdaq on November 19 in what has been described as the largest initial public offering in history, putting its stance on AI governance under sharper scrutiny from investors and policymakers alike. Amodei's message was that the company's position has not changed: AI can take on more of the work, but the decisions about how society is run are not its to make.
