Google said on November 3, 2026, that it will switch off Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom and Google Search for K-12 student accounts until a school administrator turns them on. The announcement, made by Chris Phillips, Google's vice-president and general manager of education, reverses an earlier decision — and it has sparked a debate about how students should learn with AI, and who gets to decide.

In August 2026, Google announced that AI features in its Classroom app for K-12 schools would be turned on by default, giving students access to the Gemini chatbot. Schools told Google they wanted control, and that they were concerned about students using AI to shortcut schoolwork. Google has now reversed course: access will be entirely turned off by default for K-12 student accounts until school administrators explicitly turn it on. Google says it will share more specifics with schools in the coming months, before the change takes effect.

Schools can now pick each service on its own. If they pick Search, its AI answers come with it. Google posted a clarification the same day confirming that schools can turn on one, two or all of the services, each on its own. The school decides now. Once the change rolls out, a district that does nothing gets none of the four services for its students — Search included.

## The backlash: Search is the sticking point

The pilot of Google's newly launched AI learning tools in US schools is drawing online backlash, because K-12 students in schools that opt out of AI features entirely may also lose access to Google Search.

The problem is that Search is, in Google's own description, deeply integrated with AI. If a school banned AI, students "will not have access to search, and they'll see a message from their school that indicates it's not available," Phillips was quoted as saying by The New York Times. Schools appear to have no way to disable AI features built directly into Google Search, such as AI Mode, AI Overviews and the new Guided Learning feature — they can only choose whether Search is on or off as a whole.

That has put schools in a difficult position. A school that wants to keep students away from AI chatbots may find it has also taken away the search engine its students use for ordinary research. A school that keeps Search on gets the AI features with it, whether it wants them or not. It is a trade-off that many educators and parents did not expect, and it is the heart of the backlash.

The context matters. In September 2026, school districts in New York City and Los Angeles broadly restricted student access to generative AI on school devices. The demand for control is real, and it is coming from the largest school systems in the country. Google's announcement is, in part, a response to that demand — but the Search complication shows how hard it is to separate AI from the tools schools already rely on.

## Guided Learning: the coach, not the answer key

At the centre of Google's announcement is Guided Learning, a learning mode in the Gemini app. Instead of giving the answer to a problem outright, it leads students toward understanding step by step, with hints and questions. It is Google's answer to the shortcut worry: an AI that coaches rather than completes.

Guided Learning began rolling out in Google Classroom worldwide on November 3. It is also coming to Google Search, where it will help students think through homework problems step by step. Google says it will start as a pilot at select US schools later in November, before reaching other districts.

In US cities where AI has not been banned from schools, Google has said it is giving two options for students to use Search from their school-issued accounts. One option is Search with AI Overviews turned on, similar to the current consumer experience. The other is Search via the Guided Learning tool, which walks students through problems instead of giving quick answers to schoolwork.

Google also published results from two studies of Guided Learning. In a randomised controlled trial with more than 300 University of Tokyo students, after a half-day of self-study, students who used only standard online materials raised their scores by 5.3 points, while students who could also use Guided Learning raised theirs by 8.1 points. Google says this amounts to about 1.5 times the gain from a single study session. In the UK, working with the school network Ark, Google tested Guided Learning in Google Classroom at three secondary schools for four weeks across four subjects. These results were published by Google itself and have not been released as a research paper, so they should be read as the company's own evidence, not independent findings.

## What schools must decide

Every school with K-12 accounts will now have to decide, service by service, whether students get Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom and Search. It is a decision that cannot be avoided by doing nothing: doing nothing means all four are off.

For students, the change is a reminder that the tools they use for learning are not neutral, and that the adults around them are still working out the rules. For teachers, it is a new administrative task layered on top of the old one — deciding not just what to teach, but which tools students may use to learn it. And for Google, it is a test of whether an AI company can give schools genuine control without taking away the basic tools of research at the same time.

The Search pilot begins in select US schools later in November 2026. How schools respond — and how students experience the change — will shape the next round of decisions, from Google and from school districts alike.
