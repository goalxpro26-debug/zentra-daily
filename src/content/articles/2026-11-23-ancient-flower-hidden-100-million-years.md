A flower no bigger than a grain of rice lay hidden inside a museum rock for more than 130 years. When scientists finally saw it, they found it was about 100 million years old — the oldest palm flower ever discovered, and the oldest flower yet found showing the pattern shared by palms, lilies and orchids.

The flower, named Tomlinsoniella pedicellata, measures about 1.5 millimetres high and 1.2 millimetres across. It dates from the mid-Cretaceous period, when dinosaurs still walked the Earth. Its description was published in the American Journal of Botany.

The discovery was an accident. Paleobotanist Steven Manchester, of the Florida Museum of Natural History, had borrowed a sandstone specimen from the Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History to examine a different fossil flower on its surface, known as Dakotanthus. He was scanning the rock with micro-CT — an X-ray technique that builds a three-dimensional picture of the inside of a sample — when he noticed a second floral shape completely entombed within it.

The specimen came from the Dakota Formation in Kansas, a geological unit famous for the plant fossils in its sandstones. It was collected in the 1870s to 1890s by Leo Lesquereux, a pioneer of American fossil-plant research. Nothing on the surface of the stone had given the hidden flower away.

The flower itself is long gone. After it fell into clay-rich sand, the clay hardened around it and the plant tissue decayed, leaving a cavity that faithfully replicated its shape — a fossil preserved in negative. Manchester and his colleagues scanned that void, built a three-dimensional model of it, and then inverted the model, turning the empty space into a solid object so the flower could be examined as though it had been picked yesterday.

What the scan revealed was a textbook monocot flower: three sepals, three petals, six stamens in two sets of three, and three carpels — every part repeated in threes, arranged in five concentric whorls. "Lots of monocots have gone on to evolve more complicated variants on this, but this exhibits the features of the basic monocot flower," said lead author Bruce Tiffney, a professor emeritus of paleobotany at UC Santa Barbara. "And we've got it a hundred million years ago."

The team's analysis found the flower fits most closely with the coryphoid palms, the lineage that includes fan palms. That makes it not only the oldest discovered palm flower, but the oldest flower showing the characteristic pattern of the entire monocot group — a group that today includes grasses, orchids, lilies and palms, and covers much of the plant life on Earth.

The authors are careful about what one tiny fossil can prove. More work is needed to place the flower precisely on the plant family tree, and a single specimen cannot settle how early monocots diversified. But as a window into the early history of flowering plants, a bloom that waited a century in a drawer to be noticed is a remarkable one.
