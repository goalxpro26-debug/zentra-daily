Chinese stocks jumped on Friday, heading for their sharpest one-day rise in more than two years, after Beijing promised fresh policies to steady capital markets and a bigger role for the state-backed buyers known as the "national team."

The rally began in afternoon trading, shortly after the official Xinhua news agency reported that China's cabinet had pledged new incremental measures for the capital markets, including a stronger stabilisation role for state-backed investors. Listed companies will also be pushed to raise dividends and buy back more of their own shares, and the cabinet said it would strengthen defences against risks to the markets coming from abroad.

The benchmark Shanghai Composite index, which had been languishing around 17-month lows, shot up about 3.3 percent in afternoon trade. Technology and artificial-intelligence shares leapt further: the STAR semiconductor index jumped 15 percent, Hong Kong's technology index surged 6 percent, and the yuan rallied.

The move was striking for how directly it came from the top. It is very rare for an executive meeting of the State Council, China's cabinet, to comment so pointedly on the stock market — the last time it did so was in 2024, when it rolled out a liquidity support package. The signal landed with investors who had endured months of grinding losses; analysts described the market as being in a technical bear market before Friday's rebound.

"It's a shot in the arm for markets. Sentiment has been downbeat for so long," Kenny Ng, a strategist at China Everbright Securities International in Hong Kong, told Reuters. "At least now there's something to count on: the national team will come to the rescue if things really turn south."

Others were more cautious. Kenneth Goh of UOB Kay Hian in Singapore said the intervention was positive in the short term, but its staying power would depend on consumer demand and confidence, as well as on trade barriers and restrictions. Charu Chanana, chief investment strategist at Saxo, drew a similar line: there is a difference, she said, between authorities putting a floor under the market and creating the conditions for a sustained bull market. "A policy-induced bounce needs to evolve into a fundamentally driven recovery before we can confidently call it a durable bottom," she said.

Jason Chan, a strategist at the Bank of East Asia in Hong Kong, said Beijing's determination to halt the slide was clear, but warned that the money alone would not be enough. Practical support policies — for the economy and for specific industries — would have to follow, he said, "instead of just paying lip service."

For now, the market has its answer to months of decline: Beijing is watching, and it is willing to intervene. Whether Friday's surge becomes the start of a recovery or a brief interruption in a longer slide will depend on what the cabinet does next — and on whether the economy behind the market starts to give investors something else to trade on.
