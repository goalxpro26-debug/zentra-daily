A landmark study that tracks artificial intelligence every five years has concluded that AI has gone mainstream — and that the public has had almost no say in how it got there.

The One Hundred Year Study on Artificial Intelligence, known as AI100, released its third report on Tuesday, titled "AI Goes Mainstream: The Scaling Era and the Rise of Generative AI." Administered by Stanford's Institute for Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence and led by an international panel chaired by Oxford professor Michael Wooldridge, the report assesses AI's development and societal impact across technical, ethical, economic and governance questions.

Scaling — building systems with ever more data and computing power — powered the chief advances of the last five years, the panel found. But that approach is now running into data and compute constraints, pushing the frontier toward reasoning and inference-time computing instead.

The report compares AI's disruption to that of the World Wide Web. Creative industries, mathematics and software development — fields that believed themselves beyond its reach — are already being transformed, joining journalism, music and retail among the sectors upended by technology over the past three decades. Science is moving fastest of all: AI is compressing research timescales from years to months or weeks, a shift recognised by the 2024 Nobel Prizes in physics and chemistry.

## Concentration, conflict and trust

The panel's warnings are as striking as its account of progress. Capital investment in AI infrastructure is unprecedented in scale and speed, propping up parts of the US economy and dominating capital markets — while fuelling fears of a financial bubble and straining energy and freshwater supplies. Frontier AI development is concentrated among a small number of US and Chinese actors, raising questions about who will ultimately own and have access to leading-edge systems.

Military AI, the report says, has gone from a hypothetical point of speculation to a transformative force in active conflicts. Cybersecurity has emerged as a major area of risk for AI abuse. And AI-generated imagery, audio and video have ended the century-old assumption that evidence based on photographs and recordings is inherently trustworthy.

Governance, meanwhile, is fragmented and increasingly framed in terms of national security, with little international consensus on its direction. Risks once seen as pure science fiction are now emerging, the panel warns, while existing ethical concerns demand ever more attention.

## A watershed, already dating

Wooldridge said that since 2021, powerful general-purpose AI tools had gone "from being a point of speculation to an everyday reality, used by millions of people." Sheila McIlraith, chair of the AI100 Standing Committee and a professor at the University of Toronto, called the last five years "a watershed moment in the history of AI."

The panel's twelfth and final takeaway is a warning about its own shelf life: the pace of development over the past five years was unprecedented, few people predicted where AI would be today even five years ago, and if the current pace is sustained, the next five years should surprise us just as much. The report concedes it will be out of date by the time most people read it.
