The Federal Aviation Administration has named the cause of Friday's nationwide ground stop: a newly installed router at a remote radio site in El Dorado, Arkansas, flooded the agency's telecommunications network with data, destabilising air traffic control centres across the country.

The problem began at about 3:30 p.m. Eastern on Friday, November 13. According to the FAA, its legacy equipment became unstable when data began flooding the FAA Telecommunications Infrastructure network at a rate of 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds. The flood disrupted aircraft communications and radar feeds, and the agency ordered every departing flight in the United States to stay on the ground. Flights resumed at 8:15 p.m. that evening.

The investigation points to two locations. The first is the radio communications site in El Dorado, where contractor L3Harris had been working, including deploying a new router as part of a long-running switch from ageing copper telephone lines to fibre-optic cable. The second is a radar site in Amarillo, Texas. Once L3Harris blocked both locations, the network stabilised, the FAA said.

The scale of the disruption was striking for a fault that began at a single rural site. Instability spread to 17 of the FAA's 22 Air Route Traffic Control Centers, which manage high-altitude traffic, and into several terminal radar facilities. About 3,500 aircraft were in the air when the stop was ordered; all landed safely, the agency said. Operators cancelled roughly 12,462 flights on Friday, including 9,082 by major airlines, as the backlog rippled through the weekend.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said on Sunday that the routers being flooded were bought in 2001 and had been obsolete since 2006, yet were still in service in 2026. The FAA said the failure came from its legacy time-division multiplexed system, not from its broader modernisation programme, and stressed that none of the upgraded towers had gone down.

## No sign of an attack

The FAA said there is no indication the outage was a cyberattack or the work of an outside actor, and that it was unrelated to SMART, the agency's new AI-enhanced software tool, which only aggregates data and does not feed into air traffic control.

As a precaution, the agency has disabled the broadcasting function on all the new routers, and on about 60 similar routers elsewhere in the system. The suspect Arkansas router is being examined in an L3Harris laboratory with FAA participation. The FAA and L3Harris are continuing to investigate the root cause, and the agency said it will re-evaluate when routine replacement work is carried out, after this one was done in the middle of a busy Friday afternoon.

The episode has sharpened questions about the state of the country's air traffic infrastructure. The copper lines being replaced are so old that the telephone companies are discontinuing them, and the system they support still carries the communications and radar data for the world's busiest airspace. Friday showed how quickly a fault in one small corner of that network can ground the whole country.
