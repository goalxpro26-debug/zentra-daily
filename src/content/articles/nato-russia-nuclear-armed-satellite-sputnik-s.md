NATO members' intelligence services have concluded that Russia plans to launch a satellite carrying a nuclear weapon into space this year, German media reported on Monday.

The report, by broadcasters NDR and WDR and the newspaper Süddeutsche Zeitung, said Western intelligence services — including Germany's foreign intelligence agency, the BND, and the German armed forces — had shared assessments showing the satellite, named Sputnik-S, was assembled several years ago and is stationed in the Moscow region.

Russia's embassy in Germany, the BND and NATO headquarters in Brussels did not immediately respond to requests for comment, Reuters reported.

Asked about the reports on Monday, NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte said the alliance was constantly assessing its deterrence and defence. "I can assure you, we will always make sure that we defend every inch of NATO territory. And that includes making sure that we are safe when it comes to the space element," Rutte told reporters at a joint news conference with German Chancellor Friedrich Merz.

Rutte said NATO had a variety of response options, even if the public did not always see them. "When it comes to the military domain, we will always make assessments of our deterrence and defence and make updates where necessary," he said.

The reports revive a concern that first surfaced publicly in 2024, when US officials said Russia was developing a weapon that could destroy enemy satellites by setting off a nuclear explosion in space. Russia denied the assertion at the time.

US officials have alleged for years that Moscow has been working on a nuclear weapon capable of destroying entire satellite networks, including SpaceX's Starlink internet system, which Ukrainian troops have relied on during the war.

A weapon of that kind would threaten the satellite networks that carry civilian communications, navigation and military data, which is why the reports drew a swift response from NATO's leadership. But much remains unconfirmed: no launch date has been made public, and the assessment rests on intelligence shared among allies rather than any announcement from Moscow.

For now, the alliance's public message is one of readiness rather than alarm: it is watching, it says, and it has options.
