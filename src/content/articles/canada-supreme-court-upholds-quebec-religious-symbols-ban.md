Canada's Supreme Court on Friday largely upheld Quebec's law banning public-sector workers from wearing religious symbols on the job, ending a seven-year legal battle over one of the country's most contested pieces of legislation.

The law, known as Bill 21, was passed by Quebec's National Assembly in June 2019. It bars people in positions of authority — including public school teachers, police officers, prosecutors, judges and school principals — from wearing religious symbols such as hijabs, turbans, kippahs and crosses while at work. The stated aim was to protect the religious neutrality of public institutions.

In a 520-page decision in *English Montreal School Board v. Quebec (Attorney General)*, the court confirmed that the law is valid and in force, apart from one section. Seven justices heard the case over four days in March, with a record 51 intervenors, and produced four sets of reasons.

The central question was Quebec's use of the notwithstanding clause — section 33 of Canada's Charter of Rights and Freedoms, which lets governments keep a law in force even if it violates certain Charter rights, including freedom of religion and equality. Quebec invoked the clause pre-emptively when it passed Bill 21, and renewed it in 2024.

The court found that use of the clause was lawful. Writing for the majority, Chief Justice Richard Wagner said Bill 21 was rooted in Quebec's "legitimate and historical commitment" to keeping the state secular, and that it was not open to the courts to place additional limits on how governments exercise the override power. The court also ruled that the clause can be invoked before a court has decided whether a law violates Charter rights.

At the same time, the court acknowledged the human cost. The decision said the "stigmatizing and deleterious effects" of the law on those most affected — whom it identified as Muslim women — should not be underestimated. The National Council of Canadian Muslims, one of the groups that challenged the law, called the ruling "a dark day in our constitutional democracy."

The justices were divided on several questions. Four of the seven agreed that courts can formally declare that a law violates Charter rights even when the notwithstanding clause keeps it in effect, though no such declaration was issued here. A 4–3 majority rejected the argument that the Charter's gender-equality guarantee could be used to strike the law down.

The one part of the law that fell was section 8, which required members of Quebec's National Assembly to perform their functions with their faces uncovered. The court found that provision violated the democratic right of Canadians to run for office and take part in the legislative process — a right the notwithstanding clause cannot override.

Prime Minister Mark Carney said he respects the court's independence. The ruling settles the law's constitutional status, but the debate it has stirred — over secularism, religious freedom and who bears the cost of the ban — is unlikely to end here.
