The European Space Agency has released the first major collection of data from its Euclid space telescope, covering observations of more than 900 million galaxies as scientists work to understand the dark matter and dark energy that shape the universe.

Unveiled on Thursday, it covers a sweep of sky equal to about 9,500 times the size of the Moon as seen from Earth — around 13 months of observations totalling 1.7 petabytes of data.

Launched from Florida in 2023, Euclid aims to build the largest-ever three-dimensional atlas of the cosmos, spanning more than a third of the sky, by 2031. This haul still covers just 14 percent of the area it is due to survey in visible and near-infrared light over six years.

## Mapping the cosmic web

Euclid does more than photograph galaxies. It also measures their distance, adding a third dimension to the map and placing each galaxy in space and time across 10 billion years. That lets scientists reconstruct the cosmic web — the vast network of filaments along which galaxies cluster, separated by near-empty voids — and track how it has grown.

The web's shape depends on dark matter and dark energy. Ordinary matter makes up only about 5 percent of the universe; dark matter, known only from its gravitational pull, may account for 27 percent, and dark energy, thought to drive the universe's accelerating expansion, 68 percent. Neither can be observed directly.

"It marks a major milestone for ESA's Euclid mission and for everyone involved," said Valeria Pettorino, the agency's Euclid project scientist.

## Lenses that weigh galaxies

One of the sharpest tools in the new data is gravitational lensing. Light from distant galaxies is bent as it passes massive objects in the foreground, and the strength of that bending reveals how much matter a galaxy contains — including the invisible kind.

The release includes about 15,000 strong gravitational lens candidates, more than the total found in the previous half century. Euclid combines breadth with sharpness: ground telescopes cover wide areas but are blurred by the atmosphere, while Hubble and the James Webb telescope image only tiny patches sharply. Euclid does both at once.

Euclid has also pinpointed 60 million quasars — brilliant galactic cores powered by supermassive black holes — including some whose light set off around 13 billion years ago, when the universe was just 5 percent of its current age.

## What comes next

The release will be completed next year, when the first results on dark energy and dark matter are expected.

## What is still unknown

The new data does not yet answer the central questions: what dark matter and dark energy actually are. Those first results are expected next year, and the full survey will not be complete until 2031.
