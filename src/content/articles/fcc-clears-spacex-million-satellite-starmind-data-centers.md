The Federal Communications Commission has given SpaceX permission to deploy up to one million satellites that would work as data centers in orbit, the first authorization of its kind in the United States. The decision, announced on Wednesday, hands Elon Musk's rocket company a regulatory green light for a project scientists say faces enormous technical obstacles.

The network, called Starmind, would be made up of "orbital compute modules" — satellites fitted with computer chips that process data in space for use back on Earth. SpaceX describes the idea as capturing solar energy in orbit to power low-cost, high-performance computing for artificial intelligence. The FCC called the authorization a historic first in the race to build AI infrastructure, saying the constellation would help meet rapidly growing demand for computing power.

FCC Chairman Brendan Carr said the decision "opens a bold new frontier" for the computing infrastructure needed to power American leadership. Musk welcomed the ruling on social media.

The license sets a long schedule: at least one satellite in orbit by November 2033, 100,000 by November 2035, half the constellation by November 2038, and the full one million by 2040. The company's chief financial officer has said it hopes to begin demonstrating the technology in 2027.

About 17,000 satellites currently orbit Earth, including just over 11,000 of SpaceX's own Starlink internet satellites. A full Starmind constellation would multiply that number roughly fiftyfold.

That scale is what worries scientists. Adam Becker, an astrophysicist and author, called the prospect of launching a million satellites to run data centers "a fantasy." "It's not going to happen," he told the Associated Press on Wednesday.

One problem is heat: space is cold, but it is also a vacuum, which traps heat inside objects much like a thermos — and data centers generate enormous amounts of it. Becker said there are "basic physical limits" to radiating that heat away.

Other risks include atmospheric pollution from launches and reentries, and the chance of collisions in orbit. "You could end up with a cascading set of space junk collisions," Becker said, warning of a cloud of debris in low Earth orbit, where many weather and telecommunications satellites also fly.

The American Astronomical Society said it was "disheartened and dismayed" by the decision, warning it would undermine decades of investment in astronomy and the public's ability to enjoy the night sky. The society, satellite operator Viasat, and Amazon's Kuiper Systems were among those that asked the regulator to deny the request. The FCC maintained that the constellation complies with its orbital debris rules, said the deployment serves the public interest, and set aside environmental concerns as outside its authority.

The authorization does not mean the network will be built. SpaceX itself cautioned in a filing ahead of its planned public offering that orbital data centers rely on "unproven" technology and "may not achieve commercial viability." For now, the one-million-satellite data center exists on paper — and on a fourteen-year deadline.
