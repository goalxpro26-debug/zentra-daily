Shareholders have sued Intel, accusing the US chipmaker of hiding how badly its personal computer business was deteriorating before its share price collapsed by almost 29% in a single day.

The proposed class action, filed in San Jose, California, says Intel made statements that were "materially false and misleading" because they failed to disclose the slowdown in PC sales and the impact of its data centre projects. The suit was brought by the law firm Robbins Geller Rudman & Dowd on behalf of owners of Intel common shares between 23 July and 29 October 2026. It names the company, its chief executive Tan Lip-Bu, and other company leaders, and seeks unspecified damages.

At the heart of the complaint is a mismatch between what Intel was telling investors and what its numbers later showed. In October, the company reported that its computer hardware sales had dropped sharply in the three months ended in September, undercutting earlier suggestions that demand was improving. The lawsuit says depressed demand for PCs caused a dramatic slowdown in quarterly earnings — a fall of 9%, or about $800 million, to $8.1 billion.

The market reaction was swift. Intel's shares plunged almost 29% in one day after the October report, wiping billions of dollars off the company's value, after it forecast disappointing revenues ahead. The stock has fallen 37.5% over the past month, although it remains up 51% for the year as a whole.

The case lands at an awkward moment for Intel's turnaround. The company has poured large investments into manufacturing to meet demand for computing power in data centres, as the artificial intelligence boom drives orders for server chips. But as more semiconductors are channelled into data centres, chips for PCs and other electronics have become more expensive, and supply has tightened.

Tan told analysts the industry faced an "unprecedented memory crunch" that was causing a shortage of chip supplies for PCs, adding in October: "We need to address these challenges with urgency."

For investors, the lawsuit turns on timing: whether Intel warned shareholders early enough about a slowdown it should have seen coming, or whether it kept the market in the dark while its share price stayed artificially high. The claims are allegations only and have not been tested in court. The BBC said it had contacted Intel for comment.

The case is the latest reminder that the AI boom has not lifted every chipmaker equally. Companies exposed to the PC market are caught between surging demand for data centre hardware and a consumer market that has cooled — and investors are now asking, in court, who knew what and when.
