The U.S. Army plans to detonate nearly five tons of degraded, unstable explosive at its munitions plant in south-east Iowa on Wednesday, despite a federal appeals court pausing the environmental permit for the operation. The Army told the court it can proceed under a separate emergency authority.

The blast at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown was first scheduled for Friday, November 20. Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle postponed it to Monday, saying a forecast cold snap could further destabilise the material. The Justice Department then told the court the Army intended to act Tuesday, November 24 — before the Army said on Sunday evening the detonation would instead take place Wednesday, November 25, the day before Thanksgiving, "to allow for optimal weather conditions and all mitigation measures to be emplaced." No time was given.

On Thursday night, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit issued an administrative stay of the EPA emergency permit after the Iowa Environmental Council and the Sierra Club's Iowa chapter sued. In a Saturday filing, the government argued the stay only "suspends the authority the Permit conferred" and does not bar the Army from acting under a separate legal authority — an emergency exclusion for munitions and explosives in federal hazardous-waste rules. On Sunday, the government asked the court to lift the stay; as of Monday morning, no ruling had been reported.

A sworn declaration from Col. Aaron Teller, commander of the Army's 71st Ordnance Group, says the 68 drums hold 9,773.5 pounds of material, with deterioration found in 58 of them. The lead azide was found degrading in March, after the liquid meant to keep it stable had largely evaporated; the contractor had not checked it since September 2025. Degraded lead azide is extremely sensitive to friction or movement, and the Army says moving it would risk an accidental detonation.

Health officials in Iowa and Illinois have advised people in affected areas to stay indoors on Wednesday with windows closed for 24 to 48 hours after the blast. The detonation would send a plume of lead dust into the air, larger particles settling nearby and finer dust drifting further, toward Iowa City and across the Mississippi River into western Illinois. The paused permit required a plume estimate within six hours, sampling within 24 hours, and a final report to the EPA within 30 days; the Army says it intends to follow those terms even if the permit is not in effect.

The environmental groups that sued now concede the blast may be unavoidable. "It appears that detonation may be, at this point, the only feasible option due to how long this problem was allowed to persist," said Michael Schmidt, general counsel for the Iowa Environmental Council. But a retired EPA official countered that the forecast was comparable to conditions the material had already endured. The Army has not said whether the 8 a.m. to 2 p.m. window it gave one Middletown neighbourhood to leave on the original date will apply on Wednesday.
