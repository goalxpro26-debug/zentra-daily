The Senate on Thursday passed a sweeping bipartisan bill to speed up approvals for energy and infrastructure projects, clearing the chamber 62–33 as lawmakers face pressure over rising electricity prices and surging demand from artificial intelligence.

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, which runs to more than 400 pages, would shorten deadlines for federal environmental reviews, narrow the window for lawsuits challenging projects, and limit the circumstances in which approvals can be withdrawn. It covers power plants, transmission lines, mines and data centers, and would require data centers to pay for the transmission costs they create.

The bill was written by two Republicans — Senators Shelley Moore Capito of West Virginia and Mike Lee of Utah — and two Democrats — Senators Martin Heinrich of New Mexico and Sheldon Whitehouse of Rhode Island. Fourteen Democrats and one independent joined most Republicans in supporting it; two Republicans voted against.

Supporters framed the vote as a response to household costs. The price of a kilowatt-hour has risen more than 6 percent in the last year and 39 percent in the last five years. Senate Majority Leader John Thune said the bill would "cut through decades of red tape," while Capito said its passage was a major step toward addressing "the affordability concerns of the American people." Whitehouse said Americans would "quickly begin getting some relief on their energy bills if the House follows suit." An analysis by the Rhodium Group estimated the bill would cut household electricity bills by $50 to $78 a year by 2040.

But the bill drew sharp opposition from environmental groups and many Democrats. The Sierra Club and the National Wildlife Federation argue it will weaken environmental accountability, and critics say it will make it easier to approve oil and gas projects and energy-hungry data centers while weakening protections for clean air and water. Some senators also raised concerns about tribal rights, and amendments from Oregon's senators — including one to ban data centers on public land — were not adopted.

Some climate advocates support the measure, arguing the country needs to build renewable energy projects and transmission lines faster and that the bill will help solar, wind and battery projects get built.

The bill now heads to the Republican-controlled House, where its prospects are uncertain. President Trump has urged Congress to approve it, calling it a "BIG WIN for U.S. ENERGY INDEPENDENCE and DOMINANCE."
