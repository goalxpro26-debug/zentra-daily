The frozen parts of the planet have moved beyond the safe limits that sustained human civilisation, according to a major new study published in the journal Science and presented at the COP31 climate summit in Türkiye.

The research, led by the Stockholm Resilience Centre at Stockholm University and involving more than 30 scientists, assessed the cryosphere — the planet's ice sheets, mountain glaciers, sea ice, permafrost and snow cover — against a "safe operating space" defined by the relatively stable conditions of the past 11,700 years, the Holocene epoch in which human societies developed.

Its conclusion is stark: three of the four measures it tracked have already crossed that boundary, and the fourth is heading the same way.

## What has already passed the limit

Sea ice area, snow cover and permafrost temperature have all moved beyond the safe operating space, the study finds. Land ice — almost 90 per cent of which sits in Antarctica — is still within the range today, but the authors warn that ice loss set in motion now commits the planet to centuries of further decline, so it should be treated as departing the safe zone as well.

"The cryosphere has already exceeded its planetary safe operating space, threatening the frozen foundations of our planet that have supported human civilisations for millennia," said Dr Bo Su, the study's lead author and a researcher at the Stockholm Resilience Centre and Beijing Normal University.

The degradation is driven predominantly by human-caused climate change, the paper finds, alongside regional pressures such as black carbon emissions and industrial and infrastructure expansion in frozen regions.

## Why frozen ground matters

The cryosphere plays an irreplaceable role in the planet's energy, water, carbon and methane cycles. Ice sheets and glaciers store fresh water, sea ice reflects sunlight back into space, snow cover regulates seasonal water supplies for billions of people, and permafrost locks away vast stores of carbon and methane that warming can release.

Losing those functions does not just mean higher seas. The study argues that cryosphere decline systematically threatens the Earth system conditions on which humanity depends, from water security in mountain regions to the stability of coastlines and the pace of warming itself.

## What happens next

The findings arrive during COP31 in Antalya, Türkiye (November 9–20), and in the middle of the UN Decade of Action for Cryospheric Sciences (2025–2034). The authors propose folding the cryosphere into the planetary boundaries framework — the science-based limits used to track how humanity is doing on climate, biodiversity and other global systems — so that frozen regions get the same structured attention as the atmosphere and oceans.

Two major UN-backed reports released last week reached a similar conclusion from a different angle: significant cryosphere losses are already unavoidable, and the question now is how far the overshoot goes.

The study does not set a single deadline for action. Its message is that the frozen foundations of the planet are no longer a stable backdrop — they are an active part of the climate crisis, and they are already moving.
