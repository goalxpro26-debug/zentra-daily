Scientists are lowering a set of giant sensor frames to the bottom of Lake Superior to hunt for neutrinos, the near-invisible particles physicists call "ghost particles." The project, known as the Experimental Neutrino Detector, is the first time neutrino detectors have been placed in a freshwater lake.

The effort is led by the University of Minnesota Duluth, working with the U.S. Department of Energy and two national laboratories. Its lead investigator is Alec Habig, a professor of physics and astronomy at the university.

The detectors arrived by an unusual route. The Neil Armstrong, a 238-foot ocean research vessel from the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution in Massachusetts, sailed across the Great Lakes and into Duluth this week. Crews spent Tuesday preparing the equipment at the Clure Terminal Expansion pier in Duluth.

On Thursday, the ship was to cruise about three miles out from Two Harbors, Minnesota, and crane the sensors onto the lakebed, about 200 metres below the surface. Crews will then lay fibre-optic cables connecting the detectors to a temporary facility on shore near Two Harbors.

## Five frames, 90 sensors

The installation consists of five frames, each about three stories tall and weighing roughly 8,000 pounds. Each frame holds 18 spherical sensors about the size of a beach ball, filled with phototubes. The sensors, called digital optical modules, are designed to catch the faint blue flashes of light produced when a neutrino interacts with matter.

Neutrinos almost never interact with anything, which is why they are so hard to study — and so useful. Trillions pass through every person, and through the Earth itself, every second without leaving a trace.

This experiment targets neutrinos from a beam generated at Fermilab outside Chicago, fired since 2005 and aimed underground toward northern Minnesota, passing under Wisconsin and by Lake Superior off Two Harbors. The Lake Superior detectors are designed to pick the beam's neutrinos out from the solar and other natural neutrinos that are always present.

## Why it matters

Physicists study neutrinos to answer fundamental questions about the universe, including why it is made mostly of matter rather than antimatter. A freshwater lake offers a vast, dark, naturally shielded volume in which to watch for the rare flashes that betray a neutrino's passage.

The project is sponsored by the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency, at a cost in the tens of millions of dollars, project leaders said. DARPA program manager John Bruzzese said the agency does not have a specific application in mind for the work.

The Fermilab beam is scheduled to shut off in February, because scientists have built a newer beam. When the Lake Superior experiment ends, the underwater sensors will be retrieved and sent to Europe for neutrino research deep in the Mediterranean Sea.

Minnesota has a long neutrino history: a former detector sat deep in the Soudan Underground Mine near Ely, and a laboratory still operates at Ash River outside Voyageurs National Park.
