Astronomers using the world's largest radio telescope have captured the first direct evidence of a dying star's final thermonuclear pulse — a "final nuclear heartbeat" preserved as a ring of gas around a star 12,000 light-years away.

IRAS 06530-0213, in the constellation Monoceros, is in the brief, violent transition between a bloated red giant and a glowing planetary nebula. Observations with the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) — 66 antennas in northern Chile — reveal a thin shell of gas about half a light-year wide encircling a young hourglass-shaped nebula still taking shape. The research, led by NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory's Raghvendra Sahai, was published on November 20 in The Astrophysical Journal.

By measuring how fast the gas is moving outward, the team estimates the shell was blown off around 6,500 years ago, during the star's last thermal pulse — a flare-up in which helium burning briefly intensified, flinging gas outward. Until now, such pulses had only been inferred from models. "We've caught, for the first time, a star's final nuclear heartbeat, frozen in gas," Sahai said.

The images also let the team reconstruct what happened next. For roughly 2,000 years after the pulse, the star shed material slowly. Then, over the next 1,000 years, mass loss rose sharply, building a thick, lumpy shell now seen as a tangle of curved filaments. Their spiral shape may mean an unseen companion is orbiting the star, stirring the gas. Inside the shell, the hourglass nebula and a dense doughnut of gas around its middle formed within just the last 700 years — so the star left its red giant phase very recently in cosmic terms.

Two surprises turned up: an unexpected abundance of heavy isotopes of carbon, oxygen and nitrogen — a mix that does not fit current models of how such stars forge and spread elements. And ALMA found no dust disk around the star, although such disks are usually seen around dying stars with companions and have been used to explain hourglass shapes. The team suggests a disk may have existed briefly, driving jets that sculpted the hourglass before fading.

The finding matters because stars like IRAS 06530-0213 die the way the Sun will. In billions of years, the Sun will swell into a red giant, then shed its outer layers and leave a white dwarf wrapped in a glowing shell. This is a physical snapshot of that process in action — and a sign the standard story is more complicated than assumed. "Dying stars so often have hidden companions that reshape their ejected material into intricate forms instead of simple spheres," Sahai said. "IRAS 06530 shows the story is more complicated than the tidy explanation we've been using."

Unresolved: whether a companion is really there, why the chemical mix is so unusual, and how the hourglass formed without a surviving disk. The observations are part of PANORAMA, an ALMA survey of dying stars in transition; IRAS 06530-0213 is the first object from the survey to be presented in detail.
