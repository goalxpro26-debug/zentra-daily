Oregon has confirmed its first human case of bird flu this year: a dairy worker in Morrow County who caught the virus from infected cattle and developed only mild symptoms.

The Oregon Health Authority announced the case on Thursday, November 19. Its epidemiologists had received confirmation from the Oregon State Public Health Laboratory the day before. The worker, who was in contact with an infected herd, developed conjunctivitis and upper-respiratory symptoms, was treated with the antiviral medicine oseltamivir, and did not need hospital care. People in the worker's household were offered the drug as a precaution.

The case was found through routine monitoring. Weekly testing of milk from dairies across Oregon flagged the virus in the Morrow County herd earlier in November — the state's first detection of highly pathogenic avian influenza in dairy cattle, confirmed on November 10. The farm was placed under quarantine, and the state agriculture department supplied protective equipment and safety training to workers. Genetic sequencing links the Oregon outbreak to dairy herds in Washington's Yakima Valley, where dozens of herds have recently tested positive.

Health officials say the response will stay in place for weeks. The state epidemiologist, Dean Sidelinger, said health workers will keep working with the farm for at least six more weeks, offering testing and early treatment to catch any further human cases. The agriculture department and Morrow County Public Health are monitoring people who were exposed to the virus.

This is not Oregon's first encounter with the virus in people. Public health officials have monitored more than 300 people exposed to sick animals since 2022, and one human case was identified in 2024 in connection with a commercial poultry outbreak.

For the general public, officials say the risk remains very low. The virus is not known to spread from person to person, and the health authority described the risk to the public as "extremely low." Melissa Sutton, the authority's medical director for respiratory viral pathogens, said the case was evidence the surveillance system was working: "This is not cause for alarm. Rather, it is evidence that the surveillance and response system we have in place is working. Our goal is to ensure that all symptomatic farm workers receive prompt testing and treatment to prevent severe disease."

The seasonal flu vaccine does not protect against avian influenza, though it can lower the risk of catching a human flu virus at the same time, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

The exact strain involved has not been publicly named, and officials have not released details about the worker, which is standard practice to protect patient privacy.
