Astronomers using the James Webb Space Telescope have found the most distant "little red dot" yet discovered — a faint, compact object that appears to have existed just 400 to 500 million years after the Big Bang.

The object, named CW-LRD-z10, sits at a redshift of 10.36, meaning its light set out when the universe was roughly 450 million years old. The finding is reported in a new study in *The Astrophysical Journal Letters*.

Little red dots are small, intensely red points of light that Webb first began detecting in 2022. Many are thought to be young supermassive black holes, still growing, wrapped in dense gas and dust. They have become a central puzzle because they suggest giant black holes were already in place far earlier than most theories predicted — yet until now, every one found had existed between 600 million and 1.5 billion years after the Big Bang.

The new object pushes that record back by a wide margin. The previous most distant little red dot with a measured redshift stood at 9.288.

The discovery was led by Takumi Tanaka, a graduate student at the University of Tokyo's Kavli Institute for the Physics and Mathematics of the Universe, working with professor John Silverman and an international team, using high-resolution infrared images from Webb's COSMOS-Web survey.

Finding it took a new approach. Webb's near-infrared camera can only study light at wavelengths between 0.6 and 5 micrometres, but light from such a distant object has been stretched far beyond that range by the expansion of the universe. The team combined data from the near-infrared camera, NIRCam, with the mid-infrared instrument, MIRI, to pick out the tiny object in its characteristic red.

The team then confirmed the distance with spectroscopy. The spectrum showed the Lyman-alpha break — the sharp drop in light caused by absorption from neutral hydrogen in the early universe — along with emission lines from hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and iron. It closely matches previously discovered little red dots, confirming CW-LRD-z10 as the most distant one yet.

One caveat remains: CW-LRD-z10 is still a candidate, not a confirmed black hole. The broad hydrogen lines that would clearly show a feeding black hole are stretched beyond this spectrum's reach, and the data cannot yet rule out that some of the light comes from star formation.

That uncertainty is part of why the object matters. If little red dots are the seeds of the supermassive black holes at the centres of most galaxies today, finding one this early narrows the window in which those seeds had to form. Theories have suggested such precursors could have appeared around 500 million years after the Big Bang — and now there is an object sitting almost exactly in that era.

Next, the team plans to study CW-LRD-z10 in more detail, examining its hydrogen emission lines for signs of black hole activity. Finding more little red dots from the same early epoch should help pin down when they first appeared and how they formed.
