A federal appeals court has blocked the US Army from detonating about five tons of unstable lead explosive at a munitions plant in south-east Iowa, halting a plan that alarmed residents and state officials over the toxic lead dust it would scatter.

The US Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit issued an administrative stay on Thursday night, barring the Army from using an Environmental Protection Agency emergency permit to carry out the blast. The one-sentence order, signed by Judge Susan Bindler, came hours after the Iowa Environmental Council and the Sierra Club's Iowa chapter — represented by the advocacy group Earthjustice — sued to stop it.

At issue are 68 barrels holding roughly 9,700 pounds of lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, near Burlington. The Army discovered in March that the compound, stored for about 20 years, was crystallising after the ethanol-and-water mixture meant to stabilise it had largely evaporated. Lead azide is used to set off munitions, and in its degraded state it is extremely sensitive to friction or movement.

The Army had planned two controlled detonations at about noon on Friday. Late on Thursday, Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle postponed the operation to Monday, November 23, calling that the latest possible moment before a forecast temperature drop that could further destabilise the material. The Army says a controlled blast is safer than moving the material, which could detonate by accident in transit, and that it has been planning the operation since March under an EPA emergency permit issued on November 13.

But the blast would send a plume of lead particles into the air: larger ones would probably settle within about 20 miles, while finer dust could drift further, toward Iowa City and across the Mississippi River into western Illinois. Residents were told to stay indoors for a day or two and wash down surfaces afterwards. There is no safe level of lead exposure, and Iowa already has the second-highest cancer rate of any US state.

On Friday, the Justice Department told the appeals court it intends to oppose the environmental groups' request for a longer halt, and cited a federal regulation it said could allow the Army to treat hazardous explosive waste without an EPA permit in a qualifying emergency. The filing did not say the Army had decided to use that route, and the Army is not a party to the lawsuit. The EPA said it would file its own response by November 22.

Iowa Governor Kim Reynolds activated the state's emergency operations centre on Friday, and Illinois Governor JB Pritzker pressed the Army for more transparency about the risk to his state. The Army, the EPA and the Department of Health and Human Services said in a joint statement that they were working together to carry out the detonation "in the safest fashion possible." With the permit suspended, whether Monday's blast can go ahead is now for the court to decide; it has not said when it will rule.
