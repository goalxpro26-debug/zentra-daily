Astronomers have watched a star swallow its companion whole — and, for the first time, pieced together the entire event from beginning to end.

The pair, formally known as AT 2025abao and nicknamed Bao, sits on the outskirts of the Andromeda galaxy, about 2.5 million light-years from Earth. The smaller star was slightly less massive than our Sun. Its companion was a puffy supergiant roughly seven times the Sun's mass, 350 times its radius and 18,000 times as bright. When the giant swelled up, it engulfed the smaller star, which spiralled inward through its outer layers until the two became one.

What makes this merger special is the record around it. Researchers first noticed unusual activity in the system in 2023 and kept watching. The system erupted in October 2025 — the flash was spotted by amateur astronomer Koichi Itagaki and picked up by the Zwicky Transient Facility — and NASA's James Webb Space Telescope observed it just 47 days after the eruption peaked, on December 24, 2025. That is the earliest follow-up ever made of an event like this, and it gave the team the final piece needed to reconstruct what happened before, during and after the merger.

The findings, published Wednesday in the journal Nature, describe a surprisingly fast end. Once the smaller star touched the giant's surface, the two merged after only about seven orbits — roughly seven years — far quicker than other known mergers of two smaller stars. The process is known as common-envelope evolution: the two stars briefly share a single outer layer of gas before becoming one, possibly rejuvenated, star.

The result matters because binary stars are common, and their mergers shape the objects astronomers see across the universe — from unusual bright stars to the progenitors of some supernovae. Standard models have struggled to explain how energy moves around inside a shared envelope, and Bao's unusually complete timeline gives theorists a rare test case to check their work against.

There is still room for doubt. Dust thrown off by the eruption currently hides the merged star itself, so the merger is an inference drawn from the light, the dust and the simulations — strong, but indirect. A separate modelling study, published as a preprint in October and not yet peer-reviewed, argues the two stars' cores might have survived in orbit without fully merging. Future observations will be needed to settle that question for good.

For now, Bao stands as the best-observed stellar merger yet: a slow-motion collision, caught on camera across three years, in the galaxy next door.
