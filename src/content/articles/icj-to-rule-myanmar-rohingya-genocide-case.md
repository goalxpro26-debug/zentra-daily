The United Nations' highest court will deliver its judgment on Thursday in a case accusing Myanmar of genocide against its Rohingya minority — a ruling that could make legal history.

The International Court of Justice will begin reading its judgment at 10 a.m. local time (09:00 GMT) at the Peace Palace in The Hague. Judge Yuji Iwasawa, the court's president, will read the decision in a session expected to last up to 90 minutes.

The case was brought by The Gambia, a small West African nation, in November 2019, with the backing of the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation. It accuses Myanmar of violating the 1948 Genocide Convention during a military crackdown in Rakhine State in August and September 2017 that drove more than 730,000 Rohingya — most of them Muslim — across the border into Bangladesh. Survivors have described killings, mass rape and the burning of villages. A United Nations fact-finding mission in 2018 concluded that the campaign included "genocidal acts".

Myanmar denies the charge. It says its forces were responding to attacks by armed Rohingya militants on police posts, and argues there is no direct evidence of an intent to destroy the Rohingya as a people.

That question of intent is the heart of the case. Under the Genocide Convention, genocide requires proof of a specific intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial or religious group, in whole or in part. The Gambia has argued that genocidal intent is the only reasonable inference from the scale and pattern of the 2017 operation. The court held hearings on the merits from January 12 to 29 this year.

If the court finds against Myanmar, it would be the first time the International Court of Justice has held a state responsible for committing genocide. In 2007, the court found that Serbia had failed to prevent the Srebrenica massacre in Bosnia, in which more than 8,000 Muslim men and boys were killed — but ruled that Serbia had not itself committed genocide. The Myanmar judgment would be the court's first ruling on the merits of a genocide case in more than a decade.

The judgment will be final, with no right of appeal. But the court has no way to enforce its decisions. The Gambia has asked it to order Myanmar to prosecute those responsible, pay reparations and ensure the Rohingya can return safely. If Myanmar ignores the ruling, The Gambia could ask the UN Security Council to act.

For the Rohingya themselves, the wait has been long. About 1.2 million now live in the Cox's Bazar refugee settlement in Bangladesh, and roughly 550,000 remain in Myanmar, where they face restrictions on movement, healthcare and livelihoods. A finding of genocide would formally recognise the harm they suffered, even if it brings no immediate change on the ground.

## What is still unknown

The outcome itself is not yet public, and the court could stop short of a genocide finding — for instance, by ruling that Myanmar failed to prevent or punish genocide without committing it. Whatever it decides, the judgment settles the legal question; whether it changes anything for the refugees in Cox's Bazar will depend on what states and the Security Council do next.
