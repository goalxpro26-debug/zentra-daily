Americans head into Black Friday ready to spend, but the headline sales figures will flatter a more fragile reality. Forecasters expect holiday spending to beat last year's total by 4 to 6 percent — yet with inflation running at 3.8 percent, much of that gain is higher prices rather than fuller shopping baskets.

The National Retail Federation expects November–December retail sales to rise 4.2 percent from a year ago, to just above $1 trillion. The Mastercard Economics Institute and EY Parthenon are more optimistic, each forecasting growth of more than 5 percent. Final results often beat pre-season estimates, but most forecasts predate the recent stock-market slide.

Beneath the totals, shoppers are being careful. A November survey by the National Retail Federation found that 82 percent of consumers are taking steps to manage holiday costs, such as buying less expensive gifts. "I want to get more gifts that people will actually use or need," Felicia Cox, a 50-year-old sales representative from Los Angeles, told Reuters last week. "I feel like people aren't able to afford as much stuff."

The willingness to shop is still there. Deloitte found that 87 percent of the 1,200 consumers it surveyed in October planned to take part in the five-day shopping stretch from Thanksgiving through Cyber Monday on November 30 — the highest share in a decade. But Kearney found middle- and lower-income shoppers, who have been trading down for months, were more likely than wealthier ones to spend less.

Retailers have noticed. Target, Dollar Tree and Kohl's have all pointed to belt-tightening in recent earnings calls. Kohl's chief executive Michael Bender described an "increasingly choiceful" customer on Tuesday, saying shoppers are "looking to stretch every dollar."

Toys are feeling the squeeze most sharply. Prices have risen 13.2 percent since the start of the year, driven in part by higher costs for plastics, and Barbie maker Mattel cut its annual forecast this month as shoppers balked at higher prices. Squishy NeeDoh toys were selling for $24.99 on Amazon in October, nearly triple the price a year earlier, according to e-commerce platform Omnisend.

The market backdrop adds another note of caution. The recent stock-market correction — following the midterm elections, turmoil at chipmaker Nvidia and mounting worries about an AI bubble — could dampen the "wealth effect" behind affluent shoppers' splurges on gadgets, UBS analysts said in a note on Tuesday.

Artificial intelligence may also change how the season plays out. As shoppers use AI tools to hunt for bargains or even complete purchases, and retailers get quicker at changing prices, the deepest discounts may arrive closer to Christmas, said Ryan Orabone, a consultant at Spaulding Ridge. "My advice to shoppers is to be patient," he said. "Don't jump on the first deal you see."

For now, the tills are expected to ring. But with prices doing much of the work, this year's Black Friday will measure how much Americans are willing to pay — not just how much they are willing to buy.
