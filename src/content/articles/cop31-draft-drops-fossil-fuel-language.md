Climate negotiators at COP31 in Antalya, Türkiye, have stripped all mention of ending fossil fuel use from a slimmed-down draft of the summit deal, published on Thursday as the talks approached their scheduled Friday close.

The first draft, published on Monday, had contained two references to fossil fuels and to limiting their use — a subject that divides countries at the climate talks almost every year. The revised text removes both. In their place, the draft encourages countries to scale up technologies that pull carbon out of the atmosphere, a shift in emphasis from cutting fossil fuels at the source toward capturing emissions after the fact.

The draft still acknowledges that global warming could pass the 1.5 degrees Celsius threshold countries have sought to preserve, and it calls for "deep, rapid and sustained" cuts to greenhouse gas emissions. What it no longer says is anything about winding down the fuels that produce most of those emissions.

Australia, which is leading the negotiations under a split presidency arrangement with Türkiye, had said on Monday that the opening draft was always meant to be a broad document that would then be narrowed down. The latest text is expected to be revised further before anything can be agreed, and the final wording may yet change.

The conference is scheduled to end on Friday, but that deadline is rarely kept. In recent years, efforts to secure consensus among the nearly 200 countries taking part have regularly run more than 24 hours past the scheduled close.

The need to limit rising temperatures is widely accepted among the delegations in Antalya. They disagree on how to do it, who should pay for it, and how quickly the world needs to act — and the fate of the fossil-fuel language is the clearest measure of that disagreement.

The talks are taking place as the effects of a warming climate — heatwaves, floods and droughts — grow more severe and more regular, even as they compete for policymakers' attention with wars and rising living costs.

What happens next is still open. Negotiators have a day of scheduled talks left, and the draft will go through further revisions before any deal is put to the nearly 200 participating countries. Whether fossil-fuel language returns in a later version, and what the final text says about paying for the transition, will decide how the summit is judged.
