The first shipments of a low-cost generic version of lenacapavir, a twice-yearly injection that prevents HIV infection, have arrived in Zambia, the Global Fund to Fight AIDS, Tuberculosis and Malaria announced on Wednesday.

A delivery to Uganda is expected in the coming days, and a further shipment financed by the Global Fund is due to reach Zimbabwe in early 2027. The arrivals come about a year after the branded version of the drug first became available in sub-Saharan Africa.

Lenacapavir is a long-acting form of pre-exposure prophylaxis, or PrEP. Instead of a daily pill, it is given as an injection twice a year, and clinical evidence shows it is nearly 100% effective at preventing HIV infection. The generic version is expected to cost about $40 per person per year, far less than the original drug developed by the US pharmaceutical company Gilead Sciences.

India's Hetero, one of the generic manufacturers, said on Thursday it had begun shipping the drug to Africa and plans to supply 300,000 vials to Zambia, Uganda and Zimbabwe. It plans to extend supply to 10 more African countries in early 2027.

Gilead has licensed six generic drugmakers, including Hetero and Dr. Reddy's Laboratories, to manufacture and supply the drug in 120 low- and middle-income countries. The US Food and Drug Administration gave tentative approval to a generic version earlier this month, although it cannot be sold in the United States while Gilead's branded version, Yeztugo, remains under patent.

The rollout is moving fast. More than 189,000 people across 10 countries had received a first dose of lenacapavir as of November, according to the Global Fund, and 24 countries are expected to have received supplies by the end of 2026. The Global Fund and the US government aim to reach at least 3 million people with the drug by the end of 2028, and plan to deliver four times as much lenacapavir in 2027 as in 2026.

The evidence behind the drug is striking. In 2024 trials involving more than 5,000 adolescent girls and young women in Uganda and South Africa, none of those who received the injection contracted HIV. That result matters because daily PrEP pills, while effective, are hard for many people to take consistently — and a missed dose leaves a gap in protection.

For health systems across southern and eastern Africa, where the HIV burden remains highest, the arrival of an affordable twice-yearly option could change who gets protected. The question now is delivery: getting the injections from warehouses in Lusaka and Kampala into clinics, and from clinics to the people most at risk, in time to meet the 2028 target.
