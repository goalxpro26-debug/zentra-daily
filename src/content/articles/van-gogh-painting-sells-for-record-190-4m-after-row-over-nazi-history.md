A painting Vincent van Gogh made in the final weeks of his life has sold in New York for $190.4 million (£144.8 million), the highest price ever paid at auction for a work by the Dutch artist.

*Châtaigniers en fleurs* — *Chestnut Trees in Blossom* — went under the hammer at Sotheby's on Wednesday, fetching $165 million before fees took the total to $190.4 million. The result beat the pre-sale estimate of $180 million and shattered Van Gogh's previous auction record of $117.2 million, set in 2022 when his 1888 *Orchard with Cypresses* sold at Christie's in New York. It also ranks as the fourth-most expensive painting ever sold at auction.

Van Gogh painted the work in 1890, in the final two months of his life, during a burst of creativity after his release from a psychiatric asylum that May. He died two months later. The canvas had spent decades out of public view, held in the collection of Argentina's Blaquier-Arrieta family, who acquired it in 1975 and owned it for more than half a century.

The sale almost did not happen. In the days before the auction, representatives of the heirs of Samuel Fischer — the German-Jewish publisher who bought the painting in Paris in 1909 — argued that the family had been forced to part with it under Nazi persecution. Fischer died in 1934, and the painting was sold in 1936, after the family had suffered financial losses under the Nazis. The heirs' lawyer had called on Sotheby's to show the work had been sold at its market value, and to postpone the sale until it did.

On Tuesday, a day before the auction, Sotheby's announced that an agreement had been reached between the Blaquier-Arrieta family and all of Fischer's legal descendants, allowing the sale to go ahead. The terms were not disclosed. Sotheby's said the two families had reached "a mutually satisfactory resolution" in recognition of the painting's complex Nazi-era history and the suffering endured by the Fischer family during the Holocaust. The Fischer heirs said they were grateful for a negotiation that made it possible to acknowledge that suffering.

The painting's journey traces a century of European history. After Fischer acquired it in 1909, it was deposited with the dealer Paul Cassirer in Amsterdam in 1932 or 1933, then passed to Henriette and Marcel Kapferer in Paris in 1936. Four further owners followed before the Blaquier family bought it in 1975.

Sotheby's did not immediately name the buyer. The painting was the most valuable lot offered across New York's auction houses this season, and its sale helped drive a strong week of marquee sales in the city.
