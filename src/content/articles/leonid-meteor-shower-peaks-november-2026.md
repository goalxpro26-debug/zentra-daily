The Leonid meteor shower reaches its peak this week, with the best viewing in the dark hours before dawn on Wednesday, November 18. Skywatchers under a clear, dark sky can expect roughly 10 to 15 meteors an hour — a modest show by Leonid standards, but one arriving under unusually good conditions this year.

The shower is active from November 6 to November 30, as Earth passes through a trail of dust left behind by Comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle. The peak is expected late on Tuesday, November 17: the International Meteor Organization puts the maximum at about 11:49 p.m. UTC (6:49 p.m. Eastern), and the American Meteor Society forecasts the peak around midnight UTC on November 18. In practice, the meteors worth watching for come after midnight, as the constellation Leo — the shower's radiant — climbs higher in the eastern sky and is highest at dawn.

## The Moon is out of the way this year

This year the Moon is cooperating. It reaches its first-quarter phase on November 17 and sets around midnight, leaving the sky dark for the prime viewing hours. That matters more than the raw meteor rate: a bright Moon can wash out the fainter meteors, and this year it will be gone before the best of the shower arrives.

## Fast meteors with glowing trains

Leonids are known for their speed. The dust grains strike the atmosphere at about 44 miles per second, and the brighter meteors often leave glowing trains that linger for a few seconds after the streak fades. The meteors appear to radiate from Leo, near the star Regulus, but they can streak across any part of the sky — the radiant is a starting point for the eye, not a place to stare.

## A shower with a dramatic past

The Leonids have produced some of the most famous meteor displays in history. In 1833 the shower was so intense that witnesses described the sky as falling, and in November 1966 observers in parts of the United States saw thousands of meteors per minute over about 15 minutes. Storms on that scale returned in 1999 and 2001. Nothing like that is expected this year — forecasters describe 2026 as an ordinary Leonid year — but that history is why the shower still draws a crowd every November.

## How to watch

No equipment is needed. Find the darkest sky you can, away from streetlights, and give your eyes 20 to 30 minutes to adapt. Lie back, look up, and be patient: the meteors come in bursts, and a quiet stretch does not mean the show is over. The shower is visible from both the Northern and Southern Hemispheres. As a bonus, Mars and Jupiter sit close together near Leo this week, a short distance apart in the pre-dawn sky.
