U.S. stocks fell sharply on Friday, dragging the Dow Jones Industrial Average and the S&P 500 into correction territory and wiping out the Nasdaq Composite's gains for the year, as a sell-off in artificial-intelligence stocks turned into a broad market rout.

The Dow fell 949.73 points, or 1.9 percent, to 48,147.33, more than 10 percent below its record high in August. The S&P 500 dropped 265.44 points, or 3.7 percent, to 6,910.39, also more than 10 percent below its October peak. A correction is generally defined as a decline of 10 percent or more from a recent high. The Nasdaq Composite sank 1,261.47 points, or 5.3 percent, to 22,617.73, coming close to bear-market territory — a fall of 20 percent or more from its peak.

It was the worst week for American stocks in years. For the week, the S&P 500 fell 8.6 percent, the Dow lost 8 percent and the Nasdaq tumbled 10 percent, its worst week since March 2020. The S&P 500 has now fallen for seven straight sessions, its longest losing streak since February 2020, and members of the Nasdaq lost more than $4 trillion in market value this week alone. The Cboe Volatility Index, Wall Street's "fear gauge," jumped to its highest level this year.

The selling was led by the technology companies at the heart of the AI boom. Nvidia, whose chips power the data centres behind AI, plunged 14 percent on Friday to $125.53 and finished the week down 23 percent — about half its value since its October 6 record high. Investors are still unsettled by the sudden departure of chief executive Jensen Huang this month, and by questions about the financing behind the AI buildout.

Anthropic, the AI lab behind the Claude chatbot, also slid. Its shares fell 9.1 percent to $88.30, close to the $83 IPO price, after the biggest initial public offering in U.S. history failed to calm the market.

The rout spread well beyond technology. All 11 sectors of the S&P 500 fell by more than 1 percent this week, and retailers dropped on fears that a slump in AI investment and home prices could slow the wider economy. Analysts pointed to a pile-up of worries: rising financing costs for the AI buildout, persistent inflation and a Federal Reserve seen as inclined to raise interest rates, a strengthening dollar drawing liquidity out of markets, and Democratic victories in the midterm elections raising the prospect of heavier data-centre regulation.

Some traders called the sell-off a necessary reset as investors re-evaluate the companies that have driven market gains for nearly four years. Others worry the cracks in the AI story are now too deep to ignore. Either way, a market that spent most of 2026 climbing on the promise of artificial intelligence ended the week asking whether that promise was priced too high.
