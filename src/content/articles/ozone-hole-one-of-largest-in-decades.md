This year's ozone hole above Antarctica grew into one of the largest in nearly five decades of records, reaching 27.3 million square kilometres by September 20 — an area far larger than Antarctica itself and about 31 percent above the average of the last 20 years for that time of year.

The finding, based on long-term observations including data from Europe's Copernicus Sentinel-5P satellite, marks a sharp swing from 2025, when the seasonal hole was the fifth smallest since 1992. Scientists say the hole's size varies naturally year to year, and the ozone layer's long-term recovery remains on track.

The cause this year was an unusually strong and persistent polar vortex, the swirling mass of cold air that forms over Antarctica each winter. It kept temperatures in the Antarctic stratosphere favourable for ozone depletion for longer than usual, allowing the hole to expand well beyond its normal extent.

A separate NOAA and NASA assessment, using the seasonal mean area, ranked the 2026 hole as the seventh-largest in 48 years of monitoring. It peaked on September 18 at 10.5 million square miles (27.1 million square kilometres) — about 90 percent as large as the biggest hole ever observed, on September 9, 2000. During peak depletion, September 7 to October 13, it averaged about 25 million square kilometres, more than twice the area of the United States.

But size tells only part of the story. Sentinel-5P data show the depth of this year's hole — how severely ozone was depleted inside it — stayed close to average: wider than usual, but not as deep as other large holes on record. Balloon instruments launched from the South Pole found ozone overhead at its lowest for the year, 105 Dobson units, on October 17.

The hole typically begins to form in August, grows through September and peaks between mid-September and mid-October. As the stratosphere warms in the Southern Hemisphere spring, depletion slows, the polar vortex weakens and breaks down, and by the end of December the hole has usually closed.

There are also signs the layer is healing. Scientists note the hole has been forming later in the season in recent years, and ozone-depleting chemicals have fallen sharply since their peak around 2000 under the Montreal Protocol, the 1987 agreement that phased them out. NOAA and NASA estimate the hole would have been 1 to 2 million square miles larger this year if those chemicals were still as prevalent as at their peak — and that the Antarctic hole should recover by the 2070s.

Sentinel-5P, launched in October 2017, was the first Copernicus satellite dedicated to monitoring the atmosphere. Its Tropomi instrument detects the fingerprints of atmospheric gases to image stratospheric ozone at higher resolution than earlier sensors. Its successor, Sentinel-5, and the upcoming Altius mission should keep tracking the layer's recovery with greater accuracy.
