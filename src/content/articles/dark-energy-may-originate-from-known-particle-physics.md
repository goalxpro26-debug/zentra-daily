For nearly three decades, physicists have known that the universe is not just expanding but speeding up — yet no one could say what is doing the pushing. A new study proposes an answer hiding in plain sight: the same force that binds atomic nuclei together.

Dark energy, the name given to whatever drives that acceleration, accounts for roughly 70 percent of the universe's total energy budget. Proposed explanations usually invoke new, undiscovered fields or particles — none of which has ever been detected.

Researchers at the University of British Columbia and the University of Sheffield now argue that no new ingredients are needed at all. In a paper published in *The Astrophysical Journal*, they trace dark energy to quantum chromodynamics, or QCD — the established theory of the strong nuclear force, which binds quarks inside protons and neutrons.

The key is that empty space is not truly empty. In QCD, the vacuum is a busy environment of subatomic fields and quantum energy, with topological properties that can reach across vast distances. The team proposes that as the universe expands, this vacuum structure responds differently over time, producing a small energy shift — just strong enough to drive cosmic acceleration.

Crucially, the numbers fall out of physics that is already known. The energy scale predicted by combining the QCD vacuum with gravity lands close to the dark-energy scale astronomers actually observe, emerging from known physical constants without any extra fitting parameters or ad hoc fields.

The paper was led by Dong Ha Lee, a doctoral researcher at the University of Sheffield, building on theoretical ideas developed by Ariel Zhitnitsky, a physics and astronomy professor at the University of British Columbia. Ludovic Van Waerbeke, also at UBC, worked on the analysis.

To test the idea, the Sheffield team compared the model with real astronomical data — light from distant exploding stars, or supernovae, and the leftover radiation from the early universe. The model matched the observations about as well as the standard cosmological models, while also offering a physical origin for dark energy, something the standard picture does not provide. In the standard model, dark energy is treated as a cosmological constant: a number that fits the data but explains nothing about where the energy comes from.

The authors frame this as a proposal, not a proof. Their next steps are to refine the calculations and test the framework against data from next-generation space telescopes, which are expected to map the universe's expansion history in unprecedented detail over the coming years.

If it holds up, the result would tie the largest-scale mystery in cosmology to the smallest-scale force in physics — the same force holding every atomic nucleus together.
