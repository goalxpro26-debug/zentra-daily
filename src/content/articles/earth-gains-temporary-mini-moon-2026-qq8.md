Earth has gained a second moon — but it will not stay. A tiny asteroid, roughly the size of a person, has been looping around our planet since April, and astronomers expect it to drift away in December.

The visitor is called 2026 QQ8. It measures only about 1–2 metres (3–6 feet) across, and it is so small and dim that it took astronomers several weeks to confirm what it was. The International Astronomical Union's Minor Planet Center formally named it in late October.

Hungarian astronomer Krisztián Sárneczky, a research coordinator at the Konkoly Observatory who has found hundreds of near-Earth asteroids, first spotted the object in August. Only later, once its orbit had been calculated precisely, did its unusual nature become clear: it had been captured by Earth's gravity months earlier, in April, and had been circling the planet ever since.

For a while, astronomers could not rule out that it was a piece of space junk — a defunct satellite or a fragment of one. Only by watching how sunlight pressing on the object affected its motion over several weeks could they establish that it was a rock, not hardware. Observations from the Lowell Discovery Telescope in Arizona and the University of Hawaii's 2.2-metre telescope on Mauna Kea settled the question.

The asteroid most likely came from the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter, though its exact origin is still being studied. In April it drifted close enough, and slowly enough, for Earth's gravity — helped a little by the Moon's — to bend its path into a wide, looping orbit. At its closest it stays about 568,000 kilometres away, roughly one and a half times the distance to the Moon.

There is no danger. Even if it did fall towards Earth, it would burn up in the atmosphere. "At best, you would get a bright meteor in the sky and a bit of a bang," said Matthew Payne, director of the Minor Planet Center.

Earth's temporary companions are not as rare as they sound. Previous mini-moons include 2006 RH120, which stayed about a year, and 2020 CD3, which orbited for about three years. "On average, there's probably one there at any given time," Payne said. "They hang around for a few weeks or months. Sometimes it's none, sometimes it's a couple." Most are simply too small, too far away and too fast-moving for current surveys to spot.

2026 QQ8 is not expected to be captured again soon. It approaches Earth roughly once every ten years, but its next close pass, in 2058, is not expected to end in another temporary orbit. By early 2027 it should be back on its own path around the Sun — a journey that takes it about 359 days to complete.

About 40,000 near-Earth objects have been identified so far, and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile is beginning a ten-year survey that should expand the catalogue of known solar system objects from about 1.5 million to 5 million.
