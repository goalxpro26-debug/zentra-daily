As people get older, sleep often gets shorter and more broken. Now MIT researchers have found a deeper change: the waves of fluid that flush waste out of the brain during deep sleep are significantly weaker in older adults.

The team, led by neuroscientist Laura Lewis, recruited volunteers in two age groups — 18 to 39, and 60 to 84 — and studied them as they fell asleep late at night inside brain scanners. Using standard functional MRI to track blood flow, a modified MRI technique Lewis developed to track cerebrospinal fluid, and EEG to measure brain waves, they watched what happened as volunteers drifted into deep, non-REM sleep.

In younger adults, slow brain waves grew stronger as they entered deep sleep, and the waves of cerebrospinal fluid grew larger with them. In the older group, the average fluid waves were significantly smaller. The difference was tied to a decline in the slow waves themselves — not simply to older people sleeping less.

The researchers also found the brain's blood vessels responded less eagerly in older adults. When volunteers looked at a flickering checkerboard image, neural activity rose as expected, but blood flow did not rise as much as it did in younger people. A breath-holding test showed the same pattern: a smaller increase in blood flow. A statistical model showed that weaker "neurovascular coupling" and smaller blood-flow responses explained much of the drop in fluid waves, though not all of it.

The work builds on a 2019 study from the same lab showing that slow waves during deep sleep generate the fluid waves that clear waste from the brain. None of the volunteers in the new study had been diagnosed with a neurodegenerative disorder, but some older participants showed signs of grey-matter loss, which tracked with larger reductions in fluid flow.

The team now wants to find out whether weaker waste clearance raises the risk of Alzheimer's disease and other forms of dementia, and whether the sleep patterns they measured could one day help identify people at greater risk. They also hope to test whether interventions can strengthen the process: in a recent study, short bursts of "pink noise" during sleep increased fluid flow in younger adults, but the approach has not yet been tested in older adults. The study appears in Nature Aging.
