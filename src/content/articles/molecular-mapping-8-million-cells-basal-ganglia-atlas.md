Scientists have produced the most detailed molecular map yet of the basal ganglia, the cluster of structures deep inside the human brain that governs movement, motivation, habits and decision-making. The atlas, published on November 12 in the journal *Cell*, charts about 8 million individual cells and identifies 60 distinct cell types and the communities they form.

The work was led by the University of California, Irvine, under Xiangmin Xu, a professor of anatomy and neurobiology and director of the university's Center for Neural Circuit Mapping. Using brain samples from four neurotypical donors, the team combined two spatial transcriptomics techniques — MERFISH+ and Stereo-seq — to link each cell's molecular identity to its precise location in the tissue. That combination let them see not only which cells are present, but how they are arranged into neighbourhoods inside the basal ganglia.

The basal ganglia is a small region with an outsized role in health. Its circuits are implicated in Parkinson's disease and Huntington's disease, as well as in psychiatric conditions and addiction. Until now, researchers studying those disorders lacked a fine-grained reference for what a healthy basal ganglia looks like at the level of individual cells.

## Part of a much larger mapping effort

The atlas is one of two papers led by UC Irvine in a coordinated publication package from the BRAIN Initiative Cell Atlas Network, known as BICAN. The wider package involved hundreds of scientists from 55 institutions across nine countries, and the Irvine team also contributed to four additional papers in *Cell*. A second Irvine-led paper, published in *Cell Systems*, describes the programme that procured and prepared the donated brain tissue used across the project.

The main paper — "Multiscale spatial atlas of human basal ganglia cell types and communities," led by Bereket Tesfay Berackey — was produced through UC Irvine's Center for Multiomic Human Brain Cell Atlas project, working alongside the Salk Institute, UC San Diego and Washington University in St. Louis.

## Why it matters

For researchers, the atlas is a baseline. Comparing tissue from people with Parkinson's or Huntington's against this healthy reference should make it easier to spot which cell types and communities go wrong first — and where a future treatment might need to act. The same reference could also help scientists test whether experimental therapies reach the right cells.

## What is still unknown

The map covers one region, not the whole brain. BICAN's stated goal is a complete parts list of every cell type in the human brain, and the basal ganglia atlas is described as a first major milestone toward it. The four-donor sample also means the atlas captures a snapshot of healthy variation, not the full range across ages and populations — a gap future mapping work will need to fill.
