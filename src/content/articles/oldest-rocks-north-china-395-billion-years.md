Rocks found in northern China and dated to 3.95 billion years ago are among the oldest ever discovered — and they carry chemical clues that a process resembling modern plate tectonics may already have been at work nearly 4 billion years ago.

The rocks, a trondhjemitic gneiss from the Shimen Complex in Hebei province, were analysed by a team led by Hangqiang Xie of the Chinese Academy of Geological Sciences in Beijing. The team collected three samples and dated them by studying tiny zircon crystals trapped inside the rock. Uranium in the crystals decays into lead at a known rate, which let the researchers pin the rock's formation to 3,951 million years ago, give or take 3 million years. The findings were published in the journal *Nature* on November 11.

Earth is about 4.5 billion years old, but rocks from its earliest chapter are extremely rare: most have long since been destroyed by the same geological churning they would record. Until now, the only rocks older than 3.9 billion years confirmed beyond doubt came from the Acasta Gneiss Complex in Canada. Scientists have searched China for rocks older than 3.8 billion years for decades.

## A journey down, then back up

The team wanted to know more than the rocks' age. Analysing the chemistry of the zircon crystals — including different forms of oxygen and hafnium — and running computer models of the conditions under which the rocks formed, they found that the material had once been in contact with water at or near Earth's surface. That same material was then carried more than 50 kilometres (31 miles) below the surface, where intense heat and pressure partly melted it.

The researchers propose that this rapid burial was most likely achieved through a transient subduction event: one section of Earth's crust sinking beneath another, as happens in plate tectonics today. In other words, the machinery that now builds mountains and recycles ocean floor may already have been switching on in at least one part of the planet nearly 4 billion years ago.

A second chemical clock points even further back. Analysis of hafnium isotopes in the zircon crystals suggests that the process of separating new crust from Earth's mantle had begun more than 4.3 billion years ago, during the Hadean eon — hundreds of millions of years before these rocks formed.

If the interpretation holds, it pushes the timeline for Earth's crust formation and early tectonics back substantially. But the authors describe the event as transient, and a single site in one craton cannot prove the process operated worldwide. The discovery is a new data point in a long-running debate about when plate tectonics began — not the end of it.
