Household energy bills in Britain are set to rise sharply in the new year, after the regulator Ofgem announced a 26% increase in the price cap from January.

The cap sets the maximum suppliers can charge for each unit of energy on a standard variable tariff. For a typical household in England, Scotland and Wales on a dual-fuel tariff paid by direct debit, the annual bill would rise from £1,723 to £2,166 if the new level were sustained for a full year. In practice, the increase applies for the first three months of the year, working out at roughly £37 extra a month over that period — about £126 for every £100 a household currently spends. What any family actually pays will still depend on how much energy it uses.

Ofgem said the rise was driven entirely by higher wholesale costs faced by suppliers, primarily linked to the war with Iran. The regulator's Neil Kenward also pointed to the continuing conflict across the Middle East. It is the largest increase since Russia's invasion of Ukraine sent energy prices soaring in 2022.

Millions of households will feel the change at the start of winter's coldest stretch. Charities and suppliers expect energy debt to climb and more people to need emergency support. Dame Clare Moriarty, the chief executive of Citizens Advice, warned of a "brutal winter" for households already struggling. People on prepayment meters are likely to feel the rise most sharply, because they pay for energy as they use it rather than spreading the cost across the year.

The government has announced measures to soften the blow. Millions of households will receive a £100 payment from 4 January, alongside an ongoing cut in VAT on electricity. The Warm Home Discount will also take £150 off the bills of six million households on benefits this winter. Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh said people would be "deeply concerned" by another rise in their bills.

The increase lands at a difficult moment. Wholesale gas and power prices have been climbing through the autumn as the conflict in the Middle East has disrupted energy markets, and suppliers pass those costs on to customers through the cap. Ofgem reviews the cap every three months, so the January level is not the last word — the next announcement will show whether wholesale prices have settled or are still rising.

For households, the practical advice remains the same: check whether a fixed tariff would beat the cap, make sure any benefits or discounts are claimed, and contact a supplier early if bills become unmanageable. Suppliers are required to offer support, including payment plans, to customers who fall behind.
