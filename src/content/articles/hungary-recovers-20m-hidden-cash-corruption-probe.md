Hungarian investigators have recovered nearly $20 million in cash — some of it buried in forests — during a bribery investigation into a former minister who served under Viktor Orbán.

The National Asset Recovery and Protection Office said late on Friday that searches over two days, on November 12 and 13, turned up about $19.5 million hidden in Budapest and three other parts of the country. The agency, known by its Hungarian initials as the NVVH, was set up by the new government to pursue suspected misuse of public money during Orbán's years in power.

The haul was spread across an unlikely set of hiding places: bundles of banknotes dug out of woodland, stashed in farm buildings and warehouses, kept in a home, and locked inside a high-value safe in a secluded location. Finance Minister András Kármán said 12 treasury workers spent eight hours counting the money with five banknote counters.

## Cash in three currencies

The total came to 5.07 billion forints — about $15.33 million — plus €2.66 million and $1.26 million. The mix of currencies, and the spread of locations, suggests the cash was deliberately scattered rather than kept in one place.

The discovery is part of criminal proceedings against Miklós Seszták, who was Orbán's development minister from 2014 to 2018. He is suspected of receiving more than $37.2 million in bribes and kickbacks linked to overpriced secondhand bus purchases by a state transport company and to government construction projects. Seszták has denied wrongdoing.

## A new government's anti-graft drive

The case is the most visible result so far of the anti-corruption drive promised by Prime Minister Péter Magyar, whose centre-right Tisza party ended Orbán's 16 years in power in an April election. Magyar has called the clampdown "Operation Purgatory", and a poll by the Median agency published this month found 70% of respondents considered the drive "very important".

Two other former ministers from Orbán's government and former central bank chief György Matolcsy are under arrest over graft accusations in separate investigations.

For Magyar, who pledged to mend ties with the European Union after years of conflict under Orbán, the recovery is also a political statement: his government argues that corruption was rampant during the previous administration, and that recovering the money proves the new institutions work.

## What is not yet known

The authorities have not said whether Seszták will face formal charges, or how much of the recovered cash prosecutors will be able to link to the alleged bribes. The investigation is continuing, and the money — now in state hands — is evidence in a case that is still being built.
