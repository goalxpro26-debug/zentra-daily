NASA's Voyager 1 spacecraft has reached a distance of one light-day from Earth — about 16 billion miles — becoming the first human-made object ever to travel that far from home.

NASA calculated that the probe crossed the mark on Wednesday at 5:16 a.m. ET, more than 49 years after it left Earth. Because a signal from the spacecraft now takes a full day to reach us, engineers at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory will have to wait about a day for the data confirming the crossing, and the agency has scheduled an event for Thursday to discuss the milestone.

A light-day is the distance light travels in 24 hours. At Voyager 1's distance, a radio message sent from Earth now needs an entire day to arrive, and another full day for a reply to come back — stretching a round trip to 48 hours. The probe sits roughly 173 times as far from the Sun as Earth, more than four times the distance from the Sun to Pluto.

Voyager 1 launched on September 5, 1977, sixteen days after its twin, Voyager 2, on a mission originally meant only to fly past Jupiter and Saturn. It completed those encounters in 1979 and 1980, then kept going. It crossed the heliopause — the outer edge of the Sun's protective bubble — in August 2012, becoming the first spacecraft to enter interstellar space, and it has been the most distant human-made object since 1998, when it passed the Pioneer 10 probe. It is still travelling at nearly 38,000 miles per hour.

Remarkably, the spacecraft is still working. Its remaining instruments are sending back continuous measurements of magnetic fields, cosmic rays and plasma waves from a region no other mission has explored, and have even detected the faint hum caused by vibrations of small amounts of gas in interstellar space. Recent power-saving measures could keep the two Voyager spacecraft in communication with Earth for three to four more years, with enough power to run the current instruments through the 50th anniversary of their launches next year.

"Reaching one light-day is an astounding feat, one that I never expected to see," said Suzanne Dodd, the mission's project manager.

Voyager 1 is headed in the direction of the star Gliese 445, which it is expected to pass within about 1.7 light-years of in roughly 40,000 years. Its twin is not far behind on the same scale: Voyager 2 is expected to reach one light-day from Earth in November 2035. Both spacecraft carry the Golden Record, a gold-plated copper disc engraved with sounds and images representing life on Earth.
