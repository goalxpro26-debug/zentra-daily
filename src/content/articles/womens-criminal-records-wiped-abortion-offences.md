Women in England and Wales who were convicted or cautioned for ending their own pregnancies have been automatically pardoned, with their criminal records set to be wiped, under a new law that took effect on Monday, 16 November 2026.

The change comes through section 242 of the Crime and Policing Act 2026. It pardons women convicted of, or cautioned for, an abortion offence relating to their own pregnancy, and requires police and courts to delete the details of those convictions and cautions — along with records of arrests and investigations. Where a record cannot be fully erased, it must be marked so that it counts as deleted. The authorities aim to complete the work within a month.

Nobody has to apply. The Ministry of Justice has said affected women need take no action at all, and the pardons also cover women who have died. The measure does not apply to offences involving another woman's pregnancy, and anyone who helps a woman obtain an abortion outside the law — including doctors acting outside the rules — can still be prosecuted.

The pardons are the final step in a reform Parliament approved last year. In June 2025, lawmakers voted to end prosecutions of women for terminating their own pregnancies, removing them from the reach of the 1861 Offences Against the Person Act, a Victorian-era law that carried a maximum sentence of life imprisonment. A separate provision, section 241, which came into force earlier this year, means it is no longer a criminal offence for a woman to end her own pregnancy at any stage.

The law governing how abortion services are provided has not changed. Abortion in England and Wales remains legal when carried out by an authorised provider up to 24 weeks of pregnancy, with very limited exceptions after that point, such as when the mother's life is at risk or the child would be born with a severe disability.

Labour MP Tonia Antoniazzi, who brought forward the decriminalisation amendment, said police had investigated more than 100 women suspected of illegal abortions between 2020 and 2025, and that six women had appeared in court between 2022 and 2025. She called today "a momentous and historic day for women and girls in England and Wales", adding that no pardon could erase the harm and distress of what those women went through.

The campaign for change was sharpened by the case of Nicola Packer, who was prosecuted after taking abortion medication at home during the Covid-19 lockdown in 2020 and was acquitted by a jury in 2025 after more than five years of investigation. Her friend Helle Tumbridge told the BBC the ordeal had been traumatic, and said the new law should spare other women the same experience.

Alex Davies-Jones, the minister for victims and tackling violence against women and girls, said pardoning women for abortion offences relating to their own pregnancies had been a priority, and that she was pleased to bring into force the change Parliament voted for.
