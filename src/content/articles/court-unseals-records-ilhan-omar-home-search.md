A federal judge has ordered the release of 169 pages of court records from the search of Rep. Ilhan Omar's Washington, D.C., home, revealing a constitutional dispute between prosecutors and the judge who authorised the search — but shedding little new light on the investigation itself.

Chief Judge James Boasberg ordered the partial unsealing on Monday, after more than a dozen news organisations, including The Associated Press, argued that the public has a First Amendment right to see the records. The documents, released in heavily redacted form, relate to the October 12 search of the Minnesota Democrat's home by Department of Homeland Security agents.

The filings show that U.S. Magistrate Judge Matthew Sharbaugh, who authorised the search, raised concerns before signing off about the Speech or Debate Clause — the constitutional protection that shields members of Congress from executive-branch interference in their legislative work. Sharbaugh rejected the government's argument that the privilege applies only to official congressional offices and certain electronic devices.

"That logic does not hold," the magistrate wrote, according to the records.

Sharbaugh concluded that the government's law-enforcement interests had to be balanced against Omar's constitutional rights as a member of Congress. The government ultimately agreed to a search protocol allowing Omar to invoke the privilege over any legislative material before agents carried out the search.

## What the records do — and do not — reveal

The released documents do not disclose new details of the investigation, which Omar has described as politically motivated. Federal authorities are examining potential fraud in her family's immigration paperwork and have issued grand jury subpoenas to some of her relatives, the AP reported last month.

According to The Wall Street Journal, one court brief states that the government believes Omar has committed crimes described only in redacted text "over decades," and that prosecutors sought evidence dating back to 1980. Omar, a naturalised U.S. citizen born in Somalia, has not been charged with any crime and has denied any wrongdoing.

Boasberg agreed with the government that it would be premature to unseal a 67-page affidavit supporting the search warrant, which contains sensitive details of the investigation. The warrant itself was not included in the release. The government argued last month that unsealing the records before a charging decision is made would jeopardise the investigation.

Omar has said agents seized 17 items from her home, including phones, notebooks, her passport, a Somali-language children's book and a vintage camera her husband had received as a gift.

The search took place less than a month before the November 3 midterm elections, in which Omar won a fifth term. The Justice Department has historically avoided public investigative actions involving a candidate within 60 days of an election.
