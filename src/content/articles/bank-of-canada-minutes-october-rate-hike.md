Senior Bank of Canada policymakers agreed it was prudent to raise interest rates to dampen the risk of higher inflation, even with considerable uncertainty hanging over the economy, according to minutes of the central bank's most recent policy deliberations published on Tuesday.

The Bank lifted its target for the overnight rate to 2.5% on October 28, its first increase in more than three years. Officials now expect consumer-price inflation to stay above 3% until late 2027 and only gradually return to the Bank's 2% target by mid-2028.

The deliberations, which began on October 20 and involved all six members of the Bank's policy-setting governing council, centred on an energy shock that officials expect to last. With the war in the Middle East continuing and little sign that oil-tanker traffic would soon speed up, the minutes said the risk that high inflation could persist and spread across the economy had grown.

"The prolonged energy shock and a resilient economy meant that the risk that high inflation could persist and become broad-based had increased," the minutes said. On that basis, officials decided "to raise the policy rate to 2.5% to reduce the risk that persistently high energy prices would lead to broader price increases."

## Uncertainty, but not inaction

The case for holding rates was not ignored. Canada's economy has slack, and inflation outside energy remains close to target — consumer prices excluding gasoline were running at 2.2%, with core measures near 2%.

But the council judged that uncertainty was not a reason to stand still. Officials pointed to U.S.-Canada trade friction, the U.S.-Iran war, and the speed at which firms might raise prices to recoup higher input costs. "Uncertainty did not mean inaction," the minutes said, though officials agreed they would need to proceed carefully.

The minutes reinforced Governor Tiff Macklem's message that further increases could be needed, with each decision taken one meeting at a time.

## A softer backdrop since the hike

Since the October decision, the economic picture has softened. Statistics Canada said the economy stalled in August, with early estimates pointing to a slight decline in September — putting growth on track for about 1.3% annualised, below the Bank's estimate of 2%.

October jobs data showed the economy shedding jobs for a third straight month, unemployment rising, and average hourly wage growth slowing. Traders have scaled back expectations for a follow-up rate increase at the Bank's next decision, on December 9.

For Canadian borrowers, the hike means higher costs on variable-rate mortgages and lines of credit. Whether December brings another increase will depend on whether the economy's slowdown cools price pressures faster than energy costs push them up.
