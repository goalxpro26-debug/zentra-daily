Black holes are famous for colliding and merging in some of the most violent events in the universe. A new theoretical study argues they might also be able to do the opposite: under extremely narrow conditions, a single black hole could split into two or more smaller ones.

The paper was published on October 22 in the journal Physical Review D. Its authors used several mathematical modelling methods to test whether the event horizon of a black hole, the boundary beyond which nothing can escape, could branch apart. They describe the possible shape with a homely image: a pair of pants, with one waist splitting into two legs.

For decades, physicists assumed that splitting was off the table. The reasoning rests on two obstacles. The first is entropy. A black hole's surface area cannot decrease, a result tied to Stephen Hawking's area theorem. Splitting a static black hole, the simplest kind, would break that rule, so it is strictly forbidden in ordinary four-dimensional general relativity. The second obstacle is motion: even if a split began, the fragments would be pulled back together and recombine almost immediately.

The new paper finds a possible loophole, and it depends on spin. A black hole spinning extremely close to the maximum rate allowed by general relativity carries an enormous store of rotational energy. The researchers argue that this energy could be converted into the motion needed to fling the fragments apart fast enough to escape each other. In that narrow regime, at the edge of what the laws allow, a split would not violate entropy or conservation of energy and angular momentum.

The idea changes again in more exotic settings. In models with more than four dimensions, the rules loosen. A black hole stretched into a long cylindrical form, sometimes called a black string, could pinch off into separate pieces, like beads breaking away from a string.

The fragments, if they existed, could be extraordinarily small. In one example calculated by the researchers, a black hole with ten times the mass of the Sun would favour fragments with masses comparable to a few Great Pyramids of Giza, compressed into a region smaller than a proton. Such fragments could help explain black holes too small to have formed through the usual routes, if they are ever found.

Nobody has observed a black hole splitting. The paper explores what the laws of physics permit; it is not a report of an event. The conditions it requires are so extreme that they may never occur in nature, and full simulations of the splitting process are still needed before the idea can be tested further.

Even so, the result redraws a boundary physicists thought was settled. Black holes can merge, and the mathematics now suggests that, in the rarest of circumstances, they might also be able to divide.
