Astronomers have found a trio of stars just 28 light-years from Earth that, by the known rules of how stars live and die, should not exist at all.

The system, named Welopōʻai, contains two white dwarfs — the dense, cooling embers of dead sun-like stars — and a single red dwarf, a star far dimmer than our sun. It is the closest known triple-star system containing two white dwarfs, and no comparable system is known within 100 light-years of Earth.

The discovery was made by an international team led by the Trottier Institute for Research on Exoplanets at the Université de Montréal, and is published in the journal *Astronomy & Astrophysics*. The team was not looking for a triple star at all. They were hunting for planets around a nearby red dwarf, watching for tiny wobbles in its speed that an orbiting planet would cause.

What they found was far stranger. The red dwarf's speed relative to Earth varied by about 10 miles per second — more than a thousand times larger than the wobble a planet would produce. After years of follow-up observations, the team concluded the unseen companion had to be more massive than half our sun, yet almost impossibly faint. The only plausible explanation was the remnant of a dead star.

High-resolution images from the Gemini North telescope on Maunakea confirmed it: a faint white dwarf orbiting close to the red dwarf, with a second, already-known white dwarf circling the pair at a much greater distance. The inner pair orbits roughly every 9.8 years, a little less than Jupiter's distance from the sun, while the outer white dwarf sits far beyond them.

The puzzle is how the red dwarf survived. Before becoming a white dwarf, its companion was a star about 40 percent more massive than the sun. As it died, it would have swollen into a giant hundreds of times its original size — an expansion that, in most similar systems, swallows the companion star or drags the two into a merger. Against all expectations, Welopōʻai's red dwarf is still there, in a surprisingly wide and stable orbit.

"This system should not exist," said Patrick Dufour, a professor at the Université de Montréal who specialises in white dwarfs and co-authored the study. "We have no clean explanation for how it got here. That is the question driving our next observations."

The discovery also gives astronomers an unusually precise measurement. The team weighed the newly identified white dwarf with an uncertainty of less than one percent — about 0.62 times the mass of the sun — making it one of the most precisely weighed white dwarfs within 65 light-years and a valuable test of stellar-evolution models.

The system's Hawaiian name, chosen through the ʻImiloa Astronomy Center's naming programme, combines "welo", meaning a family trait, and "pōʻai", meaning a circular path — a nod to the orbital dance of its three stars. Further observations, including from the Webb telescope, may help explain how such a family formed.
