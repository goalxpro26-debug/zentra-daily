A federal judge on Saturday temporarily blocked President Donald Trump's plan to close East Potomac Park in Washington and rebuild its public golf course, ruling one day before the park was due to shut for 15 months of renovation.

U.S. District Judge Ana Reyes, in a 30-page decision, said the administration's stated reasons for closing the park — safety concerns — were "pretextual," and that the government likely violated at least two federal laws in its haste to meet what she called an "arbitrary deadline." The order keeps the park open during the hours it was open as of November 14. The government does not have to keep running golf operations there, Reyes wrote, but it "cannot use the absence of golf operations as a reason to exclude the public." The order runs through November 28, or until the court orders otherwise.

The park sits near the Jefferson Memorial, about two miles south of the White House. It holds three golf courses totalling 36 holes, a miniature golf course and a paved loop popular with cyclists and runners. Trump's plan would replace them with a single 18-hole championship course that he has said could host events such as the U.S. Open and the Ryder Cup while remaining open to the public. In an October 18 social media post, Trump said the closure would last 15 months and would transform the park's golf offerings into "one of the Best and Most Spectacular Golf Courses anywhere in the World."

The lawsuit was brought by the DC Preservation League, a historic preservation group, along with two local golfers. They argue the redevelopment would violate federal environmental and administrative laws and would change the character of an accessible public course, making it unaffordable — a claim the president denies. In a statement after the ruling, the Justice Department said it would "always vociferously defend the Trump Administration's agenda in court," and noted that nothing in the order stops planning for the golf course from continuing.

The ruling is the latest in a series of setbacks for the project in Reyes's court. In October, she blocked the removal of trees and other irreversible changes after more than 400 trees were cut down with minimal notice. She has also ordered an inquiry into whether Justice Department lawyers misled her about the closure timeline, and the department's attempt to have her removed from the case failed.

## What is still unknown

It is not yet clear whether the administration will appeal or ask a higher court to lift the order, or how quickly the underlying lawsuit will move. The case, DC Preservation League v. Department of the Interior, will decide whether the redevelopment itself can go ahead — Saturday's order only pauses the closure while that question is litigated.
