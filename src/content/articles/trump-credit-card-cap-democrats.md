President Donald Trump said on Saturday that he would work with Democrats to cap credit card interest rates and fees, reviving an affordability proposal that stalled earlier this year.

In a Fox News interview on Saturday morning, Trump was asked whether a cap on card rates was something he could find common ground on with Democrats, who take control of both chambers in January. "I would do it," he said. "These credit card companies have made a lot — and the credit card fees that they charge. I'm all into it."

"The credit card companies, they're getting too much," he added. "The fees are too high and the interests are too high."

He framed it as fairness, not ideology, saying it was no question of free enterprise when a fee was set by "some guy... on Park Avenue" and decided was too high. "So I'm all for that," he said.

The proposal is not new. In January, Trump said on Truth Social that he would set a 10% cap on credit card interest rates for one year, warning lenders that did not comply would face legal consequences. Nothing came of that threat. Banks pushed back, saying a cap would force card cancellations for less creditworthy customers or sharp cuts to rewards. The idea also found little enthusiasm among Republicans, and the GOP-controlled Congress made no serious effort to pass it.

The politics have shifted since then. Senator Elizabeth Warren of Massachusetts, who is expected to become the next chair of the Senate Banking Committee, has long backed a cap and recently said she is ready to work with Trump on a bill. Responding to Saturday's interview, Warren said: "Let's cap credit card interest rates and save Americans money."

The stakes are large. The median credit card interest rate is roughly 22.2%, according to an August survey by the New York Federal Reserve. Researchers estimated a 10% cap would save Americans roughly $100 billion a year in interest. About 195 million Americans held cards in 2024 and paid $160 billion in interest, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says.

The industry's objections have not gone away. Banking groups say a cap would deny credit to millions by making lending too risky; credit unions put the figure at more than 47 million people losing access. Supporters counter that the industry would remain profitable even at lower rates, though rewards and other perks might be scaled back.

Whether it becomes law is unclear. Trump did not repeat the 10% figure on Saturday, no bill has been agreed, and it is not clear how high a priority Democrats will make the idea in January.
