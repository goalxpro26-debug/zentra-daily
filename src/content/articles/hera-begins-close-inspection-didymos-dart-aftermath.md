A European spacecraft has begun its close inspection of the asteroid NASA deliberately struck four years ago, opening the first detailed examination of whether a spacecraft impact can reliably deflect a dangerous space rock.

The European Space Agency announced on Wednesday that its Hera spacecraft is now studying the Didymos asteroid system from just 25 to 30 kilometres away. Didymos is a 780-metre-wide asteroid orbited by a smaller moonlet, Dimorphos, about 151 metres across — the body NASA's DART probe slammed into in September 2022 in the first test of planetary defence.

## What Hera has seen so far

Hera, a car-sized spacecraft launched in 2024, sent back its first close-up view of the system on November 5, photographed from 60 kilometres away. The image showed a side of Didymos that DART's camera never captured before impact: a rugged, boulder-strewn hemisphere marked with craters and what may be grooves or ridges across its surface. Dimorphos was partly hidden in shadow at the lower edge of the frame.

The spacecraft will move closer still. Next month it is due to approach to within 20 kilometres of the pair, and the agency plans to release the first detailed, full-resolution images of both asteroids on November 25.

## Why the follow-up matters

DART — short for Double Asteroid Redirection Test — struck Dimorphos at about 6.6 kilometres per second on September 26, 2022, and shortened the moonlet's orbit around Didymos by roughly 33 minutes. The impact sent rubble and boulders flying off Dimorphos, adding extra momentum beyond the blow itself.

But the size of the crater, and whether the impact reshaped Dimorphos as a whole, could only be guessed at from Earth. Hera's job is to measure what actually happened: it will weigh Dimorphos, map the crater and chart changes across both bodies, turning a successful demonstration into a technique scientists understand well enough to repeat if Earth ever faces a real threat.

## Two small companions to come

After its initial survey, Hera will release two shoebox-sized CubeSats, Milani and Juventas. Milani will carry out mineralogical analysis of the asteroids' surfaces, while Juventas will probe Dimorphos's interior with the first ground-penetrating radar ever flown to an asteroid.

The system sits about 197 million kilometres from Earth, an 11-minute one-way radio delay — close enough to study in detail, far enough that every command takes patience.

## What is still unknown

The detailed images due on November 25 should settle whether DART reshaped Dimorphos or merely cratered it. Until Hera's measurements are complete, the exact mass of Dimorphos, the depth of the impact crater and the internal structure of the rubble-pile moonlet remain estimates.
