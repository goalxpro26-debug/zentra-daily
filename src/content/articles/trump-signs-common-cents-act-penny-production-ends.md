President Donald Trump signed the Common Cents Act into law on Friday, formally ending production of the penny for general circulation and closing a chapter of American coinage that began in the 1790s.

Trump signed the bill in the Oval Office alongside Senator Cynthia Lummis of Wyoming, the measure's lead Senate sponsor. The legislation, H.R. 10167, passed both chambers of Congress without opposition — by voice vote in the House on September 14 and by unanimous consent in the Senate on September 28. Its sponsors spanned both parties: Lummis introduced it in the Senate with Kirsten Gillibrand of New York, James Lankford of Oklahoma and Ron Wyden of Oregon, while Representatives Lisa McClain of Michigan and Robert Garcia of California carried the companion bill in the House.

The law permanently ends production of the one-cent coin for everyday use. The U.S. Mint may still strike pennies for collectors, and the law authorises a new, lower-cost version of the nickel. It also requires the Treasury Department to give Congress 60 days' advance notice before discontinuing any other circulating coin.

The penny's end has been coming for some time. Trump directed the Treasury Department to stop minting the coin in February 2025, and the Mint struck its final circulating penny on November 12, 2025, at a ceremonial final strike at its Philadelphia facility. Friday's signing makes that halt permanent in law.

The driving reason is cost. It now takes nearly four cents to make a one-cent coin — the unit cost rose from 1.42 cents to 3.69 cents — and the Mint projects the change will save taxpayers $56 million a year. The fight to end the coin goes back nearly a decade, to the late Senator Mike Enzi's COINS Act, which first proposed suspending penny production.

What changes at the till is rounding. When a cash payment cannot be made with exact change, businesses may now round the total to the nearest five cents under a single national rule: totals ending in 1, 2, 6 or 7 cents round down, and those ending in 3, 4, 8 or 9 cents round up. Card, check and other electronic payments stay exact to the cent.

Existing pennies remain legal tender, and the roughly 114 billion already in circulation will keep changing hands for years — the Federal Reserve will keep recirculating them for as long as possible. The uniform rule matters most to businesses that still handle large amounts of cash: grocers, convenience stores and restaurants, which have been improvising rounding practices since production stopped. Nearly one in four purchases in restaurants are still made in cash, according to the National Restaurant Association.

The penny was first authorised by the Coinage Act of 1792 and first minted in 1793. The final strike last November closed a 232-year chapter. It will take many more years for the coin to cycle out of everyday use — but no new pennies will enter circulation to replace it.
