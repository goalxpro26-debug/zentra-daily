Two of the night sky's most familiar planets are about to put on a brief show together. On the night of November 15–16, Mars and Jupiter will appear a little over one degree apart in the constellation Leo — roughly the width of a little finger held at arm's length — in their closest apparent pairing in years.

Their closest approach comes at 9:04 p.m. Eastern time on Sunday, November 15, according to calculations from In-The-Sky reported by Space.com. At that exact moment, though, the pair will still be below the horizon for most of the United States. The real viewing window opens later: the planets rise over the eastern horizon around midnight local time, and the best views run through the hours before dawn on Monday, November 16. Face east once they are up, and look for Leo — the blue-white star Regulus will be glowing just below the pair.

No equipment is needed. Jupiter will dominate with a bright, steady glow, while Mars will show its familiar reddish tint nearby. A pair of 10x50 binoculars will frame both planets at once for anyone who wants a closer look, and a small telescope can pull out Jupiter's cloud bands and Mars as a small ochre disk.

## A trick of perspective

The closeness is entirely an illusion. In reality, Mars and Jupiter are separated by more than 368 million miles (592 million kilometres), and the light from Regulus below them has travelled some 79 light-years to reach Earth. The two planets simply happen to line up from our vantage point as Mars, on its faster inner orbit, catches up with Jupiter — an event astronomers call an appulse. It happens roughly once every two to two-and-a-half years, but this one is unusually tight: the pair will not look this close again until July 2029, when they meet in Virgo.

The pairing also arrives just ahead of another November sky event. The Leonid meteor shower peaks on the nights of November 17–18, so anyone out in the small hours after the conjunction may catch a few early shooting stars as well.

Clouds on the 16th need not spoil it. Mars and Jupiter will still sit close together on the mornings either side, and the two planets will drift apart only gradually over the following weeks.
