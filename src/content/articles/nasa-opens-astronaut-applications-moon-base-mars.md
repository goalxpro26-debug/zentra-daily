NASA has opened applications for its next class of astronauts, giving Americans a short window to apply for a job that could take them to the surface of the Moon.

The space agency announced the recruitment drive on Saturday, November 7 — the first time it has sought new astronauts since 2024. Applications close at 11:59 p.m. EST on Thursday, December 3, and must be submitted through the federal jobs site USAJOBS.

Those selected will join NASA as it works to return astronauts to the Moon and build a permanent base there, while laying the groundwork for the first crewed missions to Mars. NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said the new recruits could walk on the Moon, live and work at the planned Moon Base, and help develop the systems that will eventually carry crews to Mars.

## Who can apply

Applicants must be U.S. citizens with a master's degree in a science, technology, engineering or mathematics field — including engineering, biological science, physical science, computer science or mathematics — from an accredited institution. Most candidates also need at least three years of professional experience related to their degree, or 1,000 to 1,500 flight hours as a pilot. All must pass NASA's long-duration flight physical.

There is no official age limit. NASA says past selectees have ranged from 26 to 46 years old.

The job pays $164,395 a year under the 2026 federal pay schedule, though NASA notes the rate could be adjusted for 2027 and 2028. Selected candidates will train at NASA's Johnson Space Center in Houston, Texas.

## A long shot, by the numbers

The odds are steep. When NASA last opened applications in 2024, more than 8,000 people applied. Ten candidates were chosen in 2025 and are now partway through nearly two years of training. NASA currently has 32 active astronauts.

The agency expects to announce its final selections late next year, with training for the new class to begin soon after.

## Why it matters

The recruitment drive is tied to NASA's Artemis program, which aims to return humans to the Moon in 2028 — the first time since 1972. The plan calls for a sustained presence at the lunar south pole, where rotating crews would live and work while preparing for the longer journey to Mars. Before that landing, the Artemis III crew is scheduled to fly a test mission in low Earth orbit in 2027 to practise docking with the lunar landers built by Blue Origin and SpaceX.

For the handful of people eventually chosen, the application closing on December 3 is the first step toward a career that could end with footsteps on another world.
