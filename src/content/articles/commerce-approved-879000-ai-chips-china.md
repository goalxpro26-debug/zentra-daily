The US Commerce Department approved sales of more than 879,000 advanced AI chips to Chinese companies over a six-month stretch, according to documents released on Thursday by the House Foreign Affairs Committee — putting hard numbers on a trade Washington has spent years trying to restrict.

The approvals, granted by the department's Bureau of Industry and Security between December 2025 and May 2026, covered chips made by Nvidia and AMD, including Nvidia's H200 and AMD's MI325X. The committee published the figures after its chairman, Brian Mast, used powers under the Export Control Reform Act to make normally confidential licence information public.

The breakdown named some of China's largest technology companies. Tencent was approved to receive 150,000 chips, Alibaba 75,000, Baidu 43,000 and the carmaker BYD 4,000. All four are on the Pentagon's list of companies alleged to have links to the Chinese military. Companies had applied to ship 3.4 million chips to China in total during the period, so roughly a quarter of the requested volume was approved.

What the documents do not show is how many of the approved chips were actually shipped. The committee said that was not immediately clear. Nvidia said the approvals came with "strict conditions and limited volumes" and added that, despite receiving some licences, it has since stopped shipping H200 chips to China.

The disclosure also raised questions about how chips reach their buyers. Mast said two entities appearing on Nvidia's licence applications, New Matrix and Maginfra, were suspected smuggling fronts, and the committee said Nvidia had sought to rely on possible smuggling fronts to move advanced chips into China. The committee described the release as the most detailed public disclosure in the history of the Export Control Reform Act. Its ranking Democrat, Gregory Meeks, took part in the vote to make the records public.

The figures land in the middle of a long-running debate over whether US export controls are working. The controls are meant to slow China's development of advanced artificial intelligence and military technology by cutting off access to the most powerful processors. Critics of the licensing system argue that approvals on this scale undercut that goal; supporters of the current approach say licences are granted case by case, with conditions attached, and that a blanket ban would hand the market to Chinese chipmakers instead.

The same week brought a reminder that the net is tightening elsewhere: new US rules have pushed TSMC, the world's largest chipmaker, to halt advanced manufacturing orders for Chinese AI designers. Approvals and restrictions are moving in opposite directions at once — and the newly published numbers give Congress, and the industry, a concrete basis for arguing about which direction is winning.
